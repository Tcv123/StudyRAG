⚗ PAG1: Moles Determination

Apparatus

  • Conical flask with bung and delivery tube
  • Gas syringe (100 cm³)
  • Balance (to 0.001 g)
  • Measuring cylinder
  • Magnesium ribbon (weighed accurately)
  • Excess dilute HCl
  • Thermometer and barometer (to record conditions)

Method

  1. Weigh a known length of clean Mg ribbon accurately (e.g., ~0.03 g).
  2. Place excess dilute HCl (e.g., 20 cm³ of 1 mol dm⁻³) in the conical flask.
  3. Connect the delivery tube to the gas syringe. Check there are no leaks.
  4. Add the Mg ribbon to the flask, quickly insert the bung, and allow the reaction to proceed.
  5. Wait until the reaction is complete (Mg fully dissolved, no more gas produced).
  6. Record the final volume of H₂ gas in the syringe.
  7. Record room temperature and atmospheric pressure.
  8. Calculate: n(Mg) = mass/24.3. n(H₂) = n(Mg). Molar gas volume = V(gas)/n(H₂).
  9. Alternatively, use pV = nRT to calculate n from p, V, and T, then find Mᵣ.

Safety

  • H₂ gas is flammable — no naked flames.
  • Dilute HCl is irritant — goggles.
  • Gas syringe may pop out — clamp securely.

Analysis

Calculate: Molar gas volume at room conditions. Expected ~24 dm³ mol⁻¹ (24,000 cm³ mol⁻¹) at ~298 K, 101 kPa.

Alternative: Use pV = nRT to find n, then compare with calculated n from mass.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
H₂ gas escaping before sealingSystematicSeal quickly, or hang the Mg in a small tube inside the sealed flask and tip it into the acid
Gas syringe frictionSystematicLubricate; tap gently
Mg oxide layer on ribbonSystematicClean with emery paper before weighing
Gas not at room temperatureSystematicAllow time for gas to cool before reading
H₂ slightly soluble in water/acidSystematicNegligible at normal conditions; acknowledged
Exam Tip: The molar gas volume at RTP (room temperature and pressure, ~298 K, 100 kPa) is approximately 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹. At STP (273 K, 100 kPa) it is 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹. Always state the conditions. Use pV = nRT when exact conditions matter: p in Pa, V in m³, T in K, R = 8.314.
Worked Example — Molar Gas Volume:

0.0360 g of magnesium ribbon reacted with excess dilute HCl, producing 35.8 cm³ of H₂ at room temperature and pressure.

n(Mg) = 0.0360 / 24.3 = 1.48 × 10⁻³ mol = n(H₂) (1:1 ratio)
Molar gas volume = 35.8 / 1.48 × 10⁻³ = 2.42 × 10⁴ cm³ mol⁻¹ = 24.2 dm³ mol⁻¹, close to the expected 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at RTP.

⚗ PAG2: Acid–Base Titration

Apparatus

  • 50 cm³ burette, clamp and stand
  • 25.0 cm³ pipette and filler
  • Conical flasks (250 cm³), white tile
  • Suitable indicator or self-indicating reagent
  • Volumetric flask (for preparing standard solutions)

Method

  1. Standard solution: Weigh the solid accurately (weigh the boat before and after transfer), dissolve in distilled water in a beaker, transfer with rinsings to a 250 cm³ volumetric flask, make up to the mark (bottom of meniscus at eye level) and invert to mix.
  2. Acid-base: Pipette 25.0 cm³ of one solution into a conical flask. Add indicator. Titrate from the burette until permanent colour change. Repeat for concordant results (within 0.10 cm³).
  3. Back titration: React the sample with known excess of reagent. Titrate the remaining unreacted reagent. Calculate original amount by difference. Useful when the analyte is insoluble (e.g., CaCO₃ in antacid tablets).
  4. Indicator choice: strong acid/strong base — methyl orange or phenolphthalein; strong acid/weak base (e.g. HCl with NaHCO₃) — methyl orange; weak acid/strong base — phenolphthalein.
  5. Extension — Redox (KMnO₄): redox titrations use the same technique and are examinable, but PAG2 itself is an acid–base titration. Pipette acidified Fe²⁺ into flask. Titrate with KMnO₄ until permanent faint pink (self-indicating). Use H₂SO₄ to acidify (not HCl or HNO₃).
  6. Calculate using n = cV and stoichiometric ratios from balanced equations.

Safety

  • Acids and alkalis — corrosive/irritant, goggles.
  • KMnO₄ — oxidising agent, stains; wear gloves.
  • Use pipette filler — never mouth-pipette.

Analysis

Calculate: Mean concordant titre. n(known) = c × V. Use stoichiometry for n(unknown). c = n/V.

Back titration: n(original) = n(added) - n(remaining after back-titration).

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Standard solution not made up exactly to the markSystematicRinse all solid into the flask; add the last drops with a dropping pipette
Overshooting end pointSystematicAdd drop-wise near end point
Parallax reading buretteRandomEye level; bottom of meniscus (top for KMnO₄)
Not rinsing burette correctlySystematicRinse with solution to be used
Including rough titre in meanSystematicOnly average concordant results; exclude rough
Exam Tip: For back titrations, always set up the calculation as: moles reacted with analyte = total moles added − moles determined by titration. Common example: finding % CaCO₃ in limestone — add excess HCl, then titrate remaining HCl with NaOH.
Worked Example — Back Titration:

1.50 g of impure CaCO₃ was dissolved in 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl (excess). The remaining HCl required 23.05 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH to neutralise it.

n(HCl total) = 1.00 × 0.0500 = 0.0500 mol
n(NaOH) = 1.00 × 0.02305 = 0.02305 mol = n(HCl remaining)
n(HCl reacted with CaCO₃) = 0.0500 − 0.02305 = 0.02695 mol
n(CaCO₃) = 0.02695 / 2 = 0.01348 mol (CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂)
Mass CaCO₃ = 0.01348 × 100.1 = 1.349 g → percentage purity = (1.349 / 1.50) × 100 = 89.9%

⚗ PAG3: Enthalpy Determination

Apparatus

  • Neutralisation: Polystyrene cup, lid, thermometer, measuring cylinder, stopwatch
  • Combustion: Spirit burner, copper calorimeter, thermometer, balance, draught shield, measuring cylinder

Method (Neutralisation)

  1. Measure 25 cm³ of acid into the polystyrene cup. Record temperature for 3 minutes.
  2. At minute 4, add 25 cm³ of alkali. Stir. Record temperature for 6+ minutes.
  3. Use graphical extrapolation to find ΔT. Calculate q = mcΔT. Then ΔH = -q/n.

Method (Combustion)

  1. Measure 100 cm³ of water into the calorimeter. Record initial temperature.
  2. Weigh the spirit burner. Light it and heat the water until temperature rises ~20 °C.
  3. Extinguish, reweigh. Calculate mass burned, q = mcΔT, n = mass/Mᵣ. ΔH = -q/n.

Safety

  • Acids/alkalis — corrosive, goggles.
  • Alcohols are flammable — keep away from other ignition sources.
  • Hot apparatus — use tongs.

Analysis

Plot: Temperature vs. time for extrapolation (neutralisation).

Compare: Experimental vs. literature ΔH values. Discuss reasons for discrepancy.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Heat loss to surroundingsSystematicLid, insulation, draught shield, extrapolation
Incomplete combustionSystematicEnsure good air supply; clean wick
Evaporation of alcoholSystematicCap promptly; weigh quickly
Heat capacity assumptionsSystematicAcknowledged; dilute solution ≈ water
Exam Tip: The experimental ΔH(combustion) is always less negative (less exothermic) than the data book value. The main reason is heat loss to surroundings. Other factors include incomplete combustion and heat absorbed by the apparatus. Always state these clearly for full marks.

⚗ PAG4: Qualitative Analysis of Ions

Apparatus

  • Test tubes, nichrome wire, Bunsen burner
  • NaOH(aq), NH₃(aq), AgNO₃(aq), BaCl₂(aq) or Ba(NO₃)₂(aq), dilute HNO₃, HCl, Devarda's alloy
  • Limewater, litmus paper

Method

  1. Flame tests: Clean wire → dip in sample → observe colour in Bunsen flame.
  2. Cation tests: Add NaOH(aq) dropwise, then in excess — observe precipitate colour and whether it redissolves (e.g. Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ orange-brown; Zn²⁺, Al³⁺ and Pb²⁺ white precipitates that dissolve in excess NaOH).
  3. Halide tests: Acidify with HNO₃, add AgNO₃. Confirm with NH₃: AgCl dissolves in dilute NH₃, AgBr in concentrated NH₃, AgI does not dissolve.
  4. Sulfate: Acidify with HNO₃, add BaCl₂ → white precipitate.
  5. Carbonate: Add dilute HNO₃ (not HCl if you will test the same sample for halides) → effervescence, gas turns limewater milky.
  6. Ammonium: Add NaOH, warm → NH₃ turns damp red litmus blue.
  7. Nitrate(V): Warm with NaOH(aq) and Devarda's alloy → NH₃ given off (turns damp red litmus blue).
  8. Sensible order on one sample: carbonate, then sulfate, then halide — BaCO₃ is insoluble and Ag₂SO₄ is only slightly soluble, so either could give a false positive; use Ba(NO₃)₂ rather than BaCl₂ if the halide test follows.

Safety

  • AgNO₃ stains — gloves. BaCl₂ toxic — wash hands.
  • Conc. HCl and NH₃ — fume cupboard.

Analysis

Record: Observations table for each test. Match results to identify unknown ions.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
ContaminationSystematicClean all equipment; use separate droppers
Na⁺ masking flame coloursSystematicView through cobalt blue glass
Not acidifying before testsSystematicAlways acidify with HNO₃
Colour confusion (cream vs white)RandomCompare alongside known standards
Exam Tip: To distinguish between halide silver precipitates further: add dilute NH₃ — AgCl dissolves (soluble complex forms); add conc. NH₃ — AgBr dissolves. AgI does not dissolve in either. This is a common extension question.

⚗ PAG5: Synthesis of an Organic Liquid

Apparatus

  • Round-bottomed flask, anti-bumping granules
  • Reflux condenser (vertical) and distillation condenser (angled)
  • Separating funnel, conical flask
  • Drying agent (anhydrous MgSO₄, Na₂SO₄ or CaCl₂)
  • Thermometer for the still head; electric heating mantle
  • Example reagents (haloalkane): butan-1-ol, sodium bromide, concentrated sulfuric acid, sodium hydrogencarbonate solution

Method (e.g. 1-bromobutane from butan-1-ol)

  1. Reaction: Heat butan-1-ol with sodium bromide and concentrated sulfuric acid (which generate HBr) under reflux, with anti-bumping granules.
  2. Separation: Distil to collect the crude product. Wash in a separating funnel with water, then sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (removes acid; vent often), then water. 1-bromobutane is denser than water, so keep the lower layer.
  3. Drying: Add anhydrous drying agent until the liquid is clear. Decant or filter.
  4. Purification: Redistil, collecting the fraction that boils close to the data-book value (about 100–104 °C).
  5. Calculate percentage yield.

Safety

  • Organic chemicals are flammable — electric heating, no flames.
  • Work in fume cupboard.
  • Vent separating funnel to release pressure.
  • Concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive — add slowly with cooling. A written risk assessment is part of this PAG.

Analysis

Calculate: % yield = (actual/theoretical) × 100. % atom economy = (Mᵣ desired product / total Mᵣ all products) × 100.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Product lost in transfersSystematicMinimise transfers; rinse with small amounts
Incomplete reactionSystematicReflux longer; use excess reagent
Product still wet (cloudy)SystematicAdd more drying agent until the liquid is clear
Wrong fraction collectedSystematicMonitor thermometer; collect narrow range
Exam Tip: Always explain the purpose of each step: reflux = complete reaction; distillation = separate by boiling point; washing with NaHCO₃ (or Na₂CO₃) = removes acidic impurities; drying agent = removes water; redistillation = purifies the product, collected at its boiling point. Never heat a sealed system. Each step reduces yield, so high yields require careful technique.

⚗ PAG6: Synthesis of an Organic Solid

Apparatus

  • Conical flask or pear-shaped flask (with reflux condenser if heating for longer), water bath or electric heater
  • Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid), ethanoic anhydride, a few drops of concentrated phosphoric(V) or sulfuric acid as catalyst
  • Buchner funnel, filter flask, filter paper, vacuum pump; ice bath
  • Solvent for recrystallisation (e.g. hot water or an ethanol–water mixture)
  • Melting point apparatus and capillary tubes
  • TLC plate, developing chamber, capillary spotters, UV lamp

Method (Synthesis of Aspirin)

  1. Weigh about 2 g of salicylic acid accurately into a dry flask. Add excess ethanoic anhydride (about 4 cm³) in a fume cupboard, then a few drops of the acid catalyst.
  2. Warm in a water bath at roughly 80–90 °C for 10–15 minutes, swirling, until all the solid has dissolved. (Heating under reflux can be used instead; reflux may be covered in either PAG5 or PAG6.)
  3. Carefully add cold water to hydrolyse the excess ethanoic anhydride, then cool the flask in an ice bath to crystallise the crude aspirin.
  4. Collect the crystals by suction filtration using a Buchner funnel. Wash with a little ice-cold water.
  5. Recrystallise: dissolve the crude solid in the minimum volume of hot solvent, filter while hot if there are insoluble impurities, then cool slowly so that pure crystals form and soluble impurities stay in solution. Filter again under reduced pressure, wash with a little cold solvent, and dry (e.g. between filter papers or in a desiccator).
  6. Weigh the dry product and calculate the percentage yield.
  7. Check purity: measure the melting point range and compare with the data-book value (about 136 °C). Run a TLC plate spotted with the product, pure aspirin and salicylic acid.

Safety

  • Ethanoic anhydride is corrosive and its vapour is irritating to the eyes and lungs — dispense in a fume cupboard.
  • Concentrated phosphoric(V)/sulfuric acid is corrosive — goggles and gloves.
  • Ethanol (if used in recrystallisation) is flammable — heat with a water bath or electric heater, not a flame.
  • A written risk assessment is part of this PAG: identify each hazard, the risk and how it is controlled.

Analysis

Calculate: Percentage yield = (actual mass / theoretical mass) × 100, using the limiting reagent (salicylic acid; ethanoic anhydride is in excess).

Purity: A pure solid melts sharply (over 1–2 °C) at the data-book value. On the TLC plate, a pure product gives a single spot at the same Rf as pure aspirin; a spot matching salicylic acid shows unreacted starting material.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Too much solvent used in recrystallisationSystematicUse the minimum volume of hot solvent so less product stays dissolved
Product lost during transfers and filtrationSystematicMinimise transfers; rinse with small amounts of ice-cold solvent
Wet product (mass too high; mp lowered)SystematicDry thoroughly; dry to constant mass
Heating melting point apparatus too quicklySystematicHeat slowly (about 1–2 °C per minute) near the expected mp
Exam Tip: Be ready to explain each recrystallisation step: minimum hot solvent (so the product crystallises on cooling), hot filtration (removes insoluble impurities), slow cooling (purer, larger crystals), cold-solvent wash (removes soluble impurities on the crystal surface without redissolving much product). Impurities lower and broaden the melting point range.
Worked Example — Percentage Yield:

A student prepared aspirin from 2.00 g of salicylic acid. The theoretical yield is 2.61 g. The actual yield after purification was 1.85 g.

Percentage yield = (1.85 / 2.61) × 100 = 70.9%

This is a typical yield for a lab preparation. Losses occur during transfer, filtration, washing, and recrystallisation.

⚗ PAG7: Qualitative Analysis of Organic Functional Groups

Apparatus

  • Test tubes, water bath (60-70 °C)
  • Bromine water, Tollens' reagent, Fehling's A+B
  • Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, Na₂CO₃ solution, limewater, neutral iron(III) chloride solution, indicator paper

Method

  1. C=C: Add Br₂(aq) — decolourises = alkene.
  2. CHO (aldehyde): Tollens' (silver mirror) or Fehling's (brick-red precipitate).
  3. OH (alcohol 1°/2°): Warm with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ — orange to green.
  4. COOH: Add Na₂CO₃ — effervescence = carboxylic acid.
  5. Phenol: Neutral FeCl₃(aq) — purple colour. Phenols are weakly acidic (dissolve in NaOH) but do not fizz with Na₂CO₃.
  6. Amine: Solution turns damp indicator paper alkaline (lone pair on N accepts H⁺).

Safety

  • K₂Cr₂O₇ is carcinogenic — gloves, fume cupboard.
  • Tollens' — prepare fresh, dispose immediately (explosive on standing).
  • Bromine water is toxic and corrosive — fume cupboard.

Analysis

Record: Table of positive/negative results. Use the pattern to identify functional groups.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Dirty glasswareSystematicClean thoroughly; Tollens' needs very clean tubes
Insufficient heatingSystematicUse water bath at correct temperature
Using old Tollens' reagentSystematicPrepare fresh immediately before use
False positives from contaminationSystematicRun blank tests; use fresh reagents
Exam Tip: To distinguish aldehyde from ketone: only aldehydes give a silver mirror with Tollens' or a red precipitate with Fehling's; ketones give no change. To distinguish a phenol from a carboxylic acid: both are acidic, but only the carboxylic acid fizzes with carbonate, and only the phenol gives a purple colour with neutral FeCl₃. Tertiary alcohols do NOT react with K₂Cr₂O₇ (no colour change) because they cannot be oxidised without breaking C-C bonds.

⚗ PAG8: Electrochemical Cells

Apparatus

  • Beakers, metal electrodes (Zn, Cu, Fe, Mg, etc.)
  • 1.0 mol dm⁻³ metal salt solutions
  • Salt bridge (KNO₃ soaked filter paper or agar gel)
  • High-resistance voltmeter, wires, crocodile clips
  • Emery paper

Method

  1. Clean electrodes with emery paper to remove oxide.
  2. Place each electrode in its corresponding 1.0 mol dm⁻³ metal ion solution.
  3. Connect the two half-cells with a salt bridge.
  4. Connect electrodes to the high-resistance voltmeter.
  5. Record the EMF and note the polarity (which is +, which is -).
  6. Test multiple metal combinations and compare with E° values.
  7. Investigate the effect of concentration: dilute one electrolyte (e.g. the Cu²⁺ solution in a Zn/Cu cell) and record the new cell potential.

Safety

  • Metal salt solutions may be toxic — gloves and goggles.
  • Dispose of solutions correctly.

Analysis

Calculate: E°(cell) = E°(cathode) - E°(anode). Compare measured with theoretical.

Investigate: Effect of changing concentration on EMF (qualitative Nernst).

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Oxide layer on electrodesSystematicClean with emery paper before use
Non-standard concentrationsSystematicUse volumetric flask to prepare
Salt bridge drying outSystematicFresh salt bridge; good immersion
Voltmeter not high enough resistanceSystematicUse high-resistance voltmeter or potentiometer
Exam Tip: The more reactive metal is the anode (negative terminal, oxidation). The less reactive is the cathode (positive terminal, reduction). A high-resistance voltmeter is essential because if current flows, the cell does work and the measured EMF is less than the true EMF. Remember: AN OX (anode = oxidation), RED CAT (reduction = cathode).

⚗ PAG9: Rates of Reaction — Continuous Monitoring Method

Apparatus

  • Continuous: Conical flask, gas syringe, delivery tube, stopwatch, measuring cylinder
  • Alternatives: top-pan balance (mass of CO₂ lost) or colorimeter with data logger (colour change)

Method

  1. Continuous monitoring: Collect gas at timed intervals from a reaction (e.g., CaCO₃ + HCl). Plot volume vs. time. Initial rate = gradient at t = 0.
  2. Take readings at regular intervals (e.g. every 30 s) until the reaction stops, keeping the temperature constant.
  3. Convert readings into the amount of reactant remaining (proportional to Vfinal − V) and plot concentration against time to find half-lives.

Safety

  • HCl is irritant — goggles.
  • Acid spray — use a cotton wool plug if measuring mass loss.

Analysis

Continuous: Tangent at t = 0 gives initial rate. Compare different conditions.

Half-life: Constant successive half-lives = first order; then k = ln 2 / t½. A straight-line concentration–time graph = zero order.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Gas lost before sealingSystematicSeal quickly
Reading syringe and clock at the same momentRandomTwo people, or use a data logger
Temperature variationSystematicThermostatted water bath
Volume measurement errorRandomUse burettes for better precision
Exam Tip: Continuous monitoring follows one reaction mixture all the way through, so a single run gives a whole concentration–time curve. Draw tangents to find the rate at any time. For a first-order reaction the half-life is constant and does not depend on the starting concentration, so measuring two or three successive half-lives is a quick check of order.

⚗ PAG10: Rates of Reaction — Initial Rates Method

Apparatus

  • Conical flasks or boiling tubes, burettes or graduated pipettes for each solution
  • Stopwatch; thermometer; water baths (for the temperature extension)
  • Iodine clock: potassium iodide, dilute sulfuric acid, hydrogen peroxide, sodium thiosulfate (small fixed amount), starch solution, distilled water
  • Temperature extension: sodium thiosulfate and dilute hydrochloric acid, paper marked with a cross
  • Spreadsheet software to process the results

Method

  1. Mix fixed volumes of KI(aq), H₂SO₄(aq), Na₂S₂O₃(aq) and starch in a flask. Add the H₂O₂(aq) last and start the stopwatch immediately.
  2. The iodine formed (H₂O₂ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → I₂ + 2H₂O) is immediately turned back into I⁻ by the thiosulfate. When all the thiosulfate has been used, free I₂ appears and the starch suddenly turns blue-black. Record this time, t.
  3. Repeat with different volumes of KI(aq), making up the difference with distilled water so that the total volume stays the same and only [I⁻] changes.
  4. Repeat the series varying [H₂O₂] and then [H⁺] in the same way, each time keeping the other concentrations fixed.
  5. Carry out each run at the same temperature (measure it) and repeat runs to check reliability.
  6. Temperature extension: time the disappearance of a cross under a flask of Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + HCl(aq) at several temperatures, keeping all volumes and concentrations the same.

Safety

  • Dilute sulfuric acid and hydrochloric acid are irritants — goggles.
  • Hydrogen peroxide is an irritant/oxidiser — avoid skin contact.
  • The thiosulfate/acid reaction releases SO₂ — work in a well-ventilated room and dispose of the mixture promptly; take care if you have asthma.
  • Hot water baths — care with scalds.

Analysis

Orders: Because the same small amount of I₂ is formed before the colour appears, the initial rate ∝ 1/t. Plot 1/t against the concentration you varied: a horizontal line = zero order, a straight line through the origin = first order, an upward curve = second order (confirm by plotting 1/t against concentration²). Alternatively plot log(rate) against log(concentration): the gradient is the order.

Rate constant: Once the orders are known, substitute an initial rate and the concentrations into the rate equation to find k, and work out its units.

Temperature: Plot ln(1/t) (∝ ln k) against 1/T (T in K). The gradient = −Eₐ/R, so Eₐ = −gradient × 8.314.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Judging the exact moment of colour changeRandomSame observer; white background; repeat and average; use a colorimeter/light sensor
Delay between mixing and starting the clockRandomAdd the last reagent quickly and start timing at the same moment
Total volume not kept constantSystematicMake up each run to the same total volume with distilled water
Temperature varies between runsSystematicUse a thermostatted water bath; pre-equilibrate the solutions
Exam Tip: 1/t is only a fair measure of the initial rate if the reaction has gone only a small way when the colour appears — that is why only a small, fixed amount of thiosulfate is used. Always state that you keep the concentrations of the other reactants, the total volume and the temperature constant. The units of k depend on the overall order: for rate = k[A][B], k has units dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.
Worked Example — Finding an Order:

Doubling [I⁻] (all else constant) changes the time from 84 s to 42 s, so 1/t (the initial rate) doubles → the reaction is first order with respect to I⁻.

Doubling [H⁺] leaves the time unchanged at 84 s → zero order with respect to H⁺ (H⁺ does not appear in the rate equation).

⚗ PAG11: pH Measurement

Apparatus

  • Calibrated pH meter/probe
  • Burette with NaOH (or HCl)
  • Beaker with acid (or base)
  • Magnetic stirrer
  • Buffer solutions for calibration
  • Unknown solutions of equal concentration (e.g. 0.100 mol dm⁻³ strong acid, weak acid, strong base, weak base, neutral salt)

Method (A — Identifying Unknown Solutions)

  1. Calibrate the pH meter with standard buffers; rinse the probe with distilled water between samples.
  2. Measure the pH of each unknown (all the same concentration). At 0.100 mol dm⁻³: strong acid pH 1.0, weak acid roughly 2–4, neutral salt about 7, weak base roughly 10–12, strong base 13.0.
  3. For a weak acid, use [H⁺] = 10−pH and Ka = [H⁺]²/[HA] to estimate Ka.

Method (B — pH Curve and Buffers)

  1. Calibrate pH meter with standard buffers.
  2. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of acid into beaker. Record initial pH.
  3. Add base from burette in small increments. Record pH after each addition.
  4. Use 0.1-0.2 cm³ increments near the steep equivalence region.
  5. Continue well past equivalence until pH plateaus.
  6. Plot pH vs. volume to produce the titration curve.
  7. Buffer investigation: Prepare buffer (HA + NaA). Add small amounts of HCl and NaOH. Show pH barely changes compared to water.

Safety

  • NaOH — corrosive, goggles and gloves.
  • pH probes — fragile, handle carefully.

Analysis

Identify: Equivalence point, half-equivalence point (pH = pKₐ for WA/SB), buffer region.

Select indicator: Must have colour change within the steep region of the curve.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Uncalibrated pH meterSystematicCalibrate with 2-3 buffers
Too-large increments near equivalenceSystematicUse 0.1 cm³ additions
pH not stabilisedRandomWait for stable reading; stir continuously
CO₂ absorptionSystematicFresh NaOH; keep stoppered
Exam Tip: For weak acid + strong base: initial pH > 1 (partially dissociated), buffer region before equivalence, equivalence pH > 7, half-equivalence pH = pKₐ. The buffer region exists because both HA and A⁻ are present in significant quantities. Use phenolphthalein for this combination.

⚗ PAG12: Research Skills

PAG12 is not a single fixed experiment. It is an open-ended investigation in which you research a question yourself, plan and carry out your own method, and correctly reference the sources you used. It covers the CPAC competency on using online and offline research skills and citing sources.

Apparatus

  • Depends on your plan. For the iron tablet example: iron(II) sulfate tablets, dilute HCl (to model stomach acid, pH about 1–2), a water bath at 37 °C, stirrer, stopwatch, pipettes
  • For analysis of Fe²⁺: burette and standard KMnO₄(aq) for redox titration, or a colorimeter with a suitable colour-forming reagent
  • Access to textbooks, journals, reputable websites and data books for research

Method (Investigative Approach)

  1. Research: find background information and possible methods from several sources (e.g. textbooks, the CLEAPSS/RSC resources your school uses, scientific articles). Note full details of each source as you go.
  2. Plan: write an aim and a testable question. Identify the independent, dependent and control variables and choose apparatus with suitable resolution.
  3. Risk assessment: list the hazards of each chemical and procedure and how you will reduce the risk.
  4. Trial and refine: carry out a preliminary run to choose sensible quantities and sampling times.
  5. Carry out: e.g. drop a tablet into the acid at 37 °C, withdraw samples at timed intervals, and titrate each with KMnO₄(aq) to find how much Fe²⁺ has dissolved (for a KMnO₄ titration, model the stomach acid with dilute H₂SO₄ rather than HCl, because MnO₄⁻ also oxidises Cl⁻ ions; or use colorimetry instead). Repeat to check reproducibility.
  6. Evaluate and reference: compare your findings with published information and cite every source you used in a consistent referencing style.

Safety

  • Your own written risk assessment must be checked by your teacher before you start.
  • Dilute HCl and KMnO₄ are irritants — goggles; KMnO₄ is an oxidiser and stains.
  • Iron tablets must not be taken internally or removed from the lab.

Analysis

Process data: plot the amount of Fe²⁺ released against time and find when release is complete (or 50% complete). Comment on the uncertainty in each measurement.

Referencing: for each source give the author(s), title, publisher or website, year and (for web sources) the URL and date accessed. Judge reliability: is it peer-reviewed, recent, and from a reputable organisation?

Supporting Practicals (not PAGs)

The three practicals below are not Practical Activity Groups in the OCR Chemistry B (Salters) specification. They are kept because they practise examinable techniques: colorimetry can be used for continuous monitoring (PAG9), chromatography is one of the PAG6 techniques, and the equilibrium work supports the equilibrium content of the course.

⚗ Supporting: Colorimetry and Transition Metal Complexes

Apparatus

  • Colorimeter with selectable filters, cuvettes
  • Standard solutions of known concentration (e.g., CuSO₄)
  • Volumetric flasks for dilution
  • Unknown concentration solution

Method

  1. Select the appropriate colour filter (complementary to the solution colour).
  2. Zero the colorimeter using distilled water as a blank.
  3. Prepare at least 5 standard solutions of known concentration.
  4. Measure the absorbance of each standard and the unknown.
  5. Plot a calibration curve: absorbance (y) vs. concentration (x).
  6. Read the unknown concentration from the graph using its absorbance value.

Safety

  • Transition metal salts — wear gloves.
  • Handle cuvettes by the top edges only.

Analysis

Plot: Calibration curve (should be linear at low concentrations per Beer-Lambert Law).

Read: Unknown concentration by interpolation (never extrapolation).

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Fingerprints/scratches on cuvetteSystematicHandle by top; clean with lens tissue; same orientation
Wrong filterSystematicUse complementary colour; determine λmax experimentally
Bubbles in solutionRandomDegas solutions; tap to remove bubbles
Standards incorrectly preparedSystematicUse volumetric flasks and pipettes
Exam Tip: The calibration curve must pass through the origin (zero concentration = zero absorbance after blanking). Only use values within the range of the calibration standards (interpolation, not extrapolation). If the curve is not linear at high concentrations, the Beer-Lambert Law breaks down — use only the linear portion.

⚗ Supporting: Chromatography

Apparatus

  • TLC plates (silica or alumina) or chromatography paper
  • Developing chamber (beaker with watch glass/lid)
  • Capillary tubes, pencil, ruler
  • Suitable solvent(s)
  • UV lamp or ninhydrin/iodine for visualisation

Method

  1. Draw a pencil origin line 1 cm from the bottom of the plate/paper.
  2. Spot samples (and known references) onto the origin using capillary tubes.
  3. Pour solvent into the chamber to 0.5 cm depth (below origin). Allow to saturate.
  4. Place the plate/paper in the chamber. Cover. Let solvent rise.
  5. Remove when solvent front is near the top. Mark the solvent front immediately.
  6. Visualise spots (UV, iodine, or ninhydrin for amino acids).
  7. Measure distances and calculate Rf values.

Safety

  • Solvents are flammable — no flames, fume cupboard.
  • UV lamp — avoid direct exposure to eyes.
  • Ninhydrin — irritant, wear gloves.

Analysis

Calculate: Rf = distance by spot / distance by solvent. Compare with reference standards.

TLC: Adsorption chromatography — polar substances have lower Rf (interact more with polar stationary phase).

Paper: Partition chromatography — substances partition between water in paper and mobile solvent.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Spots too large / overloadedSystematicSmall concentrated spots; dry between applications
Origin below solvent levelSystematicCheck depth carefully
Chamber not saturatedSystematicLine with filter paper; wait before inserting plate
Solvent front not marked in timeRandomMark immediately on removal
Exam Tip: TLC uses adsorption (interaction with solid stationary phase). Paper chromatography uses partition (between two liquids). In TLC, more polar compounds have lower Rf because they adsorb more strongly to the polar silica. Rf values are only comparable under identical conditions.

⚗ Supporting: Equilibrium Investigation (Le Chatelier's)

Apparatus

  • Test tubes, test tube rack
  • CoCl₂ solution (equilibrium mixture)
  • Concentrated HCl, distilled water
  • Water baths (ice and hot water)
  • Teat pipettes

Method

  1. Prepare cobalt chloride equilibrium mixture (purple at room temperature).
  2. Divide into several test tubes.
  3. Add HCl: Observe shift to blue (increased [Cl⁻] shifts equilibrium right).
  4. Add water: Observe shift to pink (dilutes Cl⁻, shifts equilibrium left).
  5. Cool in ice: Observe shift to pink (exothermic direction favoured).
  6. Heat in hot water: Observe shift to blue (endothermic direction favoured).
  7. Record all observations and explain using Le Chatelier's principle.

Safety

  • CoCl₂ is toxic and a suspected carcinogen — gloves.
  • Conc. HCl is corrosive — fume cupboard.
  • Hot water — care with burns.

Analysis

Record: Table of condition changed, direction of shift, colour observed, explanation.

Key: Forward reaction is endothermic. Heating shifts right (endothermic favoured). Adding Cl⁻ shifts right. Adding H₂O shifts left.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Subjective colour judgementRandomUse colorimeter for quantitative data
Temperature not maintainedSystematicMonitor with thermometer; maintain bath
Equilibrium not re-establishedSystematicAllow sufficient time
Uncontrolled amounts addedSystematicMeasure volumes with pipette
Exam Tip: A catalyst does NOT shift the position of equilibrium and does NOT change Kc. It only increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached. In exam questions, always state that Le Chatelier's principle predicts the system opposes the change imposed. Only temperature changes Kc; concentration and pressure changes only shift the position.
