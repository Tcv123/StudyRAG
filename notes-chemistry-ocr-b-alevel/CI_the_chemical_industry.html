1. Rate Equations and the Arrhenius Equation

Rate Equations

Rate equation: An expression showing how the rate of a reaction depends on the concentrations of reactants. For a reaction involving reactants A and B: rate = k[A]m[B]n, where k is the rate constant, m and n are the orders of reaction with respect to A and B, and the overall order is m + n.

The orders m and n can only be determined experimentally (they cannot be deduced from the balanced equation). Common orders:

OrderEffect of doubling [A]Rate–concentration graph
0 (zero order)Rate unchangedHorizontal line (rate independent of [A])
1 (first order)Rate doublesStraight line through origin (rate ∝ [A])
2 (second order)Rate quadruplesUpward curve (rate ∝ [A]2)

The rate constant k changes with temperature (increases as temperature increases) and is affected by catalysts, but is independent of concentration.

Units of the Rate Constant

The units of k are not fixed: they depend on the overall order, because whatever they are, they must make the right-hand side of the rate equation come out in mol dm−3 s−1. Rearrange the rate equation to k = rate / ([A]m[B]n) and cancel.

Overall orderRate equationUnits of k
0rate = kmol dm−3 s−1
1rate = k[A]s−1
2rate = k[A]2 or k[A][B]mol−1 dm3 s−1
3rate = k[A]2[B]mol−2 dm6 s−1

The pattern is worth committing to memory: every step up in order takes one power off mol and adds three to dm. You can also spot the order of a reaction from the units you are given — if k is quoted in mol−1 dm3 s−1, the reaction is second order overall.

Determining Orders from Experimental Data

The initial rates method uses data from experiments where the initial concentration of one reactant is varied while others are held constant. Compare pairs of experiments to see how changing the concentration of one reactant affects the initial rate.

Worked Example — Rate Equation from Initial Rates:

Nitrogen monoxide, the intermediate in nitric acid manufacture, is reduced by hydrogen:
2NO(g) + 2H2(g) → N2(g) + 2H2O(g)

Experiment[NO] / mol dm−3[H2] / mol dm−3Initial rate / mol dm−3 s−1
10.100.101.2 × 10−4
20.200.104.8 × 10−4
30.100.202.4 × 10−4
Order with respect to NO: compare 1 and 2. [H2] is unchanged; [NO] doubles and the rate goes up by 4.8/1.2 = 4 = 22. Second order.

Order with respect to H2: compare 1 and 3. [NO] is unchanged; [H2] doubles and the rate goes up by 2.4/1.2 = 2 = 21. First order.

Rate equation: rate = k[NO]2[H2], overall order 3.

Rate constant: substitute experiment 1.
k = rate / ([NO]2[H2]) = 1.2 × 10−4 / (0.102 × 0.10) = 1.2 × 10−4 / 1.0 × 10−3
k = 0.12 mol−2 dm6 s−1

Graphs and Half-Lives

The second route to an order is to follow one experiment right through and plot the results. Two kinds of graph are used, and they are easy to confuse.

OrderConcentration–time graphRate–concentration graphSuccessive half-lives
ZeroStraight line with a negative gradient; the gradient is the rate, and it is constant until the reactant runs outHorizontal lineGet shorter, each one half the previous
FirstCurve of exponential decayStraight line through the originConstant
SecondSteeper curve at the start with a long flat tailUpward curve (rate ∝ [A]2)Get longer, each one double the previous
Half-life (t½): The time taken for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half of its value. For a first-order reaction the half-life is constant — it takes the same time to go from 0.80 to 0.40 mol dm−3 as it does to go from 0.40 to 0.20 — and it is related to the rate constant by

k = ln 2 / t½ = 0.693 / t½
Worked Example — Half-Life to Rate Constant:

A concentration–time graph for the decomposition of a reactant shows the concentration falling from 0.80 to 0.40 mol dm−3 in 120 s, and from 0.40 to 0.20 mol dm−3 in a further 120 s.

The two successive half-lives are equal, so the reaction is first order.

k = 0.693 / t½ = 0.693 / 120 = 5.78 × 10−3 s−1

The unit s−1 confirms the first-order assignment.

To find a rate at a particular moment from a concentration–time curve, draw a tangent to the curve at that point and measure its gradient. The tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate. A tangent drawn by eye is the least precise step in the whole experiment, so take care over it.

Measuring Rates in Practice

Two experimental strategies appear in the practical work for this module.

  • Continuous monitoring follows one reaction mixture from start to finish by measuring a property that changes as it proceeds: the volume of gas collected in a gas syringe, the loss of mass on a balance when a gas escapes, the absorbance of a coloured species in a colorimeter, the pH, or the electrical conductivity if the number of ions changes. You can also withdraw samples at timed intervals, quench them (by adding a large volume of cold water, or by neutralising the catalyst) and titrate them. The result is a concentration–time graph, from which orders come from half-lives or from tangents.
  • The initial rates method runs several separate mixtures and measures only the very beginning of each. The usual trick is a clock reaction: arrange for a sudden, easily seen change — a blue-black colour appearing with starch, or a precipitate of sulfur obscuring a cross — after a small, fixed amount of reaction has occurred. The initial rate is then proportional to 1/t. Vary one reagent's concentration at a time, keeping the total volume constant by making up with water so that nothing else changes.
Exam Tip: In initial-rates questions, look for the pair of experiments in which only one concentration has changed — that is the pair the examiner intends you to use, and comparing any other pair will only confuse you. If no such pair exists you must divide one rate expression by another algebraically. Always finish by quoting the units of k; they carry a mark of their own and are the easiest check that your order is right.

Rate-Determining Step

Rate-determining step: The slowest step in a multi-step reaction mechanism. It acts as a bottleneck and determines the overall rate. The rate equation reflects the species involved in the rate-determining step and any preceding steps.

If the rate equation is rate = k[A][B], then both A and B (or species derived from them) are involved in or before the rate-determining step. Species that appear in the balanced equation but not in the rate equation are involved in steps after the rate-determining step.

The orders tell you how many molecules of each species take part in the rate-determining step. For the reduction of nitrogen monoxide above, rate = k[NO]2[H2], so that step must involve two NO molecules and one H2 molecule — three particles in all, even though the overall equation calls for two of each. A mechanism consistent with this is:

Step 1 (slow): 2NO + H2 → N2 + H2O2
Step 2 (fast): H2O2 + H2 → 2H2O

The two steps add up to the overall equation, and the slow step matches the rate equation. The second molecule of hydrogen is used after the bottleneck, which is precisely why it does not appear in the rate equation.

Exam Tip: Be careful with the logic in both directions. A rate equation is consistent with a proposed mechanism or it is not; it can never prove a mechanism, because several mechanisms may share the same slow step. So write "these data are consistent with …" or "these data rule out …", not "this proves the mechanism is …". When you are asked to check a proposed mechanism, do two things: add the steps to see whether they give the overall equation, and read the species in the slow step to see whether they give the rate equation.

The Arrhenius Equation

k = Ae−Ea/RT

where k is the rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor (related to collision frequency and orientation), Ea is the activation energy (J mol−1), R = 8.314 J K−1 mol−1, and T is temperature in K.

Taking natural logarithms gives the linear form:

ln k = ln A − Ea / RT

Plotting ln k against 1/T gives a straight line with gradient = −Ea/R and y-intercept = ln A. This allows Ea to be determined experimentally.

Exam Tip: The Arrhenius equation quantitatively explains why rate increases with temperature. A small increase in T produces a large increase in k because of the exponential relationship. When reading values from an Arrhenius plot, ensure you convert the gradient correctly: Ea = −gradient × R, and remember units must be consistent (J not kJ for R).
Worked Example — Determining Order from Data:

Experiment 1: [A] = 0.10, rate = 3.0 × 10−4
Experiment 2: [A] = 0.30, rate = 2.7 × 10−3

[A] is multiplied by 3 (0.10 → 0.30). Rate changes by factor 2.7 × 10−3 / 3.0 × 10−4 = 9.0 = 32
Therefore order with respect to A = 2 (second order).

When rate constants are given at two temperatures rather than as a graph, subtract one form of the equation from the other. The ln A terms cancel and you are left with a version you can use directly:

ln(k2/k1) = (Ea/R)(1/T1 − 1/T2)
Worked Example — Activation Enthalpy from Two Rate Constants:

For the oxidation of sulfur dioxide over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst, k = 1.20 × 10−3 mol−1 dm3 s−1 at 500 K and 9.60 × 10−3 mol−1 dm3 s−1 at 550 K. Calculate Ea. (R = 8.314 J K−1 mol−1)

ln(k2/k1) = ln(9.60 × 10−3 / 1.20 × 10−3) = ln 8.00 = 2.079
1/T1 − 1/T2 = 1/500 − 1/550 = 2.000 × 10−3 − 1.818 × 10−3 = 1.818 × 10−4 K−1

Ea = R × ln(k2/k1) / (1/T1 − 1/T2) = 8.314 × 2.079 / 1.818 × 10−4
Ea = 9.51 × 104 J mol−1 = 95.1 kJ mol−1

Note that a 50 K rise — 10% in absolute temperature — has multiplied the rate constant by eight.
Worked Example — Activation Enthalpy from a Graph:

A plot of ln k against 1/T is a straight line of gradient −1.14 × 104 K. Calculate Ea.

gradient = −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × R
Ea = 1.14 × 104 × 8.314 = 9.48 × 104 J mol−1 = 94.8 kJ mol−1

The y-intercept of the same line is ln A, so A = e(intercept).

2. Equilibrium Constants

The Equilibrium Constant Kc

For a general reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD

Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b

where square brackets denote equilibrium concentrations in mol dm−3. Kc is constant at a given temperature. A large Kc means the equilibrium lies to the right (products favoured); a small Kc means it lies to the left (reactants favoured).

Only species in the aqueous or gaseous phase are included in the expression. Pure solids and pure liquids are excluded because their concentrations are effectively constant.

Working Out the Units of Kc

You met Kc in ES without worrying about units. Here you must work them out, and the method never varies: write the expression with mol dm−3 in place of every concentration, and cancel.

EquilibriumKc expressionUnits
H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI[HI]2 / ([H2][I2])None — the units cancel exactly
N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2[NO2]2 / [N2O4]mol dm−3
2SO2 + O2 ⇌ 2SO3[SO3]2 / ([SO2]2[O2])mol−1 dm3
N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3[NH3]2 / ([N2][H2]3)mol−2 dm6

In short, the power of (mol dm−3) in the units is the number of moles of gas or solution species on the product side minus the number on the reactant side. When those totals are equal, Kc has no units at all — and "no units" is a perfectly good answer that you must write down rather than leave blank.

Worked Example — Kc for the Contact Process, with Units:

2.00 mol of SO2 and 1.00 mol of O2 are sealed in a 1.00 dm3 vessel with a catalyst and allowed to reach equilibrium, at which point 1.80 mol of SO3 has formed. Calculate Kc.

Step 1 — amounts used. 2 mol SO3 is formed from 2 mol SO2 and 1 mol O2, so 1.80 mol SO3 has consumed 1.80 mol SO2 and 0.90 mol O2.

Step 2 — equilibrium amounts.
SO2: 2.00 − 1.80 = 0.20 mol    O2: 1.00 − 0.90 = 0.10 mol    SO3: 1.80 mol

Step 3 — concentrations. The volume is 1.00 dm3, so the concentrations are numerically the same: 0.20, 0.10 and 1.80 mol dm−3.

Step 4 — substitute.
Kc = 1.802 / (0.202 × 0.10) = 3.24 / (0.040 × 0.10) = 3.24 / 4.0 × 10−3
Kc = 810 mol−1 dm3

Kc >> 1, so the equilibrium lies well over towards sulfur trioxide — which is exactly what makes the Contact process worth running.

Measuring Kc Experimentally

To determine Kc you need the equilibrium concentrations, and the difficulty is that any measurement which consumes a reactant will disturb the very equilibrium you are trying to measure. The standard solution is to mix known amounts, leave the mixture in a thermostatted bath for long enough to be sure equilibrium has been reached (confirmed by taking readings until they stop changing), then withdraw a sample, quench it — typically by running it into a large volume of cold water, which both dilutes it and chills it so that the position of equilibrium is effectively frozen — and titrate it at once. Knowing one equilibrium amount and the starting amounts, the stoichiometry gives all the others.

The Equilibrium Constant Kp

For gaseous equilibria, Kp is expressed in terms of partial pressures:

Kp = pCc · pDd / (pAa · pBb)
Partial pressure: The pressure that a gas would exert if it alone occupied the total volume of the mixture at the same temperature. The partial pressure of gas A is: pA = xA × Ptotal, where xA is the mole fraction of A.

Mole fraction: xA = moles of A / total moles of all gases.

The total pressure equals the sum of all partial pressures (Dalton's law): Ptotal = pA + pB + pC + ...

Exam Tip: When calculating Kp, pay careful attention to units. The units of Kp depend on the stoichiometry of the reaction. Work out the overall power of pressure in the numerator minus the denominator to determine the unit (e.g. Pa, kPa, atm, or dimensionless).
Worked Example — Kp from Mole Fractions:

An equilibrium mixture of the brown gas NO2 and its colourless dimer N2O4,
N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g),
contains 0.60 mol N2O4 and 0.40 mol NO2 at a total pressure of 100 kPa. Calculate Kp.

Step 1 — total moles of gas: 0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00 mol

Step 2 — mole fractions: x(N2O4) = 0.60/1.00 = 0.60; x(NO2) = 0.40/1.00 = 0.40
(They must add to 1 — check this every time.)

Step 3 — partial pressures: p = x × Ptotal
p(N2O4) = 0.60 × 100 = 60 kPa; p(NO2) = 0.40 × 100 = 40 kPa

Step 4 — substitute:
Kp = p(NO2)2 / p(N2O4) = 402 / 60 = 1600 / 60 = 26.7 kPa

The unit is kPa because there are two moles of gas on top and one underneath: kPa2/kPa = kPa.

What Changes the Value of K — and What Does Not

This is the point of the whole section, and it is the one examiners test hardest, because the everyday language of "shifting the equilibrium" invites the wrong answer.

ChangePosition of equilibriumValue of Kc or Kp
Increase the concentration of a reactantShifts rightUnchanged
Increase the total pressureShifts towards the side with fewer moles of gas (no shift if the totals are equal)Unchanged
Add a catalystUnchanged — equilibrium is simply reached soonerUnchanged
Increase the temperature, forward reaction exothermicShifts leftDecreases
Increase the temperature, forward reaction endothermicShifts rightIncreases

The logic behind the first two rows is worth spelling out, because it is the answer to "explain why". K is a fixed number at a fixed temperature. If you raise one concentration on the bottom of the expression, the value would momentarily fall below K, so the reaction proceeds forward until the top has risen and the bottom fallen enough to restore the original value. The position has moved; the constant has not. Only a change of temperature alters K itself, and it does so in the direction that absorbs the energy you have added — which is why K for the Contact process, an exothermic reaction, gets smaller as the plant is run hotter.

3. Nitrogen Chemistry

The storyline opens with crop production and the nitrogen cycle, and the chemistry that follows is all about one awkward fact: the atmosphere is 78% nitrogen, and almost nothing can use it.

Bonding in Nitrogen, Ammonia and the Ammonium Ion

A nitrogen atom has the configuration 1s2 2s2 2p3 — five outer electrons, three of them unpaired.

  • N2 forms when two nitrogen atoms share three pairs of electrons, giving a triple bond, N≡N, and leaving a lone pair on each atom. The bond enthalpy is 945 kJ mol−1, one of the strongest bonds in all of chemistry, and this is why nitrogen gas is so unreactive: almost any reaction has to start by breaking it, so the activation enthalpy is enormous. It is also why the Haber process needs a catalyst and high temperature, and why explosives based on nitrogen compounds release so much energy — forming N≡N is hugely exothermic.
  • NH3 has three bonding pairs and one lone pair around the nitrogen. Four electron pairs arrange themselves tetrahedrally, but only three are bonds, so the shape is pyramidal. The lone pair repels more strongly than the bonding pairs, closing the H–N–H angle from 109.5° to 107°. The molecule is polar and hydrogen bonds to its neighbours, which is why ammonia is so soluble in water and can be liquefied so easily.
  • NH4+ forms when that lone pair is donated to an H+ ion, which has no electrons of its own. Both electrons in the new bond come from the nitrogen, making it a dative covalent (coordinate) bond, usually drawn as an arrow. Once formed it is identical in length and strength to the other three, so all four bonds are equivalent: four bonding pairs and no lone pair give a tetrahedral ion with bond angles of 109.5°.

Exam Tip: Use the systematic names in answers. NO3− is the nitrate(V) ion and NO2− is the nitrate(III) ion; "nitrite" is the older name for the latter and is best avoided, since the two are so easily confused. Work out the oxidation state from the charge every time rather than trying to remember: in NO2−, the two oxygens contribute 2 × (−2) = −4, and the overall charge is −1, so nitrogen must be +3.

Interconverting the Nitrogen Species

In the soil, bacteria do the work. Nitrogen fixation converts N2 to ammonia, either by root-nodule bacteria in legumes, by lightning, or industrially in the Haber process. Nitrification then oxidises the ammonium ion in two bacterial steps:

2NH4+ + 3O2 → 2NO2− + 2H2O + 4H+
2NO2− + O2 → 2NO3−

Nitrate(V) is the form plants take up, which is why it is the form fertilisers supply. Denitrification by anaerobic bacteria runs the cycle back the other way, reducing nitrate(V) all the way to nitrogen gas and returning it to the air:

2NO3− + 12H+ + 10e− → N2 + 6H2O

Industrially the same interconversions are carried out deliberately: ammonia is oxidised to NO, NO to NO2, and NO2 to nitric acid. That is the Ostwald process, and it is the subject of the next section.

Testing for Ammonium and Nitrate(V) Ions

Both tests end with the same observation — ammonia gas — so the order in which you do them matters.

  1. Ammonium, NH4+: add sodium hydroxide solution to the sample and warm gently. Ammonia is displaced from its salt by the stronger base and is detected by its sharp smell and by holding damp red litmus paper in the mouth of the tube, where it turns blue.
    NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq) → NH3(g) + H2O(l)
  2. Nitrate(V), NO3−: to a fresh portion add sodium hydroxide solution and a little Devarda's alloy (an alloy of aluminium, copper and zinc), then warm. The aluminium reduces nitrate(V) in alkaline solution all the way down to ammonia, which is detected in the same way.
    3NO3− + 8Al + 5OH− + 18H2O → 3NH3 + 8Al(OH)4−

Do the first test before the second. If ammonia is given off with sodium hydroxide alone, the sample already contained ammonium ions, and you cannot then tell whether the ammonia produced with Devarda's alloy came from nitrate or from the ammonium that was there all along.

4. Three Industrial Processes

The module hangs its chemistry on three real plants: nitric acid, sulfuric acid and ethanoic acid. You are not expected to recall the details — the specification says information will be given — but you are expected to be able to read a flow diagram and explain, in terms of rate and equilibrium, why a process is run the way it is. The three below are worth knowing well simply because they are the examples you are most likely to be handed.

Nitric Acid: the Ostwald Process

Ammonia from the Haber process is oxidised in three stages.

Stage 1 — catalytic oxidation of ammonia.

4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)    ΔH = −905 kJ mol−1

Ammonia mixed with air is passed over gauzes of platinum with about 10% rhodium at roughly 900 °C and a few atmospheres. The contact time is of the order of a millisecond: leave the gases on the catalyst any longer and the ammonia is oxidised straight through to nitrogen, which is worthless. The reaction is so exothermic that once lit it keeps the gauzes white-hot without further heating, and the waste heat downstream raises steam.

Stage 2 — oxidation of nitrogen monoxide.

2NO(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g)    ΔH = −114 kJ mol−1

The gases are cooled before this step, for two reasons that point the same way: the reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature pushes the equilibrium towards NO2, and unusually this reaction also goes faster as it is cooled. Three moles of gas become two, so raised pressure helps as well.

Stage 3 — absorption in water.

3NO2(g) + H2O(l) → 2HNO3(aq) + NO(g)

The gas stream meets water flowing the other way down a tall absorption tower. A third of the nitrogen appears as nitrogen monoxide again, so extra air is blown in and the NO is recycled through stage 2 until almost all of it has ended up as acid. Taking the recycling into account, the overall absorption is:

4NO2(g) + O2(g) + 2H2O(l) → 4HNO3(aq)

The tail gas leaving the top of the tower still contains a little NO2, which would be visible as a brown plume and is both toxic and a cause of acid rain, so it is catalytically reduced or scrubbed before release.

Sulfuric Acid: the Contact Process

Stage 1 — make sulfur dioxide, by burning molten sulfur in dry air, or by roasting a sulfide ore:

S(l) + O2(g) → SO2(g)    ΔH = −297 kJ mol−1

Stage 2 — the equilibrium step, over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst at about 450 °C and only 1–2 atmospheres:

2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g)    ΔH = −196 kJ mol−1

The catalyst works by a redox cycle, being reduced by the sulfur dioxide and reoxidised by the oxygen, so it is returned unchanged at the end of each turnover:

SO2 + V2O5 → SO3 + V2O4     2V2O4 + O2 → 2V2O5

Stage 3 — absorption. Sulfur trioxide is not added straight to water: that reaction is so violently exothermic that it produces an uncontrollable mist of sulfuric acid droplets which is impossible to condense and escapes from the plant. Instead the SO3 is absorbed into concentrated sulfuric acid to give oleum, which is then diluted with the right amount of water:

SO3(g) + H2SO4(l) → H2S2O7(l)
H2S2O7(l) + H2O(l) → 2H2SO4(l)

The net effect is the same as SO3 + H2O → H2SO4, but under control. Passing the gas through a second catalyst bed and a second absorber — the double-absorption design — raises the overall conversion above 99.5% and so cuts the sulfur dioxide leaving the chimney to a level that emission regulations permit.

Ethanoic Acid: Carbonylation of Methanol

Most of the world's ethanoic acid is now made in a single step by reacting methanol with carbon monoxide:

CH3OH(l) + CO(g) → CH3COOH(l)

The reaction is run at roughly 150–200 °C and 30–40 atmospheres over a homogeneous catalyst — a rhodium complex in the older Monsanto process, an iridium complex in the Cativa process that superseded it — with iodomethane as a promoter. Both metals are dissolved in the reaction mixture rather than sitting on a surface, which gives excellent contact with the reactants but means the catalyst has to be separated from the product and recycled.

The striking feature of this process is its atom economy of 100%: there is only one product, so every atom of methanol and carbon monoxide ends up in the ethanoic acid. Contrast that with the routes it replaced — the air oxidation of naphtha or butane, which produced a spread of oxygenated by-products that had to be separated, and the two-stage conversion of ethene to ethanal and then to ethanoic acid. Both raw materials come from synthesis gas, made from methane and steam, so the whole chain is built on natural gas.

Exam Tip: Questions on these processes give you the data and ask you to interpret it, so practise the interpretation rather than memorising flow diagrams. Look first at the sign of ΔH, then at the number of moles of gas on each side, then at whether the arrow is reversible. Those three pieces of information generate almost every mark available: exothermic and reversible means low temperature for yield but a compromise for rate; fewer gas moles on the right means high pressure helps the yield; a single arrow means yield is not the issue and you should be talking about rate and selectivity instead.

5. Choosing the Best Conditions for an Industrial Process

An industrial chemist is not trying to maximise yield. They are trying to produce the most product per pound spent, and the conditions that give the best yield are almost never the conditions that do that. Four considerations pull against each other.

Temperature: the Central Compromise

For an exothermic reaction, raising the temperature moves the equilibrium to the left and reduces K — a lower yield. But lowering the temperature reduces the rate constant exponentially, so the mixture takes far longer to reach equilibrium, and a plant producing a wonderful yield after three weeks is worthless. The chosen temperature is therefore a compromise: high enough for an acceptable rate, low enough for an acceptable yield.

Exam Tip: Never write only "a high temperature gives a faster rate" or only "a low temperature gives a better yield". The mark is for the compromise, and it is awarded for saying both halves and then naming the chosen value as the balance between them.

Pressure: Yield Against Cost

Raising the pressure shifts a gaseous equilibrium towards whichever side has fewer moles of gas, and also speeds the reaction up by bringing the molecules closer together. It costs a great deal, though: compressors consume large amounts of energy, vessels and pipework must be thicker and are more expensive to build and inspect, and a leak or rupture at high pressure is far more dangerous. High pressure is therefore used only where it buys enough yield to pay for itself.

The two headline processes make the point neatly. The Haber process runs at around 200 atm because at 1 atm the conversion to ammonia would be hopelessly low. The Contact process, by contrast, runs at barely more than atmospheric pressure — even though it too has fewer moles on the right — because the conversion at 1–2 atm is already over 99%, and there is almost nothing left to gain.

Catalysts

A catalyst provides an alternative route of lower activation enthalpy, so a much greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to react. It increases the rate of the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor, so it changes neither the position of equilibrium nor the value of K — equilibrium is simply reached sooner. The real economic benefit is indirect: because the catalyst has restored the rate, the plant can be run at a lower temperature, which for an exothermic reaction improves the yield and cuts the fuel bill at the same time. Catalysts are expensive and can be poisoned by impurities in the feedstock, which is why plants include purification steps upstream of the reactor.

Removing Product and Recycling Reactants

Two further tricks appear on almost every flow diagram. Removing the product as it forms — condensing ammonia to a liquid, absorbing nitrogen dioxide into water — keeps the top of the equilibrium expression low, so the reaction keeps moving forward and a modest single-pass conversion becomes a high overall conversion. And recycling the unreacted gas back to the reactor means that a single pass converting only 15% of the feed can still convert nearly all of it in the end. Both are reasons why a poor equilibrium yield need not rule a process out.

6. Costs, Benefits and Risks

The last learning outcome in this module is not chemistry at all; it is the question of whether a process is worth running. You will be given data and asked to analyse it, so what matters is knowing which headings to sort that data under.

Where the Money Goes

TypeWhat it coversBehaviour

Set against these are the credits. A co-product is something made deliberately alongside the main product and sold; a by-product is something the reaction produces whether you want it or not. If a by-product can be sold rather than disposed of, it turns a cost into income — the recovered heat from the Contact process is sold as steam or used to generate electricity, and ammonium sulfate from acid-gas scrubbing is sold as fertiliser. A process with a low atom economy is far more tolerable when its by-product has a market.

Benefits

  • Nitric acid and ammonia underpin the fertiliser industry, and with it a large part of the world's food supply; they also lead to nylon precursors, dyes and explosives.
  • Sulfuric acid goes into phosphate fertilisers, detergents, pigments, batteries and the manufacture of almost every other industrial chemical — which is why its consumption has traditionally been used as a rough measure of a country's industrial activity.
  • Ethanoic acid leads to ethenyl ethanoate for adhesives and emulsion paints, to cellulose acetate fibres and films, and to solvents.

Hazards and Risks

Hazard: A property of a substance or a situation that has the potential to cause harm.

Risk: The likelihood that the harm will actually occur in the circumstances of use. Risk depends on the hazard, the quantity involved, and the control measures in place — which is why a very hazardous substance can be handled at low risk, and a mildly hazardous one at high risk if it is handled carelessly or in bulk.

Exam Tip: "Evaluate" and "discuss" questions here are marked for balance and for using the figures you are given. Quote numbers from the stem, put points on both sides, and finish with a judgement that follows from what you have written. A common trap is to treat a low atom economy or a high energy cost as automatically damning: say instead that it is a drawback, weigh it against the yield, the rate, the hazards and whether the by-product can be sold, and only then conclude.
