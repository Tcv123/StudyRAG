1. Oxidation States and Redox Reactions

Oxidation States (Oxidation Numbers)

Oxidation state: A number assigned to an atom in a compound that represents the number of electrons it has lost, gained, or shared unequally compared to the uncombined element. It is a bookkeeping device for tracking electron transfer.

Rules for assigning oxidation states:

  • Uncombined elements have an oxidation state of 0.
  • In simple ions, the oxidation state equals the charge (e.g. Na+ = +1, Cl− = −1).
  • Oxygen is −2 (except in peroxides where it is −1, and in OF2 where it is +2).
  • Hydrogen is +1 (except in metal hydrides where it is −1).
  • Fluorine is always −1.
  • The sum of oxidation states in a neutral compound is 0; in a polyatomic ion it equals the ionic charge.

Redox Definitions

Oxidation: Loss of electrons / increase in oxidation state.

Reduction: Gain of electrons / decrease in oxidation state.

Reducing agent: A substance that donates electrons (is itself oxidised).

Oxidising agent: A substance that accepts electrons (is itself reduced).

Remember: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Half-Equations

A redox reaction can be split into two half-equations: one showing oxidation and one showing reduction. To write and balance half-equations:

  1. Write the species being oxidised or reduced on each side.
  2. Balance atoms other than O and H.
  3. Balance oxygen by adding H2O.
  4. Balance hydrogen by adding H+.
  5. Balance charge by adding electrons (e−).

To combine half-equations, multiply each so the electrons cancel, then add the two equations together and simplify.

Exam Tip: When balancing redox equations, always check that atoms and charges balance on both sides. A common mistake is forgetting to balance hydrogen atoms after adding water molecules. Also check that the electrons cancel completely when combining half-equations.

2. Dynamic Equilibrium

Dynamic equilibrium: A state reached in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant but are not necessarily equal. Both forward and reverse reactions continue to occur at the molecular level.

Conditions for dynamic equilibrium:

  • The system must be closed (no substances can enter or leave).
  • The forward and reverse rates must be equal.
  • The concentrations of all species remain constant over time.
  • The reaction must be reversible.

Le Chatelier's Principle

Le Chatelier's principle: If a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions (concentration, pressure, or temperature), the position of equilibrium will shift in the direction that tends to oppose the change, so as to partially restore the original conditions.
ChangeEffect on equilibrium positionEffect on K
Increase concentration of a reactantShifts to the right (towards products)No change
Decrease concentration of a productShifts to the rightNo change
Increase pressureShifts towards the side with fewer moles of gasNo change
Increase temperatureShifts in the endothermic directionChanges (increases if forward reaction is endothermic)
Add a catalystNo change (speeds up both forward and reverse equally)No change
Exam Tip: A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium or the value of K. It only increases the rate at which equilibrium is reached. Temperature is the only factor that changes the value of the equilibrium constant.

The Equilibrium Constant, Kc

Le Chatelier's principle tells you which way an equilibrium will move, but not how far. For that you need a number, and the number is the equilibrium constant.

Equilibrium constant Kc: For a homogeneous reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc is defined by

Kc = [C]c[D]d / ([A]a[B]b)

where the square brackets mean equilibrium concentrations in mol dm−3. Products go on top, reactants underneath, and each concentration is raised to the power of its balancing number. Kc has a fixed value for a given reaction at a given temperature.

"Homogeneous" means every species is in the same phase — all gases, or all in solution. You are not asked to work out the units of Kc in this storyline; that comes later, in CI.

The size of Kc tells you at a glance where the equilibrium sits:

Value of KcPosition of equilibriumWhat you would see
Kc >> 1Far to the rightAlmost complete conversion to products
Kc > 1To the rightMore product than reactant at equilibrium
Kc ≈ 1Neither side favouredComparable amounts of both
Kc < 1To the leftMore reactant than product at equilibrium
Kc << 1Far to the leftBarely any reaction appears to happen
Worked Example — Writing and Evaluating Kc:

The Deacon process oxidises waste hydrogen chloride back to chlorine:
4HCl(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2Cl2(g) + 2H2O(g)

Kc = [Cl2]2[H2O]2 / ([HCl]4[O2])

At a particular temperature the equilibrium concentrations are [HCl] = 0.20, [O2] = 0.10, [Cl2] = 0.30 and [H2O] = 0.30 mol dm−3.

Kc = (0.302 × 0.302) / (0.204 × 0.10)
    = (0.090 × 0.090) / (1.6 × 10−3 × 0.10)
    = 8.1 × 10−3 / 1.6 × 10−4 = 50.6

Kc is comfortably greater than 1, so under these conditions the equilibrium lies towards the chlorine.
Worked Example — Kc from Starting Amounts:

1.00 mol of HCl and 0.50 mol of O2 are sealed in a 2.00 dm3 vessel and allowed to reach equilibrium, at which point 0.40 mol of Cl2 is present. Calculate Kc.

Step 1 — use the stoichiometry. 2 mol Cl2 is made from 4 mol HCl and 1 mol O2, and makes 2 mol H2O. So making 0.40 mol Cl2 uses 0.80 mol HCl and 0.20 mol O2, and makes 0.40 mol H2O.

Step 2 — equilibrium moles.
HCl: 1.00 − 0.80 = 0.20 mol    O2: 0.50 − 0.20 = 0.30 mol
Cl2: 0.40 mol    H2O: 0.40 mol

Step 3 — divide by the volume (2.00 dm3) to get concentrations:
[HCl] = 0.10, [O2] = 0.15, [Cl2] = 0.20, [H2O] = 0.20 mol dm−3

Step 4 — substitute.
Kc = (0.202 × 0.202) / (0.104 × 0.15) = 1.6 × 10−3 / 1.5 × 10−5 = 107 (3 s.f.)

Using Kc to Explain a Shift

Le Chatelier's principle is a rule of thumb; Kc explains why the rule works. The argument is always the same: Kc cannot change unless the temperature changes, so if you alter one concentration, the others must adjust to put the expression back to the same value.

Take the equilibrium that gives bleach its chemistry:

Cl2(aq) + 2OH−(aq) ⇌ ClO−(aq) + Cl−(aq) + H2O(l)

Add more hydroxide ions and the bottom of the Kc expression becomes larger. For Kc to stay the same, the top must become larger too, so [ClO−] and [Cl−] rise: the position of equilibrium moves to the right and more bleach is made. Remove chlorate(I) ions — by using the bleach — and the top becomes smaller, so the bottom must fall as well: more chlorine and hydroxide react, and the equilibrium again moves right. Both conclusions are exactly what Le Chatelier's principle predicts, but now you can say why.

Exam Tip: Two habits will protect the marks here. Put the products on top every time, even when the equation is written backwards on the page, and raise each concentration to its balancing number rather than multiplying by it — [Cl2]2, not 2[Cl2]. And when a question gives you moles and a vessel volume, convert to concentrations before substituting: forgetting to divide by the volume is the single commonest way to lose every mark in an otherwise perfect Kc calculation.

3. Halogen Chemistry

Sea water is a halide solution, and the halogens are the industrial products won from it. Group 17 elements have the outer configuration ns2np5, are one electron short of a noble gas configuration, and exist as diatomic molecules X2.

Physical Properties

HalogenAppearance and state at room temperatureBoiling point / °CColour in waterColour in cyclohexane
F2Pale yellow gas−188Reacts with water—
Cl2Pale green gas−34Very pale greenPale green / almost colourless
Br2Red-brown liquid59Orange / yellowOrange
I2Grey-black solid, sublimes to a purple vapour184Barely soluble; pale brownViolet / purple

Boiling points rise and volatility falls down the group. The molecules are non-polar, so the only intermolecular bonds are instantaneous dipole–induced dipole bonds. Going down the group each molecule has more electrons, so the induced dipoles are larger, these bonds are stronger, and more energy is needed to separate the molecules.

The halogens are only slightly soluble in water (they are non-polar) but dissolve readily in non-polar organic solvents such as cyclohexane. Shaking an aqueous halogen with cyclohexane and looking at the colour of the upper organic layer is the standard way of telling bromine from iodine.

The Halogens as Oxidising Agents

A halogen reacts by gaining one electron per atom:

X2 + 2e− → 2X−

Oxidising power decreases down the group. Down the group the atomic radius increases and there is more shielding, so the nucleus attracts an incoming electron less strongly and the halogen gains electrons less readily. Chlorine is therefore a stronger oxidising agent than bromine, which is stronger than iodine.

Displacement Reactions of the Halogens

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. These are the reactions to know, with observations:

MixtureObservationIonic equation
Cl2(aq) + KBr(aq)Colourless solution turns orangeCl2 + 2Br− → 2Cl− + Br2
Cl2(aq) + KI(aq)Colourless solution turns brown; a dark solid may appearCl2 + 2I− → 2Cl− + I2
Br2(aq) + KI(aq)Orange solution turns brownBr2 + 2I− → 2Br− + I2
Br2(aq) + KCl(aq)No changeNo reaction
I2(aq) + KCl(aq) or KBr(aq)No changeNo reaction

Each is a redox reaction. Taking chlorine and bromide as the example, the half-equations are:

Reduction: Cl2 + 2e− → 2Cl−     Oxidation: 2Br− → Br2 + 2e−

Chlorine is the oxidising agent (it is reduced, −1 from 0); the bromide ion is the reducing agent (it is oxidised, 0 from −1).

Extracting the Halogens from the Sea

Sea water contains about 65 parts per million of bromide ion. Bromine is recovered by exploiting exactly the displacement chemistry above:

  1. The sea water is acidified and chlorine is blown through it, oxidising bromide to bromine: Cl2 + 2Br− → 2Cl− + Br2
  2. Air is blown through to carry the volatile bromine out of the very dilute solution.
  3. The bromine vapour is absorbed in sulfur dioxide and water, which reduces it back to bromide and concentrates it: Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O → 2HBr + H2SO4
  4. Chlorine is used again on the concentrated solution to release pure bromine: Cl2 + 2HBr → 2HCl + Br2

Chlorine itself is made by the electrolysis of concentrated sodium chloride solution (brine), covered in the next section.

Exam Tip: The bromine extraction looks circular — bromine is made, turned back into bromide, then made again. The point of the middle two steps is concentration: bromine in sea water is far too dilute to collect directly, so it is transferred into a small volume of solution before the final oxidation. Say that explicitly if you are asked to explain the process.

Testing for Halide Ions with Silver Nitrate

Add dilute nitric acid (to remove carbonate and hydroxide ions, which would also give precipitates) and then silver nitrate solution:

HalidePrecipitateIonic equationDilute NH3Concentrated NH3

The three precipitate colours are hard to tell apart in poor light, which is why the ammonia step matters: solubility in ammonia decreases from AgCl to AgI, so the sequence of tests identifies the halide unambiguously. Silver halides darken in sunlight as Ag+ is reduced to silver metal — the chemistry behind photographic film.

The Hydrogen Halides

Adding concentrated sulfuric acid to a solid halide shows the reducing power of the halide ions increasing down the group.

Because sulfuric acid oxidises HBr and HI, it cannot be used to prepare them. Instead the solid halide is treated with phosphoric acid, which is not an oxidising agent, so pure HBr or HI is obtained.

The hydrogen halides share two properties and differ in a third:

  • Acidity (similar): all dissolve in water to give strongly acidic solutions, e.g. HCl(g) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + Cl−(aq).
  • Reaction with ammonia (similar): all give dense white smoke of the ammonium halide, e.g. NH3(g) + HCl(g) → NH4Cl(s).
  • Thermal stability (different): decreases down the group, because the H–X bond gets longer and weaker. HCl is essentially unaffected by a hot wire; HBr partly decomposes; HI decomposes readily to give purple iodine vapour.

Iodine–Thiosulfate Titrations

Iodine is a convenient thing to titrate because the end point is so sharp. Sodium thiosulfate reduces iodine back to iodide:

I2(aq) + 2S2O32−(aq) → 2I−(aq) + S4O62−(aq)

Thiosulfate is run in from the burette until the brown iodine colour fades to pale straw. Only then is starch solution added, turning the mixture blue-black, and the titration is finished dropwise until the blue-black colour disappears. Starch is added late because it forms a stubborn complex with iodine at high concentration, which would make the end point drift.

Worked Example — Iodine–Thiosulfate Titration:

A 25.0 cm3 portion of an iodine solution required 22.40 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 sodium thiosulfate solution to reach the end point. Calculate the concentration of the iodine solution.

n(S2O32−) = 0.100 × 22.40/1000 = 2.24 × 10−3 mol
The equation shows 2 mol thiosulfate per 1 mol iodine, so n(I2) = 2.24 × 10−3 / 2 = 1.12 × 10−3 mol
c(I2) = 1.12 × 10−3 / (25.0/1000) = 0.0448 mol dm−3

Chlorine: Weighing Risks Against Benefits

Chlorine dissolves in and reacts with water in a disproportionation reaction — chlorine is simultaneously oxidised to +1 and reduced to −1:

Cl2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq)

Chloric(I) acid, HClO, is the species that kills bacteria. With cold dilute sodium hydroxide the same disproportionation gives household bleach:

Cl2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H2O(l)
RisksBenefits
Toxic gas that attacks the lungs; used as a chemical weapon in the First World WarSterilising drinking water has saved enormous numbers of lives from cholera and typhoid
Transported as a liquid under pressure, so a tanker accident could release a dense cloud that hugs the groundBleaching paper and textiles
Can form chlorinated organic by-products in treated waterFeedstock for PVC, solvents and many medicines

The judgement the storyline asks for is not "chlorine is dangerous" but a comparison: the very small risk from a carefully regulated supply chain set against a very large public-health benefit, and against the risks of the alternatives.

4. Electrolysis

Electrolysis: The decomposition of a molten or dissolved ionic compound by passing a direct electric current through it. The compound being decomposed is the electrolyte; it must contain ions that are free to move.

The two electrodes do opposite jobs, and the names are worth fixing once:

Electrolysis of Molten Salts

With no water present, the only ions available are those of the salt itself, so the products are simply the element from each ion.

Molten sodium chloride:

Cathode: Na+ + e− → Na      Anode: 2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−

Molten aluminium oxide (dissolved in molten cryolite to lower the operating temperature):

Cathode: Al3+ + 3e− → Al      Anode: 2O2− → O2 + 4e−

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

In solution the ions of the salt have to compete with water, and the rules are:

ElectrodeRuleHalf-equation
CathodeIf the metal is in Group 1 or Group 2, or is aluminium, hydrogen is given off from water instead of the metal2H2O + 2e− → H2 + 2OH−
Any other metal is plated onto the electrodee.g. Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu
AnodeIf a halide ion is present in reasonable concentration, the halogen is released2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−
Otherwise (sulfate, nitrate) oxygen is released from water2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e−

Concentrated sodium chloride solution (brine) is the industrially important case, and it is where the chlorine of this storyline comes from:

Cathode: 2H2O + 2e− → H2(g) + 2OH−(aq)      Anode: 2Cl− → Cl2(g) + 2e−

Sodium ions and hydroxide ions are left behind, so the chlor-alkali process yields three saleable products from sea salt and electricity: chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide. A modern membrane cell uses an ion-exchange membrane that lets Na+ through but keeps the chlorine and the hydroxide apart — if they mixed they would react to give bleach rather than the two separate products.

Copper(II) sulfate solution with inert (graphite or platinum) electrodes:

Cathode: Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu(s)      Anode: 2H2O → O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e−

Copper is not in Group 1 or 2, so it plates out; sulfate is not a halide, so oxygen comes off instead. The solution loses its blue colour and becomes acidic as H+ builds up.

Exam Tip: Electrons never appear in an overall equation, only in half-equations. When you combine them, scale each half-equation so the electrons cancel exactly — for brine, the cathode and anode half-equations both involve 2e−, so they add directly to give 2Cl− + 2H2O → Cl2 + H2 + 2OH−. Always check that charge as well as atoms balances in a half-equation.

5. Atom Economy

The three routes to a hydrogen halide met earlier in this storyline all work, and all give a good yield. They are nonetheless very different processes to run, and the reason is the one this section is about: how much of the material you put in ends up in the product you actually want.

Atom economy: The proportion of the mass of the reactants that ends up in the desired product.

Atom economy = (Mr of the desired product × its balancing number) / (sum of the Mr of all products, each × its balancing number) × 100%

Because a balanced equation has the same total mass on both sides, you may equally well divide by the total Mr of the reactants; the answer is the same.

Atom Economy is Not Percentage Yield

These two measures are constantly confused, and they answer different questions.

Percentage yieldAtom economy
What it measuresHow much of the theoretical maximum you actually obtainedHow much of the reactant mass is capable of ending up in the product
Depends onHow well the reaction and the separation were carried out; losses, side reactions, position of equilibriumThe balanced equation alone
Can it be improved by better technique?YesNo — only by choosing a different reaction
Calculated fromExperimental massesRelative formula masses

A reaction with only one product has an atom economy of 100% however badly the experiment goes. Addition reactions are therefore always 100% atom-economical; substitution and elimination reactions never are, because something else is always formed alongside the product.

Three Routes to a Hydrogen Halide Compared

Worked Example — Atom Economy of Three Preparations:

(Ar: H 1.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, P 31.0, S 32.1, Cl 35.5, Br 79.9)

Route 1 — direct synthesis of HCl: H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl
There is only one product, so the atom economy is 100%. Every atom put in comes out in the hydrogen chloride.

Route 2 — HCl from salt and sulfuric acid: NaCl + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HCl
Mr(HCl) = 36.5; Mr(NaHSO4) = 23.0 + 1.0 + 32.1 + 64.0 = 120.1
Atom economy = 36.5 / (36.5 + 120.1) × 100 = 36.5 / 156.6 × 100 = 23.3%
Over three quarters of the mass handled leaves as sodium hydrogensulfate.

Route 3 — HBr from a bromide and phosphoric acid: NaBr + H3PO4 → NaH2PO4 + HBr
Mr(HBr) = 80.9; Mr(NaH2PO4) = 23.0 + 2.0 + 31.0 + 64.0 = 120.0
Atom economy = 80.9 / (80.9 + 120.0) × 100 = 80.9 / 200.9 × 100 = 40.3%

The comparison makes the industrial choice obvious in principle: burning hydrogen in chlorine wastes nothing, while the salt-and-acid route throws away three quarters of its mass as a by-product that must then be disposed of or sold. Route 3 nevertheless remains the laboratory method for hydrogen bromide, for the reason given earlier — sulfuric acid would oxidise the HBr to bromine, so a low atom economy is accepted in exchange for a clean product.

Recovering Chlorine: the Deacon Process

Many industrial processes that chlorinate an organic compound produce hydrogen chloride as an unwanted by-product, so half the chlorine bought ends up as waste acid. The Deacon process turns that waste back into the starting material:

4HCl(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2Cl2(g) + 2H2O(g)

Its atom economy with respect to chlorine is 2 × 71.0 / (2 × 71.0 + 2 × 18.0) × 100 = 142.0 / 178.0 × 100 = 79.8%, and the only by-product is water. That is a strikingly good figure for a substitution-type process, and it is the reason the reaction is still of interest despite being reversible and needing a catalyst.

Exam Tip: Check the equation is balanced before you start, and remember to multiply each Mr by its balancing number — leaving out the 2 in "2HCl" is the usual slip. If a question asks you to justify choosing one route over another, do not stop at the atom economy: a high atom economy is only one consideration alongside yield, reaction rate, energy cost, hazards, and whether the by-product can be sold rather than disposed of. The aspirin synthesis in WM is the classic case of a lower-atom-economy route being the better one overall.
