1. Aromatic Chemistry

Structure of Benzene

Benzene (C6H6): A planar, cyclic hydrocarbon with a ring of six carbon atoms. Each carbon is sp2 hybridised with bond angles of 120°. The unhybridised p orbital on each carbon overlaps sideways with those on adjacent carbons, forming a delocalised π system above and below the plane of the ring.

Kekulé model: Originally proposed as alternating single and double bonds (cyclohexatriene). However, this model is inconsistent with experimental evidence:

  • All C−C bond lengths in benzene are equal (0.139 nm), intermediate between a single bond (0.154 nm) and a double bond (0.134 nm).
  • Benzene does not undergo addition reactions typical of alkenes (e.g. it does not readily decolourise bromine water).
  • The enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene (−208 kJ mol−1) is less exothermic than predicted for three C=C bonds (3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol−1), showing benzene is more stable than the hypothetical cyclohexatriene by about 152 kJ mol−1 (the delocalisation/resonance energy).

Delocalised model: The six π electrons are spread evenly over all six carbon atoms in a ring of electron density. This delocalisation makes benzene more thermodynamically stable than the Kekulé structure and explains its tendency to undergo substitution rather than addition reactions (preserving the stable delocalised ring).

Electrophilic Substitution of Benzene

The delocalised π electron ring is a region of high electron density that attracts electrophiles. However, benzene undergoes substitution rather than addition to preserve the stability of the aromatic ring.

Nitration:

C6H6 + HNO3 ⟶ (conc. H2SO4, 50°C) C6H5NO2 + H2O

The electrophile is the nitronium ion (NO2+), generated by the reaction of concentrated nitric acid with concentrated sulfuric acid: HNO3 + H2SO4 → NO2+ + HSO4− + H2O. The mechanism involves the NO2+ being attracted to the π system, forming a positively charged intermediate (Wheland intermediate), followed by loss of H+ to restore aromaticity. Temperature must be kept below 55 °C to prevent dinitration.

Halogenation:

C6H6 + Br2 ⟶ (AlBr3 or FeBr3 catalyst) C6H5Br + HBr

A halogen carrier catalyst (Lewis acid such as AlBr3, FeBr3, or AlCl3) is needed to generate the electrophile. The catalyst polarises the Br−Br bond or forms Br+: Br2 + AlBr3 → Br+ + AlBr4−. The Br+ then attacks the π ring.

Friedel-Crafts alkylation:

C6H6 + RCl ⟶ (AlCl3) C6H5R + HCl

The AlCl3 catalyst generates a carbocation electrophile (R+) from the halogenoalkane. This introduces an alkyl group onto the benzene ring.

Friedel-Crafts acylation:

C6H6 + RCOCl ⟶ (AlCl3) C6H5COR + HCl

The electrophile is an acylium ion (RCO+). This introduces an acyl group (C=O bonded to R) onto the ring. Acylation is preferred over alkylation in synthesis because it does not give multiple substitutions (the C=O group deactivates the ring).

Exam Tip: In all electrophilic substitution mechanisms on benzene, draw two curly arrows: one from the π ring to the electrophile (forming the new bond), and one from the C−H bond to the ring (restoring aromaticity by losing H+). Show the intermediate Wheland complex with its positive charge delocalised over the ring.

2. Carbonyl Compounds

Aldehydes and Ketones

Both contain the C=O (carbonyl) group. Aldehydes have the carbonyl at the end of the chain (RCHO); ketones have it within the chain (RCOR'). The C=O bond is polar (δ+ on C, δ− on O) because oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, making the carbonyl carbon susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

Nucleophilic Addition

The key mechanism for carbonyl compounds. A nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbon of the C=O group:

With HCN (hydrogen cyanide): The nucleophile is CN−. It attacks the δ+ carbon, breaking the π bond and forming an alkoxide intermediate, which is then protonated by HCN to give a hydroxynitrile (cyanohydrin). This reaction extends the carbon chain by one carbon atom and introduces both an OH and CN group. A trace of base (NaCN or KCN) is used as a catalyst to generate CN−.

With NaBH4 (sodium borohydride): A reducing agent. The H− ion acts as the nucleophile, attacking the δ+ carbonyl carbon. Aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols; ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols. NaBH4 is used in aqueous/ethanolic solution.

Tollens' and Fehling's tests work because aldehydes can be oxidised to carboxylic acids, but ketones cannot be oxidised under these mild conditions. The 2,4-DNP test confirms the presence of a carbonyl group (aldehyde or ketone) but does not distinguish between them. The melting point of the 2,4-DNP derivative can be used to identify the specific aldehyde or ketone.

3. Colour, Dyes and Dyeing

Where Colour Comes From

A molecule is coloured because it absorbs some wavelengths of visible light and not others. Absorbing a photon promotes an electron from a filled to an empty energy level, and the photon absorbed is the one whose energy exactly matches the gap.

Chromophore: The part of a molecule responsible for absorbing light — in organic dyes, a system of delocalised electrons. Typical chromophores are conjugated chains of alternating double and single bonds, aromatic rings and the azo group, −N=N−.

The key relationship is between the extent of the delocalisation and the energy absorbed:

  • A short delocalised system has widely spaced energy levels, so a large energy gap, so it absorbs short-wavelength, high-energy radiation — in the ultraviolet. The compound looks colourless.
  • Extending the delocalisation brings the levels closer together, so the gap shrinks, so the absorption moves to longer wavelengths — into the visible. The compound is coloured.
  • Extending it further moves the absorption further towards the red end.

Benzene, with delocalisation over one ring, absorbs in the UV and is colourless. Beta-carotene, the pigment of carrots, has eleven conjugated C=C bonds; its delocalised system is long enough that it absorbs blue-violet light, and it looks orange.

Complementary colour: What we see is the light that is not absorbed. A substance that absorbs one colour appears the complementary colour.

Diazonium Compounds

Azo dyes — the largest single class of synthetic dye — are made in two steps, and both conditions matter.

Step 1: diazotisation. Phenylamine is treated with nitrous acid, made in situ from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid, at a temperature below 10 °C:

C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl → C6H5N2+Cl− + 2H2O

The product is benzenediazonium chloride. The low temperature is essential: above about 10 °C the diazonium ion decomposes, losing nitrogen gas and giving phenol instead, which ruins the preparation.

C6H5N2+ + H2O → C6H5OH + N2 + H+    (the unwanted reaction above 10 °C)

Step 2: coupling. The cold diazonium solution is added to an alkaline solution of a phenol or an aromatic amine. The diazonium ion acts as an electrophile and substitutes into the other ring, usually at the position opposite the −OH or −NH2 group:

C6H5N2+ + C6H5OH → C6H5N=NC6H4OH + H+

The product is an azo compound, and it is strongly coloured. The −N=N− group joins the two rings into one continuous delocalised system spanning the whole molecule, which is why an azo dye absorbs in the visible when neither starting material does. Alkaline conditions are used for the coupling because they convert the phenol to the phenoxide ion, which is more electron-rich and so reacts faster with the electrophile.

Different coupling partners give different colours: phenol gives yellow-orange dyes, naphthalen-2-ol gives reds, and aromatic amines give oranges and browns. Historically, Perkin's mauveine of 1856 was the first commercially successful synthetic dye, and picric acid and chrysoidine were among the early industrial products that made the chemistry of colour worth money.

Work through it systematically: find the extended delocalised system (the chromophore), then look for ionic or polar groups such as −SO3− (solubility, and ionic bonding to wool or nylon), and then for anything that could form a covalent bond to an −OH group of cellulose. You are not expected to recall dye structures — they are given — only to interpret them.

4. Fats and Oils

Fats and oils: Naturally occurring mixed esters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) with long-chain carboxylic acids (fatty acids). Because all three −OH groups of the glycerol are esterified, they are also called triglycerides or triesters.

A triglyceride is formed by three separate esterification (condensation) reactions, one at each −OH group, with a molecule of water lost each time:

glycerol + 3 RCOOH → triglyceride + 3H2O

Taking octadecanoic (stearic) acid, C17H35COOH, as the fatty acid:

C3H8O3 + 3C17H35COOH → C57H110O6 + 3H2O

Check it balances by mass: 92 + (3 × 284) = 944 on the left, and 890 + (3 × 18) = 944 on the right. "Mixed" ester means the three R groups in a natural fat are usually not all the same.

The whole difference between a fat and an oil is therefore two hydrogen atoms and a kink. Note that the argument is about shape and packing, not about the strength of the C=C bond itself — a very common confusion.

Reactions of Fats and Oils

Testing for unsaturation. The C=C bonds decolourise bromine water by electrophilic addition. The volume of bromine or iodine that a known mass of fat absorbs measures how unsaturated it is.

Hydrogenation. Passing hydrogen through a vegetable oil over a nickel catalyst at about 150–200 °C adds H2 across some of the C=C bonds. The chains become straighter, pack better, and the melting point rises, turning a liquid oil into a spreadable solid — this is how margarine is made.

−CH=CH− + H2 → −CH2−CH2−    (Ni catalyst)

Hydrolysis. Being esters, fats are hydrolysed back to glycerol and fatty acids. With hot aqueous acid the products are glycerol and the free carboxylic acids; with hot aqueous alkali, the process called saponification, the products are glycerol and the sodium salts of the fatty acids — soap.

triglyceride + 3NaOH → glycerol + 3RCOO−Na+

Soap cleans because each anion has a long non-polar hydrocarbon tail that mixes with grease and an ionic −COO− head that is attracted to water.

Worked Example — Saponification:

Calculate the mass of glycerol obtained by completely hydrolysing 89.0 g of tristearin, C57H110O6 (Mr = 890), with sodium hydroxide. (Mr glycerol C3H8O3 = 92.0)

n(tristearin) = 89.0 / 890 = 0.100 mol
Each triglyceride gives one glycerol, so n(glycerol) = 0.100 mol
Mass of glycerol = 0.100 × 92.0 = 9.20 g

The soap produced is 0.300 mol of sodium stearate, C17H35COO−Na+ (Mr = 306), a mass of 91.8 g. Check: 89.0 g of fat plus 0.300 × 40.0 = 12.0 g of NaOH gives 101.0 g of products, and 9.20 + 91.8 = 101.0 g. Consistent.

Analysing Fats by Gas–Liquid Chromatography

The food-testing context of this storyline needs a way of finding out which fatty acids a fat actually contains. The fat is hydrolysed, the fatty acids are converted to their volatile methyl esters, and the mixture is injected into a gas–liquid chromatograph. The sample is vaporised and carried by an inert gas through a column holding a high-boiling liquid on a porous support; each ester spends a different proportion of its time dissolved in the stationary phase, so each emerges at a different retention time. Comparing retention times with standards identifies each fatty acid, and peak areas give the proportions. Feeding the outflow into a mass spectrometer (GC–MS) identifies components that no standard is available for.

Exam Tip: If asked why an unsaturated oil has a lower melting point than a saturated fat, three things must appear for full marks: the cis C=C introduces a kink; the kinked chains cannot pack as closely; so the instantaneous dipole–induced dipole bonds between molecules are weaker and less energy is needed to separate them. Saying only "it has double bonds" earns nothing.
