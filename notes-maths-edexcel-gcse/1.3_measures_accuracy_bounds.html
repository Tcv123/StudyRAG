1.3.1 Standard Units and Compound Measures (N13)

Edexcel N13 asks you to use standard units of mass, length, time, money and other measures — including standard compound measures such as speed, density and pressure — using decimal quantities where appropriate. Metric conversion factors are not given on the paper, so you must know them. Conversions between metric and imperial units (for example 5 miles ≈ 8 km) are always given in the question.

MeasureConversions you must know
Length10 mm = 1 cm    100 cm = 1 m    1000 m = 1 km
Mass1000 mg = 1 g    1000 g = 1 kg    1000 kg = 1 tonne
Capacity1000 ml = 1 litre    100 cl = 1 litre    1 ml = 1 cm³    1000 cm³ = 1 litre
Time60 s = 1 min    60 min = 1 hour    24 h = 1 day    7 days = 1 week    365 days = 1 year (366 in a leap year)
Money100p = £1
Which way? Changing to a smaller unit gives a bigger number, so multiply. Changing to a larger unit gives a smaller number, so divide. A quick sense check: there are more centimetres than metres in any length.
Change 0.86 km into centimetres.

$0.86 \text{ km} = 0.86 \times 1000 = 860 \text{ m}$
$860 \text{ m} = 860 \times 100 = \mathbf{86\,000 \text{ cm}}$

Area and Volume Units

An area unit is a square, so the length factor is applied twice (squared). A volume unit is a cube, so the length factor is applied three times (cubed).

Area
$1 \text{ cm}^2 = 10^2 = 100 \text{ mm}^2$
$1 \text{ m}^2 = 100^2 = 10\,000 \text{ cm}^2$

Volume and capacity
$1 \text{ cm}^3 = 10^3 = 1000 \text{ mm}^3$
$1 \text{ m}^3 = 100^3 = 1\,000\,000 \text{ cm}^3$
$1 \text{ m}^3 = 1000 \text{ litres}$,   $1 \text{ litre} = 1000 \text{ cm}^3$
Change 3.7 m² into cm².

$3.7 \times 100 \times 100 = 3.7 \times 10\,000 = \mathbf{37\,000 \text{ cm}^2}$
A water butt holds 2.5 m³. How many litres is this? How many 750 cm³ jugs could be filled from 3 litres?

$2.5 \times 1000 = \mathbf{2500 \text{ litres}}$
$3 \text{ litres} = 3000 \text{ cm}^3$, and $3000 \div 750 = \mathbf{4 \text{ jugs}}$
Do not convert m² to cm² by multiplying by 100. Picture the 1 m by 1 m square: it is 100 cm by 100 cm, so it contains 10 000 cm². The same trap appears with m³ (factor 1 000 000, not 100).

Time and Compound Measures

Time is base 60, not base 10. 2.15 hours is not 2 hours 15 minutes: $0.15 \times 60 = 9$, so it is 2 hours 9 minutes. To turn minutes into a decimal of an hour, divide by 60.

$$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \qquad \text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} \qquad \text{pressure} = \frac{\text{force}}{\text{area}}$$ Units follow the formula: km ÷ hours gives km/h; g ÷ cm³ gives g/cm³; N ÷ m² gives N/m².
A lorry travels 162 km in 2 hours 15 minutes. Work out its average speed.

$15 \text{ min} = 15 \div 60 = 0.25 \text{ h}$, so the time is 2.25 h.
$\text{speed} = 162 \div 2.25 = \mathbf{72 \text{ km/h}}$
A metal block has mass 540 g and volume 200 cm³. Work out its density.

$\text{density} = 540 \div 200 = \mathbf{2.7 \text{ g/cm}^3}$

1.3.2 Rounding and Truncating (N15)

You must round numbers and measures to an appropriate degree of accuracy — to a given number of decimal places (d.p.) or significant figures (s.f.) — and decide on sensible accuracy in a context.

How to round: locate the last digit you will keep and look at the digit straight after it (the "decider").
  • Decider 5, 6, 7, 8 or 9 → the kept digit goes up by one
  • Decider 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 → the kept digit stays the same

Decimal Places

Count the digits after the decimal point. Keep trailing zeros that are part of the required accuracy (4.9973 to 2 d.p. is 5.00, not 5).

Significant Figures

Start counting at the first non-zero digit. Zeros in front of it are not significant; zeros between or after non-zero digits (in a decimal) are. When rounding a large number, use zeros as place holders so the number keeps its size.

Round 0.0040519 to 3 significant figures.

The significant figures are 4, 0, 5, then the decider is 1. That is less than 5, so the 5 stays.
Answer: 0.00405

Truncation

To truncate a number, chop it off at the required place and ignore everything after — no rounding up ever happens. 5.678 truncated to 1 d.p. is 5.6 (rounded to 1 d.p. it would be 5.7).

Sensible Accuracy in Context

  • Money is written to 2 d.p.: £8.5 should be written £8.50.
  • Let the context decide the direction: 70 people travelling in 16-seat minibuses need $70 \div 16 = 4.375 \Rightarrow$ 5 minibuses, but £70 buys only 4 tickets at £16 each.
  • Do not give an answer more accurately than the measurements it came from; matching the least accurate value used is a good rule.
On the calculator papers, keep the full value on your calculator throughout a multi-step question and round only at the very end. Rounding part-way can push the final answer outside the accepted range.

1.3.3 Estimating and Checking (N14)

N14 asks you to estimate answers and to check calculations using approximation and estimation, including answers obtained using technology. These questions usually appear on Paper 1 (non-calculator).

Method: round each number to 1 significant figure unless the question says otherwise, write the rounded values down, then work out the simpler calculation. The rounded values earn the method mark.
Work out an estimate for $\dfrac{7.83 \times 41.2}{0.19}$.

$7.83 \approx 8$, $\;41.2 \approx 40$, $\;0.19 \approx 0.2$
$\dfrac{8 \times 40}{0.2} = \dfrac{320}{0.2} = \mathbf{1600}$   (the exact value is about 1698)
Zara uses her calculator to work out $52.3 \times 0.187$ and gets 97.801. Use estimation to check her answer.

Estimate: $50 \times 0.2 = 10$. Her answer is about 10 times too big, so it must be wrong (the correct value is 9.7801).

Is the Estimate Too Big or Too Small?

  • Rounding a number you add or multiply up makes the estimate an overestimate.
  • Rounding a number you divide by up makes the estimate an underestimate (you divide by more).
  • If the roundings pull in different directions you cannot tell straight away — you need to compare with the exact answer.
Dividing by 0.2 is the same as multiplying by 5: $320 \div 0.2 = 1600$. If you get a smaller answer when dividing by a number less than 1, something has gone wrong.

Estimating with Powers and Roots

Work out an estimate for $\dfrac{\sqrt{80.6} \times 2.1^3}{0.52}$.

$\sqrt{80.6} \approx \sqrt{81} = 9$, $\;2.1^3 \approx 2^3 = 8$, $\;0.52 \approx 0.5$
$\dfrac{9 \times 8}{0.5} = \dfrac{72}{0.5} = \mathbf{144}$

1.3.4 Error Intervals (N15)

A rounded or truncated value hides the true value somewhere in a range. Edexcel asks you to write this range, the error interval, using inequality notation.

If a value has been rounded to a certain unit, the true value is within half a unit of it:
$$\text{value} - \tfrac{1}{2}\text{unit} \le x < \text{value} + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{unit}$$ Use $\le$ at the bottom (that value rounds up to the given value) and $<$ at the top (that value would round up to the next one).
The mass $w$ of a bag of sand is 23 kg, correct to the nearest kilogram. Write down the error interval for $w$.

Half of 1 kg is 0.5 kg: $\mathbf{22.5 \le w < 23.5}$
$n = 5800$, correct to 2 significant figures. Write down the error interval for $n$.

The second significant figure is the hundreds digit, so this is "to the nearest 100". Half of 100 is 50: $\mathbf{5750 \le n < 5850}$

Error Intervals after Truncation

A truncated value can only have lost digits, never gained them, so the true value runs from the stated value up to (but not including) the next value at that accuracy:
$x = 9.2$ truncated to 1 d.p. ⇒ $9.2 \le x < 9.3$
A number $n$ is 3.4 when rounded to 1 d.p., but 3.3 when truncated to 1 d.p. Find the error interval for $n$.

Rounding gives $3.35 \le n < 3.45$. Truncation gives $3.3 \le n < 3.4$.
Both must hold, so $\mathbf{3.35 \le n < 3.4}$
Write the upper limit as 23.5, not 23.49 or 23.4999… The strict "<" sign is what shows the limit itself is excluded.

1.3.5 Limits of Accuracy and Bounds (N16)

The lower bound (LB) and upper bound (UB) of a measurement are the ends of its error interval — the least and greatest values it could have had before rounding. Interpreting limits of accuracy for a single value is on both tiers.

Calculating with Upper and Lower Bounds Higher

The 1MA1 Higher statement of N16 adds "including upper and lower bounds": you combine bounds in calculations. Choose, for each value, the bound that pushes the answer as high as possible (for the UB) or as low as possible (for the LB).

CalculationUpper boundLower bound
$a + b$$\text{UB}_a + \text{UB}_b$$\text{LB}_a + \text{LB}_b$
$a - b$$\text{UB}_a - \text{LB}_b$$\text{LB}_a - \text{UB}_b$
$a \times b$$\text{UB}_a \times \text{UB}_b$$\text{LB}_a \times \text{LB}_b$
$a \div b$$\text{UB}_a \div \text{LB}_b$$\text{LB}_a \div \text{UB}_b$
Subtracting or dividing by a smaller number gives a larger answer, so the second value takes the opposite bound.
A rectangle measures 7.3 cm by 2.6 cm, both correct to 1 d.p. Work out the lower bound of its area.

$\text{LB} = 7.25 \times 2.55 = \mathbf{18.4875 \text{ cm}^2}$
$a = 15.4$ and $b = 6.8$, both correct to 1 d.p. Work out the upper bound of $a - b$.

$\text{UB}_a - \text{LB}_b = 15.45 - 6.75 = \mathbf{8.7}$
A cyclist rides 400 m, correct to the nearest 10 m, in 61.2 s, correct to 1 d.p. Work out the upper bound of the average speed.

$\text{UB of speed} = \dfrac{\text{UB distance}}{\text{LB time}} = \dfrac{405}{61.15} = 6.623\ldots \approx \mathbf{6.62 \text{ m/s}}$ (3 s.f.)

Choosing a Suitable Degree of Accuracy Higher

Find both bounds and round them. The answer is given to the greatest accuracy at which the two bounds round to the same value; state this as your reason.

$x = 8.7$ and $y = 3.1$, both correct to 1 d.p. $z = x \div y$. Work out $z$ to a suitable degree of accuracy.

$\text{UB} = 8.75 \div 3.05 = 2.868\ldots$    $\text{LB} = 8.65 \div 3.15 = 2.746\ldots$
To 2 s.f.: 2.9 and 2.7 — they differ. To 1 s.f.: 3 and 3 — they agree.
So $z = \mathbf{3}$ (1 s.f.), because both bounds round to 3 to 1 s.f.
A square has area 30 cm², correct to the nearest cm². Work out the lower bound of its side length.

$\text{LB of area} = 29.5$, so $\text{LB of side} = \sqrt{29.5} = 5.431\ldots \approx \mathbf{5.43 \text{ cm}}$
Write the bounds you are using before you calculate (e.g. "UB distance 405, LB time 61.15"). Each correct bound usually scores a B1 mark even if the final calculation goes wrong. Never round the bounds themselves.
