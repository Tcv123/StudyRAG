5.1 Probability

Pearson Edexcel GCSE Mathematics 1MA1 — Probability — spec refs P1–P9 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

5.1.1 The 0–1 Scale and Theoretical Probability (P3, P4, P7)

A probability is a number from $0$ (impossible) to $1$ (certain) that measures how likely an event is. Give probabilities as fractions, decimals or percentages. Answers written as a ratio (“3 : 10”) or in words (“3 out of 10”) are not accepted by Edexcel mark schemes.

Equally Likely Outcomes

If every outcome is equally likely (a fair dice, a random choice): $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in the event}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}}$$
A bag contains 4 red, 7 blue and 9 green counters. One counter is taken at random. Find $P(\text{blue})$ and $P(\text{not red})$.

Total $= 20$.   $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{7}{20}$.   $P(\text{not red}) = \dfrac{16}{20} = \dfrac{4}{5}$

Probabilities That Sum to 1 (P4)

The probabilities of an exhaustive set of mutually exclusive outcomes add up to $1$, so $$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$$
A biased spinner lands on red, blue, green or yellow. $P(\text{red}) = 0.25$, $P(\text{blue}) = 0.4$ and $P(\text{green}) = 0.15$. Find $P(\text{yellow})$.

$P(\text{yellow}) = 1 - (0.25 + 0.4 + 0.15) = 1 - 0.8 = \mathbf{0.2}$
A four-sided spinner has $P(A) = 2x$, $P(B) = x$, $P(C) = 0.35$ and $P(D) = x + 0.05$. Find $x$.

$2x + x + 0.35 + x + 0.05 = 1 \Rightarrow 4x + 0.4 = 1 \Rightarrow 4x = 0.6 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{0.15}$
Sense-check every answer: a probability must lie between 0 and 1. If you get $1.3$ or a negative value, look again. You do not need to simplify fractions unless asked, but an incorrect “simplification” can lose the accuracy mark.

5.1.2 Experiments, Relative Frequency and Expected Results (P1, P2, P3, P5)

Relative frequency $= \dfrac{\text{number of times the outcome happened}}{\text{total number of trials}}$. It is an estimate of the probability, found by experiment rather than theory.

By P5, as the number of trials grows, the relative frequency of an unbiased experiment settles towards the theoretical probability. That is why results from more trials give a more reliable estimate — and why a few odd results do not prove something is biased.

A coin is thrown 250 times and lands on heads 140 times. Estimate $P(\text{heads})$ and comment on whether the coin is fair.

Relative frequency $= \dfrac{140}{250} = 0.56$. This is higher than $0.5$, so the coin might be biased towards heads, but 250 throws cannot prove it. Throwing it many more times would give a more reliable estimate.

Fairness and Randomness (P2)

A fair (unbiased) dice, coin or spinner has all outcomes equally likely. Selecting “at random” means every item has the same chance. Even a fair dice rolled 30 times will rarely give exactly 5 of each score — randomness produces variation.

Expected Outcomes (P2, P3)

$$\text{Expected number of times} = P(\text{outcome}) \times \text{number of trials}$$
A game costs £1.50 to play. The probability of winning the £8 prize is $0.1$. The game is played 300 times. Work out the expected profit for the organiser.

Expected wins $= 0.1 \times 300 = 30$. Takings = 300 × £1.50 = £450. Prizes = 30 × £8 = £240.
Expected profit = £450 − £240 = £210

Frequency Trees (P1)

A frequency tree splits a group in stages and records frequencies (counts) on the branches, not probabilities. The numbers at each split must add up to the number before it.

From the tree: $P(\text{a woman who wears glasses}) = \dfrac{40}{150} = \dfrac{4}{15}$, and $35 + 50 = 85$ people do not wear glasses.

Two fair dice are rolled and the scores are multiplied. Find the probability that the product is odd.

5.1.4 Venn Diagrams and Sets (P6)

A Venn diagram sorts items into overlapping sets inside a rectangle that holds everything (the universal set $\xi$). Start with the overlap, then subtract to fill each “only” region, and finally the outside.

40 students: 18 cycle, 22 swim and 7 do both.

Overlap $= 7$. Cycle only $= 18 - 7 = 11$. Swim only $= 22 - 7 = 15$.
Neither $= 40 - (11 + 7 + 15) = 7$.
$P(\text{swims but does not cycle}) = \dfrac{15}{40} = \dfrac{3}{8}$

5.1.5 Combined Events: Adding and Multiplying (P4, P8)

Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together. Independent events do not influence each other: knowing one happened does not change the probability of the other.
OR (mutually exclusive events): $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$

AND (independent events): $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$

At least one: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$
The probability of rain on Saturday is $0.4$ and on Sunday is $0.3$. Assume the days are independent. Find the probability that (a) it rains on both days (b) it rains on neither day.

(a) $0.4 \times 0.3 = \mathbf{0.12}$    (b) $0.6 \times 0.7 = \mathbf{0.42}$
Three fair coins are thrown. Find $P(\text{at least one head})$.

$P(\text{no heads}) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{8}$, so $P(\text{at least one head}) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{8} = \mathbf{\tfrac{7}{8}}$

5.1.6 Tree Diagrams, Independent and Dependent Events (P6, P8)

A tree diagram shows events in order. Multiply along a path to get the probability of that combined outcome; add the results of different paths for “or”. The branches leaving any point must add to $1$, and all end results together add to $1$.

A spinner lands on green (G) with probability $0.3$ and is spun twice. $P(\text{exactly one green}) = 0.21 + 0.21 = 0.42$. Check: $0.09 + 0.21 + 0.21 + 0.49 = 1$ ✓

Without Replacement (Dependent Events)

When an item is replaced, the second-stage probabilities are the same as the first (independent events). When it is not replaced, the events are dependent: the total drops by 1, and so does the number of the type already taken.

A bag holds 4 red and 6 blue counters. Two are taken at random without replacement. Find $P(\text{one of each colour})$.

$P(R, B) = \dfrac{4}{10} \times \dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac{24}{90}$    $P(B, R) = \dfrac{6}{10} \times \dfrac{4}{9} = \dfrac{24}{90}$
$P(\text{one of each}) = \dfrac{48}{90} = \mathbf{\dfrac{8}{15}}$
Higher A bag contains $n$ sweets, 3 of which are orange. Two are taken without replacement and $P(\text{both orange}) = \dfrac{1}{12}$. Find $n$.

$\dfrac{3}{n} \times \dfrac{2}{n-1} = \dfrac{1}{12} \Rightarrow n(n-1) = 72 \Rightarrow n^2 - n - 72 = 0$
$(n - 9)(n + 8) = 0$, so $n = \mathbf{9}$ (reject $-8$). This needs Higher-tier quadratic solving (A18).
P8 asks you to “know the underlying assumptions”: multiplying probabilities assumes independence, and using a relative frequency assumes the experiment is a fair estimate. Look for “without replacement”, “takes two” or “eats” — then the second denominator must go down by 1.

5.1.7 Conditional Probability (P9) Higher

A conditional probability is the probability of $A$ given that $B$ has happened, written $P(A \mid B)$. Knowing that $B$ happened shrinks the set of possible outcomes to those in $B$. $$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

The 1MA1 spec asks you to work with conditional probability through expected frequencies in two-way tables, tree diagrams and Venn diagrams.

From a Two-Way Table

A student who travels by bus is chosen at random. Find the probability that they are in Year 11.

Use only the “Bus” column: 51 students, 33 of them in Year 11.
$P(\text{Year 11} \mid \text{bus}) = \dfrac{33}{51} = \mathbf{\dfrac{11}{17}}$   (compare $P(\text{Year 11}) = \dfrac{55}{100}$)

From a Tree Diagram

$P(\text{snow}) = 0.2$. If it snows, $P(\text{school closes}) = 0.6$; if it does not snow, $P(\text{school closes}) = 0.05$. Given that the school is closed, find the probability that it snowed.

$P(\text{closed}) = 0.2 \times 0.6 + 0.8 \times 0.05 = 0.12 + 0.04 = 0.16$
$P(\text{snow} \mid \text{closed}) = \dfrac{0.12}{0.16} = \mathbf{\dfrac{3}{4}}$

From a Venn Diagram

$P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.5$ and $P(A \cup B) = 0.7$. Find $P(A \cap B)$, $P((A \cup B)')$ and $P(A \mid B)$.

$P(A \cap B) = 0.4 + 0.5 - 0.7 = \mathbf{0.2}$,   $P((A \cup B)') = 1 - 0.7 = \mathbf{0.3}$
$P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.2}{0.5} = \mathbf{0.4}$   (equal to $P(A)$, so here $A$ and $B$ are independent)

Expected Frequencies

Turning probabilities into counts out of a convenient total makes conditional questions much easier. In the snow example, out of 100 days: 20 snowy days give $12$ closures and 80 other days give $4$ closures, so $12$ of the $16$ closures were on snowy days — $\tfrac{12}{16} = \tfrac{3}{4}$ again.

Spot the trigger words: “given that”, “of those who”, “a student who … is chosen”. The denominator becomes the size of that given group, not the grand total. If $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, the events are independent.
