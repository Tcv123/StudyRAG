6.2 Averages, Spread & Grouped Data

Pearson Edexcel GCSE Mathematics 1MA1 — Statistics — spec refs S3, S4 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

In 1MA1, S4 is on both tiers, but the Higher version adds box plots, quartiles and the inter-quartile range. S3 (histograms with equal and unequal class widths, and cumulative frequency graphs) appears only in the Higher content list. Sections marked Higher are Higher tier only.

6.2.1 Types of Data (S4)

Discrete data — can only take certain values, usually found by counting (number of texts sent, shoe size).
Continuous data — can take any value within a range, found by measuring (mass, time, length).
Grouped data — values sorted into classes such as $10 < t \leq 20$, used when there are many different values.

Inequalities matter: in $10 < t \leq 20$, a time of exactly 10 belongs to the class below, and exactly 20 belongs in this class.

6.2.2 Mean, Median, Mode and Range (S4)

$$\text{Mean} = \frac{\text{total of the values}}{\text{number of values}} \qquad \text{Median} = \text{the }\left(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\right)\text{th value once the data is in order}$$ Mode = the most common value.   Range = largest value $-$ smallest value (a measure of spread).
Data: 12, 7, 15, 9, 7, 18, 10, 14.

Mean $= \dfrac{92}{8} = 11.5$
In order: 7, 7, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 18. With $n = 8$ the median is the $4.5$th value, halfway between 10 and 12, so median $= 11$.
Mode $= 7$.   Range $= 18 - 7 = 11$.

Working backwards from a mean

Use $\text{total} = \text{mean} \times \text{number of values}$.

The mean of five numbers is 16. A sixth number is added and the mean becomes 17. Work out the sixth number.

Old total $= 5 \times 16 = 80$. New total $= 6 \times 17 = 102$. Sixth number $= 102 - 80 = 22$.
14 boys did a mean of 6.5 hours of homework; 16 girls did a mean of 8 hours. Work out the mean for all 30 pupils.

$\dfrac{14 \times 6.5 + 16 \times 8}{30} = \dfrac{91 + 128}{30} = \dfrac{219}{30} = 7.3$ hours.
(Averaging the two means gives 7.25, which is wrong because the groups are different sizes.)

Outliers and choosing an average

An outlier is a value that is much bigger or smaller than the rest. It may be a mistake or a genuine extreme value. Outliers have a big effect on the mean and the range, but very little effect on the median (or the IQR).
AverageBest when…Weakness
MeanNumerical data without outliers; uses every valuePulled towards outliers
MedianData has outliers or is skewed (rents, salaries, house prices)Ignores most of the values

Monthly rents on a street: £650, £700, £720, £740, £780, £2400.

Mean $= 5990 \div 6 = 998.33$, i.e. £998.33.   Median $= \dfrac{720 + 740}{2} = 730$, i.e. £730.
The median is more typical: five of the six rents are below the mean, which has been dragged up by the outlier £2400.

6.2.3 Averages from a Frequency Table (S4)

$$\text{Mean} = \frac{\sum fx}{\sum f} = \frac{\text{total of (value} \times \text{frequency)}}{\text{total frequency}}$$
Goals scored by a team in 25 matches:

Mean $= 35 \div 25 = 1.4$ goals.   Mode $= 1$ goal.
Median $=$ 13th value; the running total passes 13 in the "1" row (values 7 to 14), so median $= 1$ goal.   Range $= 4 - 0 = 4$.
Divide by the total frequency (25), not the number of rows (5). The mode is the value with the highest frequency (1 goal), not the frequency itself (8).

6.2.4 Grouped Frequency Tables and Frequency Polygons (S4)

With grouped data the individual values are lost, so:
  • the mean can only be estimated, using the midpoint of each class;
  • we give the modal class (highest frequency) rather than a mode;
  • we find the class that contains the median using running totals.
Times taken by 50 people to solve a puzzle:

Time $t$ (min)$f$Midpoint $x$$fx$Running total
$0 < t \leq 4$72147
$4 < t \leq 8$1669623
$8 < t \leq 12$181018041
$12 < t \leq 16$91412650
Total50416
Estimated mean $= 416 \div 50 = 8.32$ minutes.
Modal class: $8 < t \leq 12$.   The median is the $25.5$th value; running totals 23 then 41, so it lies in $8 < t \leq 12$.
Sense-check: an estimated mean must lie between the smallest and largest possible values (0 and 16 here). Getting 104 means you divided by 4 classes instead of 50 people.

Frequency polygons

Plot each class frequency above the class midpoint and join the points with straight lines (do not join the first and last points to the axis unless told to). Two frequency polygons on the same axes are a quick way to compare distributions.

6.2.5 Comparing Distributions (S4)

A full comparison has two parts, each using numbers and written in context:
  1. Average (mean or median): which group is higher or lower on average?
  2. Spread (range, or IQR on Higher): which group is more consistent? A smaller spread means more consistent.
Morning group's quiz scores: 14, 18, 15, 20, 17, 16, 19. Afternoon group: median 15, range 11.

Morning: in order 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, so median $= 17$ and range $= 20 - 14 = 6$.
The morning group had the higher median (17 vs 15), so they scored better on average.
The morning group had the smaller range (6 vs 11), so their scores were more consistent.
Write comparisons like "Group A's median is higher, so on average Group A ..." and "Group B's range is smaller, so Group B's ... were more consistent." Be careful with context: for race times, a lower average means faster.

6.2.6 Quartiles and Inter-Quartile Range (S4) Higher

For ordered data with $n$ values:
  • Lower quartile (LQ) — the $\left(\tfrac{n+1}{4}\right)$th value; a quarter of the data is below it.
  • Median — the $\left(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\right)$th value.
  • Upper quartile (UQ) — the $\left(\tfrac{3(n+1)}{4}\right)$th value; three quarters of the data is below it.
$$\text{IQR} = \text{UQ} - \text{LQ}$$ The IQR is the spread of the middle half of the data, so extreme values do not affect it.
Reaction times (hundredths of a second) of 11 students: 13, 15, 18, 20, 21, 24, 26, 27, 30, 33, 36.

$n + 1 = 12$: LQ $=$ 3rd value $= 18$; median $=$ 6th value $= 24$; UQ $=$ 9th value $= 30$.
IQR $= 30 - 18 = 12$.   Range $= 36 - 13 = 23$.
Testing for outliers. A common rule: a value is an outlier if it is more than $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ above the UQ or below the LQ.
Data: 2, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 16, 31.   LQ $= 6$, UQ $= 14$, IQR $= 8$, and $1.5 \times 8 = 12$.
Upper limit $= 14 + 12 = 26$; lower limit $= 6 - 12 = -6$. Since $31 > 26$, 31 is an outlier; no value is below $-6$.

6.2.7 Cumulative Frequency Graphs (S3) Higher

Cumulative frequency is a running total of the frequencies. Plot each cumulative frequency at the upper class boundary, start at the lowest boundary with cumulative frequency 0, and join the points with a smooth curve or straight lines.

With $n$ values: median at $\tfrac{n}{2}$, LQ at $\tfrac{n}{4}$, UQ at $\tfrac{3n}{4}$ on the cumulative frequency axis.
Heights of 100 seedlings:

Height $h$ (cm)FrequencyCumulative frequencyPoint to plot
$0 < h \leq 5$88$(5, 8)$
$5 < h \leq 10$2230$(10, 30)$
$10 < h \leq 15$3565$(15, 65)$
$15 < h \leq 20$2590$(20, 90)$
$20 < h \leq 25$10100$(25, 100)$
Start the graph at $(0, 0)$.

Reading the graph: LQ $\approx 8.9$ cm, median $\approx 12.9$ cm, UQ $\approx 17$ cm, so IQR $\approx 17 - 8.9 = 8.1$ cm. (A curve drawn by hand gives slightly different readings; mark schemes accept a range.)

"How many are more than…?" Estimate how many seedlings are taller than 18 cm.

Read up from 18 cm: cumulative frequency $\approx 80$. So about $100 - 80 = 20$ seedlings are taller than 18 cm.
Plot at the upper boundary, never the midpoint. Leave your reading lines on the graph — they earn method marks. For "more than" questions, subtract your reading from the total.

6.2.8 Box Plots (S4) Higher

A box plot is drawn on a scale and shows five values: minimum, LQ, median, UQ, maximum. The box goes from LQ to UQ (its length is the IQR) with a line at the median; whiskers reach out to the minimum and maximum.
Group A (from 6.2.6): min 13, LQ 18, median 24, UQ 30, max 36.   Group B: min 10, LQ 20, median 28, UQ 31, max 45.

IQR: A $= 30 - 18 = 12$; B $= 31 - 20 = 11$.
Average: A's median (24) is lower than B's (28), so Group A generally reacted faster.
Spread: B's IQR (11) is slightly smaller than A's (12), so the middle half of Group B was slightly more consistent, even though B's range (35) is much larger than A's (23).
A box plot tells you proportions: 25% of values are below the LQ, 50% lie inside the box, 75% are below the UQ. It does not show the mean or how many values there are. When comparing, use the median and IQR rather than the range, since the range depends on just two (possibly extreme) values.

6.2.9 Histograms (S3) Higher

A histogram shows grouped continuous data on a continuous horizontal scale, with no gaps between bars. The area of each bar represents its frequency, so the vertical axis is frequency density. When all classes have the same width, bar heights are in proportion to the frequencies; with unequal widths you must use frequency density.
$$\text{Frequency density} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}} \qquad \text{Frequency} = \text{frequency density} \times \text{class width}$$
Masses of 65 letters:

Mass $m$ (g)FrequencyClass widthFrequency density
$0 < m \leq 20$1220$12 \div 20 = 0.6$
$20 < m \leq 30$1810$18 \div 10 = 1.8$
$30 < m \leq 35$145$14 \div 5 = 2.8$
$35 < m \leq 40$115$11 \div 5 = 2.2$
$40 < m \leq 60$1020$10 \div 20 = 0.5$
Part of a class. Estimate how many letters have a mass between 25 g and 50 g.

25 to 30: $5 \times 1.8 = 9$;   30 to 40: $14 + 11 = 25$;   40 to 50: $10 \times 0.5 = 5$.
Estimate $= 9 + 25 + 5 = 39$ letters (assuming the values are spread evenly within each class).
Finding the total. A histogram has bars $0$–$10$ with FD 1.4, $10$–$25$ with FD 2 and $25$–$45$ with FD 0.65.

Frequencies: $10 \times 1.4 = 14$, $15 \times 2 = 30$, $20 \times 0.65 = 13$. Total $= 57$.
If the frequency density axis has no numbers, use a class whose frequency you know to find the scale (frequency $\div$ width $=$ FD for that bar's height). Label the axis "Frequency density", never "Frequency".
