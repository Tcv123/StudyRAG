2.6.1 Generating Sequences (A23)

A sequence is a list of numbers (terms) in a particular order, following a rule. The position of a term is written $n$: the first term is at $n = 1$, the second at $n = 2$, and so on.

Term-to-term rules

A term-to-term rule says how to get the next term from the one before. It only works if you also know a starting term.

The first term of a sequence is 4. The term-to-term rule is "multiply by 2 then add 3". Write down the next three terms.

$4 \times 2 + 3 = 11$,   $11 \times 2 + 3 = 25$,   $25 \times 2 + 3 = 53$
Sequence: 4, $\mathbf{11, 25, 53}$

You may see a term-to-term rule written in suffix notation, where $u_n$ is the $n$th term and $u_{n+1}$ is the next one. For example $u_{n+1} = 3u_n - 2$ with $u_1 = 2$ gives 2, 4, 10, 28, … The question will always explain the notation it uses.

Position-to-term rules (the $n$th term)

A position-to-term rule, or $n$th term, lets you find any term directly by substituting its position for $n$ — no need to list all the terms before it.

The $n$th term of a sequence is $6n - 1$. Write down the first three terms and work out the 30th term.

$n = 1$: $6 - 1 = 5$;   $n = 2$: $12 - 1 = 11$;   $n = 3$: $18 - 1 = 17$
$n = 30$: $6 \times 30 - 1 = \mathbf{179}$
Write down the first three terms of the sequence with $n$th term $n^2 - 2$.

$1 - 2 = -1$,   $4 - 2 = 2$,   $9 - 2 = 7$   →   $\mathbf{-1, 2, 7}$

Sequences from patterns and diagrams

Each extra triangle shares one stick with the previous one, so it needs only 2 more sticks. The numbers of sticks are 3, 5, 7, … with $n$th term $2n + 1$, so pattern 50 needs $2 \times 50 + 1 = 101$ sticks.

Triangular numbers

Each new triangle of dots adds a row that is one dot longer than the last, so the gaps between triangular numbers are 2, 3, 4, 5, … Using the formula, the 15th triangular number is $\dfrac{15 \times 16}{2} = 120$.

Arithmetic progressions

In an arithmetic (linear) sequence the same number is added each time. This is the common difference $d$, which can be negative: 25, 21, 17, 13, … has $d = -4$.

Fibonacci-type sequences

In a Fibonacci-type sequence each term (after the first two) is the sum of the two terms before it. The Fibonacci sequence is 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, … but the rule can start from any two numbers.

With first two terms $a$ and $b$, the terms are $a,\ b,\ a + b,\ a + 2b,\ 2a + 3b,\ 3a + 5b, \ldots$ Writing terms like this turns "find the first two terms" questions into simultaneous equations.

The 3rd term of a Fibonacci-type sequence is 9 and the 5th term is 23. Work out the first two terms.

3rd term: $a + b = 9$    5th term: $2a + 3b = 23$
Double the first: $2a + 2b = 18$. Subtract from the second: $b = 5$, so $a = 4$.
Sequence: $\mathbf{4, 5}$, 9, 14, 23 ✓

Geometric progressions

In a geometric sequence each term is multiplied by the same number, the common ratio $r$. Find $r$ by dividing any term by the term before it. On the Foundation tier $r$ is a positive rational number, such as 4, $\tfrac{1}{3}$ or 1.5.
Geometric sequence with first term $a$ and common ratio $r$: $$a,\ ar,\ ar^2,\ ar^3, \ldots \qquad n\text{th term} = ar^{n-1}$$
A geometric sequence has first term 3 and a positive common ratio. The 3rd term is 48. Work out the 5th term.

$3r^2 = 48 \Rightarrow r^2 = 16 \Rightarrow r = 4$
Terms: 3, 12, 48, 192, $\mathbf{768}$
A geometric sequence begins 81, 27, … Write down the next two terms.

$r = 27 \div 81 = \tfrac{1}{3}$, so the next terms are $27 \times \tfrac13 = \mathbf{9}$ and $\mathbf{3}$.

Quadratic sequences

In a quadratic sequence the first differences are not constant, but the second differences (the differences between the differences) are. Recognising and continuing quadratic sequences is on both tiers.

The second difference is always 2, so 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, … is a quadratic sequence. The next first differences are +12 and +14, giving the next terms 42 and 56.

Identify the type first: same first difference ⇒ arithmetic; same second difference ⇒ quadratic; same ratio ⇒ geometric; each term is the sum of the two before ⇒ Fibonacci-type.

Surd ratios and other sequences Higher

On the Higher tier the common ratio of a geometric sequence can be a surd, and you can be asked about "other sequences" such as ones with a negative ratio, or rules defined in the question.

A geometric sequence begins $5,\ 5\sqrt{3},\ 15, \ldots$ Work out the 7th term.

$r = \dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{5} = \sqrt{3}$
7th term $= 5 \times (\sqrt{3})^6 = 5 \times 3^3 = 5 \times 27 = \mathbf{135}$
A geometric sequence has first term 4 and second term $4\sqrt{2}$. Find the first term that is greater than 1000.

$r = \sqrt{2}$, so every two terms the value doubles: 4, $4\sqrt{2}$, 8, $8\sqrt{2}$, 16, …
The odd-numbered terms are powers of 2 times 4: 17th term $= 4 \times 2^8 = 1024$.
16th term $= 4 \times 2^7 \times \sqrt{2} = 512\sqrt{2} \approx 724.1$, which is less than 1000. So the answer is the $\mathbf{17th}$ term (1024).

A negative common ratio makes the terms alternate in sign: 3, $-6$, 12, $-24$, 48, … has $r = -2$.

2.6.3 The $n$th Term of a Linear Sequence (A25)

For a linear sequence with common difference $d$: $$n\text{th term} = dn + (\text{first term} - d)$$ "First term $- d$" is the zeroth term: the number that would come just before the first term.
Find the $n$th term of 5, 12, 19, 26, …

The common difference is 7, so compare with $7n$: 7, 14, 21, 28, …
Each term is 2 less than $7n$, so the $n$th term is $\mathbf{7n - 2}$.
Find the $n$th term of 50, 44, 38, 32, … and work out the 20th term.

$d = -6$; zeroth term $= 50 + 6 = 56$. $n$th term $= \mathbf{56 - 6n}$
20th term $= 56 - 120 = \mathbf{-64}$

Is a number in the sequence?

Set the $n$th term equal to the number and solve for $n$. The number is a term only if $n$ is a positive integer.

Is 131 a term of $9n - 4$?   $9n - 4 = 131 \Rightarrow 9n = 135 \Rightarrow n = 15$. Yes, it is the 15th term.
Is 90 a term of $5n + 2$?   $5n = 88 \Rightarrow n = 17.6$. This is not a whole number, so no.

Problems with linear sequences

Sequence A has $n$th term $6n + 1$. Sequence B begins 79, 76, 73, … Find the position at which the two sequences have the same term.

B: $d = -3$, zeroth term 82, so $n$th term $= 82 - 3n$.
$6n + 1 = 82 - 3n \Rightarrow 9n = 81 \Rightarrow n = 9$. Both 9th terms are $\mathbf{55}$.
1 table seats 5 people, 2 tables pushed together seat 8, and 3 tables seat 11. What is the least number of tables needed to seat 60 people?

$n$th term $3n + 2$. Need $3n + 2 \ge 60 \Rightarrow n \ge 19.33\ldots$, so $\mathbf{20}$ tables (19 tables seat only 59).
The number in front of $n$ is the common difference, not the first term — 4, 7, 10, 13 is $3n + 1$, not "$n + 3$". Check your expression by substituting $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ before moving on.

2.6.4 The $n$th Term of a Quadratic Sequence Higher (A25)

For a quadratic sequence with $n$th term $an^2 + bn + c$: $$a = \frac{\text{second difference}}{2}$$
  1. Work out the second difference and halve it to get $a$.
  2. Subtract $an^2$ from each term of the sequence.
  3. Find the $n$th term of what is left (a linear sequence $bn + c$).
  4. Combine to give $an^2 + bn + c$, and check with $n = 1$ and $n = 2$.
Find the $n$th term of 4, 11, 22, 37, 56, …

Find the $n$th term of 10, 15, 18, 19, 18, …

First differences: 5, 3, 1, $-1$   Second difference: $-2$, so $a = -1$.
Term $- (-n^2)$, i.e. term $+ n^2$: 11, 19, 27, 35, 43 ⇒ $8n + 3$
$n$th term $= \mathbf{-n^2 + 8n + 3}$. Check: $n = 2$: $-4 + 16 + 3 = 15$ ✓
The $n$th term of a sequence is $n^2 + 3n$. Show that 130 is a term of the sequence.

$n^2 + 3n = 130 \Rightarrow n^2 + 3n - 130 = 0 \Rightarrow (n + 13)(n - 10) = 0$
$n = 10$ (reject $n = -13$, since positions are positive). 130 is the $\mathbf{10th}$ term.
The coefficient of $n^2$ is half the second difference — using the full second difference is the most common error. A negative second difference gives a negative $n^2$ term.
