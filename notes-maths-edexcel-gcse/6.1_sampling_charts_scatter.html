6.1 Sampling, Charts & Scatter Graphs

Pearson Edexcel GCSE Mathematics 1MA1 — Statistics — spec refs S1, S2, S5, S6 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

In 1MA1, statements S1, S2, S5 and S6 appear word for word in both the Foundation and the Higher content lists, so everything on this page is assessed on both tiers — there is no Higher-only material here. The topic covers collecting data from samples, using a sample to describe a population, displaying data in tables and charts, and analysing pairs of variables with scatter graphs.

6.1.1 Populations and Samples (S1)

Population — every person or item you want information about (e.g. all the customers of a shop, every egg laid on a farm this week).

Sample — the part of the population that is actually asked, measured or tested.

Census — a survey that includes every member of the population.

Why take a sample?

  • It saves time and money.
  • The population may be very large, or it may not be possible to contact everyone.
  • Some tests destroy the item (e.g. checking how long a battery lasts, or the breaking strength of a rope).

Limitations of sampling

  • A sample only gives an estimate; a second sample would probably give a slightly different answer.
  • A small sample is less likely to represent the population.
  • A biased sample over- or under-represents some groups, so conclusions may be wrong however large it is.
Random sample — every member of the population has the same chance of being chosen.

Method: make a list of the whole population (the sampling frame) and number it, e.g. 1 to 1200. Use a calculator's random number key or a computer to generate numbers, and select those members, ignoring any repeats, until you have the sample size you need.

Biased sample — a sample that does not fairly represent the population.

Spotting bias

How the sample was chosenWhy it is biased
Asking people in a library how often they readLibrary users probably read more than average
Asking the first 15 people to arrive at a gym on MondayOnly one day and time; early Monday users may be different from others
A phone-in or website pollOnly people who choose to respond are included, often those with strong views
Asking only your own class or friendsOne age group; not chosen at random
Checking only the last batch made each dayMachines or workers may perform differently at other times
"It is biased" earns nothing on its own. Explain who is over- or under-represented, in the context of the question, then suggest an improvement: choose randomly from a list of the whole population, use a larger sample, or include all groups/times/places.

6.1.2 Using a Sample to Describe a Population (S1, S5)

If a sample is random and reasonably large, the proportion with a property in the sample is used as an estimate of the proportion in the whole population.

$$\text{Estimate for the population} = \frac{\text{number in the sample with the property}}{\text{sample size}} \times \text{population size}$$
A school has 1350 pupils. In a random sample of 60 pupils, 16 buy a school lunch every day. Work out an estimate for the number of pupils in the school who buy a school lunch every day.

$\dfrac{16}{60} \times 1350 = \dfrac{21\,600}{60} = 360$ pupils.

Assumption: the sample is representative of all pupils in the school.
A farm packs 7200 eggs a week. A random sample of 150 eggs contains 4 cracked eggs. Estimate the number of cracked eggs packed in a week.

$\dfrac{4}{150} \times 7200 = 4 \times 48 = 192$ cracked eggs.

With only 4 cracked eggs in the sample, the estimate could easily be out by quite a lot — a larger sample would make it more reliable.

Categorical data — non-numerical categories (e.g. type of phone, eye colour).
Discrete numerical data — numbers that can only take particular values, usually counted (e.g. number of siblings).

6.1.4 Bar Charts and Vertical Line Charts (S2)

Bar chart — bars of equal width separated by equal gaps; bar heights show frequencies. Used for categorical data and ungrouped discrete data.

Dual bar chart — bars for two groups side by side in each category, with a key.
Composite bar chart — one bar per group, split into sections that add up to the group total.
Vertical line chart — thin vertical lines instead of bars; used for ungrouped discrete numerical data.
Using the dual bar chart: Year 10 — hot meal 12, sandwich 18, salad 6; Year 11 — hot meal 16, sandwich 10, salad 8.

(a) How many students were surveyed? $\;(12 + 18 + 6) + (16 + 10 + 8) = 36 + 34 = 70$.
(b) What fraction of Year 11 chose a hot meal? $\;\dfrac{16}{34} = \dfrac{8}{17}$.
(c) Compare the two years: Year 10's most popular choice was a sandwich; Year 11's was a hot meal.
When drawing a chart: label both axes, use an even scale that starts at 0, keep bars the same width, and leave equal gaps between bars for categories. Add a key to any dual or composite bar chart.

6.1.5 Pictograms (S2)

A pictogram uses a symbol to stand for a fixed number of items. It must have a key. A fraction of a symbol stands for the same fraction of that number.
Key: one cup symbol represents 8 cups of coffee sold.

  • Wednesday shows $3\tfrac{3}{4}$ symbols: $3.75 \times 8 = 30$ cups.
  • Thursday sold 20 cups: $20 \div 8 = 2.5$, so draw 2 whole cups and half a cup.
  • Friday sold 14 cups: $14 \div 8 = 1.75$, so draw 1 whole cup and three-quarters of a cup.
Work out what one symbol represents before reading anything else; if the key is missing, use a row where the frequency is given (e.g. 3 symbols $= 24$, so 1 symbol $= 8$).

6.1.6 Pie Charts (S2)

A pie chart shows how a total is shared between categories. Each sector's angle is proportional to its frequency, and the angles add up to $360^\circ$. Pie charts show proportions; they do not show the actual numbers unless the total is known.
$$\text{Angle} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}} \times 360^\circ \qquad \text{Frequency} = \frac{\text{angle}}{360^\circ} \times \text{total}$$
40 people were asked for their favourite drink: tea 15, coffee 11, juice 8, water 6.

$360 \div 40 = 9^\circ$ per person.

Reading a sector. A pie chart shows 200 people. The "cycle" sector is $81^\circ$.
$\dfrac{81}{360} \times 200 = 45$ people.

Finding the total. A $54^\circ$ sector represents 21 people. Then $1^\circ$ represents $21 \div 54 = \tfrac{7}{18}$ of a person, so the total is $\tfrac{7}{18} \times 360 = 140$ people. (Or: $360 \div 54 = \tfrac{20}{3}$, and $21 \times \tfrac{20}{3} = 140$.)
Comparing pie charts. School X (300 students) has a $72^\circ$ sector for "drama club". School Y (720 students) has a $40^\circ$ sector.

X: $\dfrac{72}{360} \times 300 = 60$ students.   Y: $\dfrac{40}{360} \times 720 = 80$ students.

School Y has more students in drama club even though its sector is smaller — it is a smaller proportion of a much larger total.
On Paper 1 (non-calculator) start with "degrees per person" $= 360 \div \text{total}$. Check the angles add to $360^\circ$, draw them with a protractor to within $2^\circ$, and label each sector.

6.1.7 Time Series Graphs (S2)

A time series graph plots data collected at regular time intervals, with time on the horizontal axis. Consecutive points are joined with straight lines.

Trend — the overall direction over time (increasing, decreasing or roughly constant).
Seasonal variation — a pattern that repeats in every cycle (every year, week, day…).
Takings (£ thousands): 2024 — 22, 30, 38, 26; 2025 — 24, 33, 41, 28.

Seasonal variation: takings rise to a peak in Q3 (summer) and are lowest in Q1 every year.
Trend: every quarter of 2025 is higher than the same quarter of 2024, and the yearly total rose from $22+30+38+26 = 116$ to $24+33+41+28 = 126$ thousand pounds — an increasing trend.
Percentage increase in Q3: $\dfrac{41 - 38}{38} \times 100 = 7.9\%$ (1 d.p.).
Comparing Q3 2024 (£38k) with Q1 2025 (£24k) and saying "takings are falling" is wrong — that drop is seasonal. Judge the trend by comparing the same season in different years, or yearly totals.

You may be asked why a chart is misleading. Look for: a vertical axis that does not start at 0 (so small differences look huge), an uneven scale, bars or pictures of different widths, 3D effects, missing labels or keys, and pie charts with no total.

6.1.9 Scatter Graphs and Correlation (S6)

Bivariate data gives two values for each person or item. A scatter graph plots each pair as a point to see whether the variables are related.
  • Positive correlation — as one variable increases, the other tends to increase.
  • Negative correlation — as one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.
  • No correlation — no linear relationship between the variables.
  • Strong correlation: points close to a straight line. Weak: more scattered, but a trend is still visible.

Correlation is not causation

A correlation shows that two variables are related, not that one causes the other. Often a third variable drives both.

  • Umbrella sales and the number of people wearing wellies are positively correlated — rain causes both.
  • Across towns, the number of cafés and the number of road accidents are positively correlated — bigger towns have more of both.

Lines of best fit

A line of best fit is a single straight ruled line that follows the trend, with the points roughly balanced either side of it. It need not go through the origin or through any of the plotted points. Ignore outliers when placing it, and only draw one when there is correlation.
Data (hours, words per minute): (1, 22), (2, 27), (3, 33), (4, 36), (5, 43), (6, 47), (7, 52), (8, 58).

Correlation: strong positive — people who practise more tend to type faster.
Estimate for 4.5 hours: go up from 4.5 to the line and across: about 40 words per minute (a mark scheme would accept roughly 37–43 from a sensible line).

Interpolation and extrapolation

Interpolation — estimating inside the range of the data; fairly reliable when the correlation is strong.

Extrapolation — estimating outside the range of the data; unreliable because the pattern may not continue (typing speed cannot keep rising forever).
For days between 4 °C and 16 °C, a line of best fit for daily heating cost $y$ pence against temperature $x$ °C passes through $(5, 64)$ and $(15, 24)$.

Gradient $= \dfrac{24 - 64}{15 - 5} = \dfrac{-40}{10} = -4$, so $y = -4x + c$. Using $(5, 64)$: $64 = -20 + c$, so $c = 84$ and $y = -4x + 84$.

At 9 °C (interpolation): $y = -36 + 84 = 48$p.
At 25 °C (extrapolation): $y = -100 + 84 = -16$p — a negative cost is impossible, which shows why extrapolating is dangerous.

Meaning of the gradient: the cost falls by about 4p for every 1 °C rise in temperature.

Outliers

An outlier is a point that does not follow the pattern of the others (e.g. a kiosk that sold very few hot drinks on a cold day because it closed early). Identify it, give a possible reason in context, and leave it out when drawing the line of best fit.

"Describe the relationship" needs the type of correlation and a sentence in context, e.g. "Negative correlation: the warmer the day, the lower the heating cost." For "Is this estimate reliable?", comment on whether it is inside the data range and how close the points are to the line.
