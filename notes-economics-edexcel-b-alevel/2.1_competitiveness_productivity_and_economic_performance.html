Theme 2.1 Competitiveness, Productivity and Economic Performance — Edexcel B

1. Measuring National Income — GDP

Gross Domestic Product (GDP): The total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given time period (usually a year or quarter). It is the most widely used measure of economic output and national income.

1.1 Three Methods of Measuring GDP

All three approaches should give the same figure (the national income identity):

MethodWhat It MeasuresCalculation
Output (Production)Value added by each sector of the economySum of value added in agriculture + manufacturing + services + ...
IncomeTotal incomes earned by factors of productionWages + rent + interest + profits
ExpenditureTotal spending on final goods and servicesC + I + G + (X - M)
GDP (Expenditure) = C + I + G + (X - M)

Where: C = consumer spending, I = investment, G = government spending, X = exports, M = imports.

1.2 Real vs Nominal GDP

  • Nominal (money) GDP: Measured at current prices. Changes in nominal GDP may reflect price changes (inflation) rather than real changes in output.
  • Real GDP: Adjusted for inflation (measured at constant/base-year prices). Shows the actual change in the volume of output. This is the more meaningful measure.
Real GDP = Nominal GDP adjusted for inflation using a GDP deflator

1.3 GDP per Capita

GDP per capita = GDP ÷ Population

A better indicator of average living standards than total GDP because it accounts for population size. A country with high GDP but a very large population may have low GDP per capita (e.g., India).

1.4 Limitations of GDP as a Measure of Living Standards

  • Distribution of income: GDP per capita is an average — it masks inequality. A country could have high GDP per capita but most wealth concentrated among a few.
  • The informal/shadow economy: GDP does not capture unrecorded economic activity (cash-in-hand work, illegal trade, subsistence farming). This is larger in developing countries.
  • Non-marketed output: Unpaid work such as housework, childcare, and voluntary work is excluded.
  • Quality of life factors: GDP ignores leisure time, environmental quality, health, education quality, personal freedom, and happiness.
  • Negative externalities: GDP counts all output, including production that causes pollution and environmental destruction. "Bads" like oil spill clean-ups increase GDP.
  • Composition of output: GDP does not distinguish between spending on weapons and spending on hospitals.
  • Purchasing Power Parity (PPP): When comparing GDP across countries, exchange rate differences can distort comparisons. PPP-adjusted figures give a more accurate picture of relative living standards.

1.5 Alternative Measures of Living Standards

MeasureWhat It IncludesAdvantage over GDP

GNI (Gross National Income)GDP + net income from abroadCaptures income earned by nationals overseas

Case Study: China's GDP Growth and Living Standards
China's GDP grew at an average of around 10% per year from 1980 to 2015, lifting hundreds of millions out of poverty. However, GDP growth overstated improvements in living standards for several reasons: severe air pollution in cities like Beijing imposed enormous health costs not captured by GDP; the Gini coefficient rose from 0.30 in 1980 to over 0.47 by 2012 (highly unequal); and much growth came from environmentally destructive heavy industry. China's HDI (0.788 in 2022) ranks significantly lower than its GDP ranking would suggest, illustrating GDP's limitations.

2. Inflation

Inflation: A sustained increase in the general price level over a period of time — meaning money loses purchasing power. It is measured as the annual percentage change in a price index.

Deflation: A sustained decrease in the general price level (negative inflation rate).

Disinflation: A fall in the rate of inflation (prices still rising, but more slowly).

2.1 Measuring Inflation — CPI and RPI

FeatureCPI (Consumer Price Index)RPI (Retail Price Index)
Used byGovernment's target measure; used by the Bank of EnglandIndex-linked gilts, some pensions, rail fare increases
Includes housing costs?Partially (CPIH includes owner-occupier housing costs)Yes — includes mortgage interest payments
CoverageBroader populationExcludes top 4% earners and pensioners relying on state benefits
Typical valueUsually lower than RPIUsually higher than CPI

Both use a weighted basket of around 700 goods and services, updated annually to reflect changing spending patterns. The Living Costs and Food Survey samples around 5,000 households to determine weights. Items are added (e.g., oat milk, smartwatches) and removed (e.g., DVD players) each year to keep the basket representative.

2.2 Causes of Inflation

Demand-Pull Inflation

Caused by aggregate demand (AD) increasing faster than aggregate supply (AS) — "too much money chasing too few goods." When the economy is near or at full capacity, increased spending leads to price rises rather than output increases.

Causes of rising AD: Consumer confidence, lower interest rates, tax cuts, rising asset prices (wealth effect), increased government spending, weaker exchange rate (boosting exports).

Cost-Push Inflation

Caused by increases in the costs of production — firms pass on higher costs as higher prices. This shifts the SRAS curve to the left.

Causes: Rising oil/energy prices, higher wages (above productivity growth), weaker exchange rate (making imports more expensive), higher commodity prices, increased indirect taxes, supply chain disruptions.

Figure 1: Demand-pull inflation — AD shifts right, price level rises from P0 to P1.

2.3 Consequences of Inflation

EffectExplanation
Reduced purchasing powerMoney buys less — real incomes fall if wages do not keep pace with inflation.
Menu costsFirms must update price lists, catalogues, and vending machines more frequently.
Shoe-leather costsPeople spend more time and effort managing their money to minimise the erosion of real value.

2.4 Consequences of Deflation

Deflation can be even more dangerous than inflation:

  • Delayed spending: Consumers postpone purchases expecting further price falls — reduces AD further (deflationary spiral).
  • Rising real debt burden: The real value of debt increases, causing defaults and financial instability.
  • Falling profits and wages: Firms see revenue fall but may have fixed nominal costs — squeezing profits and leading to redundancies.
  • Monetary policy ineffectiveness: Interest rates cannot go below zero (the zero lower bound), making it hard to stimulate the economy.
Case Study: UK Cost-of-Living Crisis 2022-23
UK CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% in October 2022, the highest in 41 years. The primary drivers were cost-push: energy prices surged following Russia's invasion of Ukraine (wholesale gas prices rose over 400%), global supply chain disruptions persisted post-COVID, food prices rose sharply due to higher fertiliser and transport costs, and a weakened pound made imports more expensive. Real wages fell for many months — average earnings growth of around 6% was far below inflation — causing the sharpest fall in living standards since records began. The Bank of England raised the base rate from 0.1% to 5.25% to combat inflation.

3. Unemployment

Unemployment: When people who are willing and able to work, and are actively seeking employment, cannot find a job. The UK uses two measures: the Claimant Count (those claiming unemployment-related benefits) and the ILO/Labour Force Survey measure (those without a job who have sought work in the last 4 weeks and are available to start within 2 weeks).

3.1 Measuring Unemployment

MeasureMethodStrengthsWeaknesses
Claimant CountNumber claiming JSA or UC (searching for work element)Monthly, cheap, accurate countExcludes those not eligible or not claiming; changes with benefit rules
ILO/LFSQuarterly survey of 40,000 householdsInternationally comparable; counts hidden unemploymentSample size — subject to sampling error; published with a lag

3.2 Types and Causes of Unemployment

Cyclical (Demand-Deficient) Unemployment

Caused by a fall in aggregate demand during a recession or downturn. When AD falls, firms reduce output and lay off workers. This is the most significant type during recessions and is directly linked to the economic cycle.

Structural Unemployment

Caused by a long-term decline in certain industries due to changes in technology, international competition, or consumer demand. Workers' skills no longer match the jobs available — there is a mismatch between the skills of the unemployed and the requirements of available vacancies. Can be geographical (jobs in different regions) or occupational (different skills needed).

Frictional Unemployment

Short-term unemployment that occurs as people move between jobs. Inevitable in a dynamic economy — people leave one job and take time to find the next. Generally considered non-harmful and even desirable (better job matching).

Seasonal Unemployment

Unemployment that occurs at certain times of year due to seasonal patterns in demand (e.g., tourism workers in winter, agricultural workers between harvests).

Real Wage (Classical) Unemployment

Occurs when wages are held above the market-clearing level (e.g., by minimum wage laws, trade union power), creating excess supply of labour. At the above-equilibrium wage, quantity of labour supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

3.3 Natural Rate of Unemployment (NRU)

Natural rate of unemployment: The level of unemployment that exists when the economy is at its long-run equilibrium (full employment) — consisting of frictional, structural, and seasonal unemployment. At the NRU, there is no cyclical unemployment, and inflation is stable. Also known as the NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment).

Case Study: UK Post-Industrial Structural Unemployment
The decline of coal mining, steel production, and shipbuilding in the 1980s left communities in South Wales, Northern England, and Central Scotland with severe structural unemployment. Workers had skills specific to heavy industry that were not transferable to the growing service sector concentrated in the South East. Decades later, these areas still have higher unemployment rates and lower average incomes than the national average — a clear example of hysteresis and the geographical immobility of labour. Government responses have included Enterprise Zones, regional development agencies, and retraining programmes, with mixed success.

4. Balance of Payments

Balance of payments: A record of all financial transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period of time. It always balances overall (surpluses in one account are offset by deficits in another).

4.1 Structure

AccountWhat It RecordsKey Components
Current AccountTrade in goods and services, plus income and transfersTrade balance (goods), trade in services, primary income (investment income), secondary income (transfers, e.g., foreign aid, contributions to international organisations)
Capital AccountCapital transfers and non-produced, non-financial assetsDebt forgiveness, sale of patents, EU structural funds (small account)
Financial AccountInvestment flows and changes in reservesForeign Direct Investment (FDI), portfolio investment, reserve assets, other investment
Current Account + Capital Account + Financial Account = 0

4.2 Current Account Deficit

The UK has run a persistent current account deficit since the mid-1980s (typically around 2-4% of GDP in recent years). This means the UK imports more goods and services than it exports, and pays out more in investment income and transfers than it receives.

Causes of the UK's Current Account Deficit

  • High consumer spending on imports: UK consumers have a high marginal propensity to import — strong demand for foreign goods (cars, electronics, energy).
  • Decline of manufacturing: De-industrialisation since the 1980s reduced the UK's goods export capacity.
  • Strong pound (historically): Made UK exports expensive and imports cheap.
  • Low savings rate: UK consumers save less and spend more, including on imports.
  • Primary income deficit: Foreign ownership of UK assets means investment income flows out.

Consequences of a Persistent Deficit

  • Must be financed by capital inflows (selling assets to foreigners, borrowing).
  • Increases foreign ownership of domestic assets — income flows out in future.
  • Could lead to currency depreciation if financed by short-term "hot money" that can withdraw suddenly.
  • May not be a problem if deficit reflects high investment inflows (FDI creates jobs and growth).
Evaluation: How Significant Is a Current Account Deficit?
  • A deficit financed by FDI may actually be beneficial — foreign firms create jobs and transfer technology.
  • The US has run a deficit for decades without crisis — the dollar's reserve currency status helps.
  • A deficit reflects high domestic demand — may indicate a strong economy.
  • However, a deficit financed by short-term capital flows is vulnerable to sudden stops.
  • Persistent deficits may indicate a structural competitiveness problem.

5. The Economic (Business) Cycle

Economic cycle: The regular fluctuations in real GDP around the long-run trend rate of growth. The economy moves through phases of boom, slowdown, recession, and recovery.
Figure 2: The economic cycle — real GDP fluctuates around the long-run trend rate of growth.

5.1 Phases of the Cycle

PhaseCharacteristics
BoomHigh GDP growth, low unemployment, rising wages, high consumer/business confidence, rising inflation, positive output gap. Risk of economy overheating.
SlowdownGrowth decelerates, confidence falls, some firms fail, unemployment begins rising.
RecessionTechnically defined as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth. Rising unemployment, falling business investment, falling consumer spending, falling inflation or deflation, negative output gap.
RecoveryGDP begins to grow again, unemployment starts falling, confidence returns, investment picks up.

5.2 The Output Gap

Output gap: The difference between actual GDP and potential GDP (the maximum sustainable output of the economy).
Positive output gap: Actual GDP > potential GDP — economy overheating, inflationary pressures.
Negative output gap: Actual GDP < potential GDP — spare capacity, higher unemployment, deflationary pressures.
Figure 3: A negative output gap — actual output Y1 is below potential output Yf, indicating spare capacity and unemployment.
Case Study: The 2008-09 UK Recession
The global financial crisis triggered the UK's deepest recession since WWII. GDP fell by 6.3% from peak to trough. Unemployment rose from around 5% to over 8% (peaking at 8.5% in late 2011). The banking system was on the verge of collapse — the government had to nationalise Northern Rock and inject £45.5 billion into RBS and over £20 billion into Lloyds. The Bank of England cut rates to 0.5% and introduced quantitative easing. The fiscal deficit reached over 10% of GDP. The recovery was slow — real GDP did not return to its pre-crisis peak until 2013, and the decade of low productivity growth that followed is called the "lost decade."

6. Productive Efficiency

Spec 2.3. National economic performance is not only a set of headline numbers — it is built out of how efficiently individual firms turn inputs into output. This section moves from the macro picture to the factory floor and back again.

6.1 Productivity

Productivity: output per unit of input in a given time period. Labour productivity is usually measured as output per worker or output per hour worked.
Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers (or hours worked)
Worked example. A plant produces 96,000 units a year with 60 workers. Labour productivity = 96,000 ÷ 60 = 1,600 units per worker per year. After investing in new machinery, output rises to 115,200 units with 64 workers: 115,200 ÷ 64 = 1,800 units per worker. Percentage change in productivity = (1,800 − 1,600) ÷ 1,600 × 100 = 12.5%. Note that output rose 20% while productivity rose only 12.5% — part of the extra output came from hiring, not from working more efficiently. Separating those two is exactly what a data-response question is testing.

Factors influencing productivity

  • Capital per worker — better machinery, software and premises. This is the main long-run driver, and it requires investment, which requires finance.
  • Skills, education and training — human capital. Slow to build, easily lost if trained workers leave.
  • Management quality — how work is organised, scheduled and measured. Often the cheapest source of gains.
  • Motivation — pay structure, job design, security.
  • Technology and process innovation — automation, better information systems.

Why productivity matters beyond the firm

  • Productivity and competitiveness: higher output per worker lowers unit labour cost, so the firm can undercut rivals or hold price and earn a wider margin. The same logic applies to a whole economy's exports.
  • Productivity and wages: over the long run, real wages can only rise sustainably if output per worker rises. A pay rise not matched by productivity raises unit costs and feeds through to prices.
  • Productivity and economic growth: an economy grows either by using more inputs or by using them better. Once the workforce stops growing, productivity growth is the only source of rising GDP per head — which links this section directly back to the living-standards discussion in section 1.

Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

Labour intensiveCapital intensive

6.2 Capacity Utilisation

Capacity utilisation: the proportion of a firm's maximum possible output that it is currently producing.
Capacity utilisation (%) = current output ÷ maximum possible output × 100

Full capacity means output is at the maximum the existing plant and workforce can produce. Spare capacity is the gap between that maximum and actual output.

Worked example. A factory can make 25,000 units a month and is currently making 17,500. Capacity utilisation = 17,500 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = 70%.

Fixed costs are £150,000 a month, so fixed cost per unit = £150,000 ÷ 17,500 = £8.57 (to 2 d.p.).
If orders rise to 22,500 units, utilisation = 22,500 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = 90%, and fixed cost per unit falls to £150,000 ÷ 22,500 = £6.67.

Raising utilisation from 70% to 90% cut fixed cost per unit by £8.57 − £6.67 = £1.90, a fall of 1.90 ÷ 8.57 × 100 = 22.2% — without buying a single new machine. That is the whole argument for managing capacity.
Under-utilisation (low %)Over-utilisation (near or at 100%)
Fixed costs spread over few units, so average cost is highNo slack to handle a breakdown, a rush order or an absence
Idle machinery and staff; morale and job-security worriesMachines run without maintenance windows, so failures rise
Signals to rivals and investors that demand is weakOvertime and rushed work raise costs and lower quality
But: room to accept a large new order immediatelyStaff burnout, higher turnover, missed delivery dates

Most firms therefore aim for high but not maximum utilisation — typically somewhere around 85–90% — leaving a deliberate margin for maintenance and for demand that arrives unannounced.

Ways of improving capacity utilisation

  • Raise demand — promotion, new markets, off-peak pricing to fill quiet periods.
  • Subcontract in — produce for another firm under its brand, filling spare capacity with someone else's orders.
  • Rationalise — cut the maximum: close a site, sell equipment, reduce hours. This raises utilisation by shrinking the denominator, and it is largely irreversible.
  • Redeploy — move staff and machinery to product lines where demand is stronger.

6.3 Efficiency and Competitiveness Using Lean Production

Lean production is a set of methods aimed at producing more with less — less stock, less waste, less time, less rework.

MethodWhat it doesWhat it costs or risks
Quality controlInspects finished output and rejects defects at the end of the lineDefective units are already made, so the materials and labour in them are wasted
Quality assuranceBuilds checks into every stage so defects are caught where they occurNeeds training and worker responsibility at every stage
Total Quality Management (TQM)Makes quality the responsibility of every employee, with the internal "next stage" treated as a customer; aims at zero defectsA culture change, not a procedure — slow to embed and easily lost when staff turn over
Kaizen (continuous improvement)Many small improvements suggested by the people doing the work, applied continuously rather than in big projectsRequires genuine willingness to act on suggestions; gains are incremental, so it will not rescue a failing product
Just in time (JIT)Stock arrives as it is needed rather than being held; frees warehouse space and working capitalNo buffer, so one late delivery, strike or border delay stops production entirely
Evaluation: lean production and competitive advantage
  • The cost case: less stock means less working capital tied up and lower storage and insurance costs; fewer defects means less wasted material and less rework.
  • The revenue case: consistent quality supports a price premium and repeat purchase; shorter development lead times let the firm reach the market before rivals, which matters most where products date quickly.
  • The resilience case against: JIT converts a cost saving into a supply-chain risk. A firm holding no buffer stock saves money in every normal year and loses production in the abnormal one.
  • It depends on supplier reliability, the distance and complexity of the supply chain, how predictable demand is, and whether the workforce is stable enough for TQM and Kaizen to take hold.
  • Counter-argument: lean methods are widely known and widely copied, so they may be necessary to compete without being sufficient to win. Sustained advantage usually needs something rivals cannot imitate.

6.4 Impact on Costs and Sales Revenue

  • Average costs fall for two separate reasons: higher utilisation spreads fixed costs over more units, and less waste lowers variable cost per unit. Keep the two mechanisms apart in an answer — examiners reward the distinction.
  • Minimising waste of resources — scrap, rework, excess stock, unnecessary movement and waiting time are all inputs paid for that produce nothing saleable.
  • Short product development lead times — getting from idea to sale faster means responding to a demand shift before rivals, charging a higher launch price, and recovering development costs sooner.

7. Research and Development and Innovation (spec 2.1.3)

Lean production (6.3) is how a firm gets more out of what it already makes. Research and development is how it changes what it makes and how it makes it. Both are routes to competitive advantage; this one is slower, riskier and harder for rivals to copy.

Research and development (R&D): systematic spending aimed at creating new knowledge, and at turning that knowledge into new or improved products and processes.
Invention is creating something new. Innovation is bringing it successfully to market. Firms are paid for the second, not the first.

7.1 Product and Process Innovation

Product innovationProcess innovation
What changesWhat is sold — a new or improved good or serviceHow it is made or delivered
Effect on the demand curveShifts demand right and makes it less price elastic — the product has fewer close substitutesLittle direct effect
Effect on costsRaises them in the short run (development, tooling, launch)Lowers average cost, raising the margin at any given price
Competitive routeDifferentiation — sell at a higher priceCost leadership — sell at a lower price and still profit
ExampleA manufacturer adding a feature no rival offersRobotic picking in a distribution centre cutting the labour hours per order

7.2 The Incentive: Innovation and Market Power

A firm spends on R&D because a successful innovation gives it, for a period, something no rival has. That is market power: demand for the product becomes less elastic, the firm can hold price above the competitive level, and the supernormal profit funds the next round of R&D. Patents and other intellectual property rights extend the period by making imitation illegal — a UK patent runs for up to 20 years if renewal fees are paid. The link runs both ways, and 9EB0 examines both directions: innovation creates market power, and the prospect of market power is what makes the spending worthwhile.

The counter-argument matters for evaluation. Sustained market power can reduce the incentive to innovate: a firm with no credible rival can raise profit more cheaply by cutting costs than by inventing anything. Whether competition or concentration produces more innovation is a genuine debate, not a settled result, and a 12 or 20-mark answer should say so.

7.3 The Role of State Funding

R&D produces knowledge spillovers: once a discovery is made, others learn from it without paying. The private return to the firm is therefore below the return to society, so a purely private market funds less R&D than is socially optimal — a positive externality, and the economic case for state support. The main instruments are direct grants and public research funding, R&D tax credits that cut the post-tax cost of qualifying spending, and public research institutions and universities whose output firms use free.

Case Study: who pays for UK R&D
ONS figures published on 7 August 2026 put total research and development performed in the UK at £79.4 billion in 2024, equal to 2.71% of GDP, marginally down from 2.72% in 2023. Businesses performed £55.6 billion of it — 70% of the total, and £2.4 billion (4.5%) more than in 2023 — with higher education performing £17.9 billion, or 23%. Two points for the exam. First, business is much the largest performer, so policy that changes the private return, such as tax credits, moves the largest block of spending. Second, an R&D intensity that is flat in a growing economy means R&D spending is growing at about the same rate as output, not faster, which is the standing complaint about the UK's productivity record in 6.1.
Evaluation: is R&D spending worth it?
  • It is a sunk cost with an uncertain return. Most projects fail. The spending is committed before anyone knows whether demand exists, which is precisely the risk the entrepreneur is rewarded for bearing.
  • Long payback. Pharmaceutical development runs for a decade or more, so the firm needs the cash and the patience. A firm with weak cash flow cannot play.
  • Imitation. Where patents are weak or easily designed around, the innovator carries the cost and rivals take the market — the first-mover pays to educate the customer.
  • The alternative uses. The same money could buy a rival, cut price to win share, or be returned to shareholders. R&D has to beat those, not just be positive.
  • It depends on the market. In a dynamic market (Topic 1.1, 11.5) not spending is the bigger risk; in a stable one the spending may never be recovered.

7.4 The Product Life Cycle and Extension Strategies

Innovation has a commercial clock attached to it. The product life cycle traces sales of a product over time through five stages.

Extension strategies are attempts to lengthen maturity rather than let decline begin: a redesign or new variant; new packaging or formats; new uses for the same product; a new market, at home or, as Topic 3.1 sets out, overseas where the product may still be in its growth phase; price repositioning; or renewed advertising. They are cheaper than developing a replacement, which is exactly why firms reach for them, and that is also the criticism — an extension postpones the decision to innovate.

Exam Tip: the life cycle is a planning tool, not a law. Plenty of products never leave development, some jump straight to decline, and a few (Coca-Cola, Heinz baked beans) have held maturity for a century. Use it to explain why a firm is doing something — cutting price, extending, or cross-subsidising a new launch from a mature product's cash — and then say what else could explain the same decision.

8. The Digital Economy (spec 2.1.4)

The specification treats the digital economy as a change in market conditions that alters information, costs and the boundaries of a market — not as a topic about technology. Organise an answer by the three channels it names.

8.1 Market Information

  • Price comparison sites cut consumers' search costs almost to zero. Better-informed buyers switch more readily, so demand for any one supplier becomes more price elastic and price dispersion narrows. This is the market moving towards the perfect-information assumption of Topic 4.1. Against that: sites are funded by commission from the firms listed, so what looks like the whole market may be only the paying part of it.
  • Viral marketing spreads a promotional message through consumers' own sharing. The marginal cost of an extra person reached is near zero, which is why a small firm can reach a national audience without a national advertising budget — but the firm does not control where it goes or what is said about it.
  • Social media gives firms a direct, continuous and cheap channel to customers, and gives customers a public one back. Reviews and ratings are secondary market research that arrives free and in real time; they also mean a quality failure is visible to every potential buyer within hours.

8.2 The Supply Side

  • Micromarketing — using transaction and browsing data to target a promotion at a very small, precisely defined group, or at an individual. This is segmentation (Topic 1.1, 10.3) taken to its limit, and it raises the return on a marketing pound by cutting the share of it spent on people who were never going to buy.
  • Online retailing and distribution — selling without a shop removes rent, rates and shop staff from the cost base and replaces them with warehousing, picking and delivery. Fixed costs fall relative to a store estate, so the minimum efficient scale of entry falls and new firms can enter markets that once required capital most of them did not have.
  • Digital skills — the firm now has to recruit, train and retain people who can run the analytics, the platform and the security. Where those skills are scarce they command a wage premium, so part of the saving on premises returns as a saving on labour foregone.

8.3 The Demand Side

  • The long tail. A physical shop stocks only what sells fast enough to justify its shelf space. An online retailer with a central warehouse, or a streaming service with no physical stock at all, can carry titles that sell a handful of copies a year. Aggregate enough of them and the slow-selling "tail" becomes a large share of revenue. The consumer gain is choice; the producer gain is that niche demand becomes servable at all.
  • Wider geographical markets. A firm's market is no longer the area people will travel to. A specialist producer in a small town can serve national or international demand, which makes a niche strategy viable at a scale it was not before — and means the local firm's competitors are now everywhere.

8.4 Impact on Markets and Firms

  • Costs: lower distribution and search costs; higher spending on technology, data and delivery.
  • Prices: more elastic demand and easier comparison push prices down in commoditised markets; better data on willingness to pay supports finer segmentation and price discrimination, which pushes them up for some buyers.
  • Profit and loss: winners take a larger share because network effects and near-zero marginal cost on digital goods reward scale; losers include high-street retailers carrying a fixed cost base against falling footfall.
  • Firm creation and destruction: lower entry barriers raise the birth rate of firms, and the same forces raise the death rate of incumbents that cannot match them. This is creative destruction (Topic 1.1) running faster.
Case Study: how big is online retail now?
ONS retail sales figures for August 2026 put the proportion of retail sales made online at 28.8%, up from 28.4% in July 2026. Roughly three pounds in every ten spent with UK retailers is now spent without entering a shop. For a retailer with a store estate that is a structural problem, not a cyclical one: the fixed costs of rent, rates and staff sit against a shrinking share of the market, so the break-even output of each store rises even when total sales are steady. For an online-only entrant it is the reason a national market can be attacked without a single shop.

9. How Small Firms Compete (spec 2.1.5)

Economies of scale (Topic 4.1) say that large firms should have lower unit costs and drive small ones out. Most firms are small anyway. Business Population Estimates put the UK private sector at 5.7 million businesses at the start of 2025, of which 5.64 million had fewer than 50 employees; 99.9% of all UK businesses were small or medium-sized, and only 8,335 were large. The specification asks how they survive, and the answer is that they compete on things scale does not buy.

  • Product differentiation and unique selling points. A USP is the one thing a buyer can get here and nowhere else — a recipe, a design, a location, a guarantee. It makes demand less price elastic, so the small firm does not have to win on price against a rival with lower unit costs.
  • Flexibility in responding to customer needs. A small firm can change a product, accept a special order or reprice today; a national chain needs a category review. In a dynamic market that speed is worth more than a cost advantage.
  • Customer service. Personal, knowledgeable service is labour-intensive and therefore expensive to scale, which is exactly why a large rival struggles to copy it. It also raises switching costs, because the relationship is with a person.
  • Targeting niche markets. A segment too small to interest a large firm can be profitable for a small one with low overheads. The niche's low volume is a barrier that protects the incumbent as much as it limits them.
  • Relationships with stakeholders. Long-standing suppliers give better terms and priority in a shortage; local employees stay; local customers and the community provide word-of-mouth promotion at no cost. These relationships are an intangible asset a new entrant cannot buy.
Evaluation: how secure is any of this?
  • A USP that works attracts imitation, and a large rival can copy a product faster than a small one can build a brand.
  • Niche protection ends the moment the niche grows enough to be worth a large firm's attention.
  • Cost disadvantage is real and permanent: no amount of service offsets a rival buying inputs 15% cheaper if the customer is buying on price.
  • Small firms are more exposed to the cycle (5) and to credit conditions (Topic 1.2) — they hold less cash and have less collateral.
  • On the other side, the digital economy (8) has cut the cost of reaching a national niche audience, so the small firm's position is stronger than it was.

10. Competing on Price (spec 2.2.2)

Elasticity (Topic 1.1, 7) says how demand responds to price. This section is about the decision itself: which pricing strategy, and why.

Calculating a mark-up. 9EB0 asks for this as a 4-mark calculation. Mark-up is on cost: mark-up % = (price − unit cost) / unit cost × 100. Margin is on price: margin % = (price − unit cost) / price × 100. A £40 cost sold at £58 is a mark-up of 18/40 = 45.0% and a margin of 18/58 = 31.0%. Read which one the question wants.

10.2 Choosing Between Them

Spec 2.2.2(b) lists the factors, and an 8 or 10-mark answer is built by working through them for the firm in the extract:

  • Number of USPs and the amount of differentiation. A strongly differentiated product supports skimming or a premium competitive price; an undifferentiated one leaves only cost plus or matching the market.
  • Price elasticity of demand. Inelastic demand means a price rise raises revenue, so a premium strategy pays. Elastic demand means penetration or competitive pricing.
  • Amount of competition. The more rivals and the closer their products, the less freedom the firm has to depart from the market price.
  • Strength of brand. A strong brand is the source of the inelasticity, and it is why the same physical product sells at different prices under different labels.
  • Stage in the product life cycle. Skimming at launch, competitive pricing at maturity, discounting in decline. This is the direct link back to 7.4.
  • Costs and the need to make a profit. No strategy survives a price below average total cost for long. Penetration is a deliberate, financed loss with an end date, not a business model.

10.3 Pricing and Social Trends

Spec 2.2.2(c) asks how pricing has changed with online selling and comparison sites. Three effects to use:

  • Prices are now visible. A firm setting a price knows that any customer can see every rival's price in seconds, so unexplained premiums are punished and headline prices converge, even where the total cost including delivery does not.
  • Prices move. Dynamic and algorithmic pricing changes the price by time of day, device, stock level and demand. This is cost plus and competitive pricing recalculated continuously rather than quarterly.
  • The comparison site becomes the market. Ranking on the site determines whether a firm is seen at all, so price is set to hit a position in a list rather than to hit a margin — and the site's commission is a cost that has to come out of that price.

11. Non-Price Competition and Marketing (spec 2.2.3)

Price is one variable. Everything else the firm does to shift demand is non-price competition, and the economics of it is a single idea: successful marketing shifts the demand curve to the right and makes it steeper (less price elastic). The rightward shift raises volume at any price; the steeper slope means the firm can raise price without losing proportionate volume. Both raise revenue, and both have to be set against what the marketing cost.

Figure 5: successful marketing. Demand shifts from D to D1 — at the same price P the firm sells Q1 rather than Q — and D1 is steeper, so a subsequent price rise costs less volume than it would have done on D.

11.1 The Methods

  • Product differentiation — real differences (design, quality, features, reliability) and perceived ones (brand, packaging, association). Real differentiation is more durable; perceived differentiation is faster and cheaper to create.
  • Advertising and other promotion — paid media, sponsorship, influencer and content marketing, sales promotions, loyalty schemes, public relations. Heavy advertising is also a sunk cost that acts as a barrier to entry (Topic 4.1), because a new entrant must spend at the same level to be noticed and cannot recover it if it fails.
  • Distribution methods — being available where and when the customer wants to buy: shelf position, next-day delivery, a marketplace listing, an app. Availability is a demand determinant in its own right; a product that cannot be bought conveniently is not competing at all.

11.2 Devising an Appropriate Approach

The right marketing approach follows from everything earlier in this topic: the segment being targeted (Topic 1.1, 10.3), the product's stage in the life cycle (7.4), whether the market is mass or niche, whether the firm is large or small (9), and the budget available. A niche firm advertising nationally wastes most of the spend; a mass-market firm relying on word of mouth will not reach the volume its cost base needs. Digital channels (8) have made narrow targeting cheap, which is why micromarketing has displaced part of the traditional advertising budget.

Evaluation: price or non-price competition?
  • Price cuts are immediate but symmetric — a rival can match one the same day, so the share gain is temporary while the revenue loss is not. Non-price advantages take longer to build and longer to copy.
  • Non-price competition raises costs. Advertising, service and better product specification all have to be paid for, so the firm needs the higher price the differentiation earns.
  • It depends on the buyer. Where the product is a commodity and the buyer is informed and price-sensitive, non-price spending is largely wasted. Where quality is hard to judge before purchase, brand does the work.
  • Market structure matters. In oligopoly, where price cuts are matched (Topic 4.1), non-price competition is the main form competition takes — which is why advertising intensity is highest there.
  • It can be self-cancelling. If every firm raises its advertising spend together, shares may end up unchanged and the whole industry's costs are higher.
Exam Tip: When discussing macroeconomic objectives, always acknowledge potential conflicts — reducing unemployment by raising demand may push up inflation; boosting growth may worsen the current account as imports are sucked in. Productivity is the one variable that can relieve several conflicts at once, because it allows output and real wages to rise without adding to cost pressure. The best answers explain when a trade-off binds and when it does not. On the firm-level half of this topic, the equivalent habit is to say what the firm gives up: every route to competitive advantage here — R&D, digital investment, service, advertising — is a cost incurred before the revenue arrives.
