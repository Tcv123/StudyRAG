Theme 1.1 Scarcity, Enterprise, Markets and Consumers — Edexcel B

1. Scarcity, Choice and Opportunity Cost

Scarcity: The fundamental economic problem — human wants are unlimited, but the resources (factors of production) available to satisfy those wants are finite. This forces societies, firms, and individuals to make choices about how to allocate resources.

1.1 The Economic Problem

Every society must answer three fundamental questions:

  1. What to produce? — Which goods and services should be produced, and in what quantities?
  2. How to produce? — What combination of resources and methods should be used?
  3. For whom to produce? — How should the output be distributed among members of society?

1.2 Factors of Production

1.3 Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost: The value of the next best alternative foregone when a choice is made. It is the real cost of any decision — measured not in money but in what you give up.

Opportunity cost applies at every level:

  • Individual: A student choosing university gives up the salary they could have earned working full-time.
  • Firm: A company investing in a new factory gives up the returns from investing that money elsewhere.
  • Government: Spending on the NHS means less for education, defence, or infrastructure.
Case Study: HS2 Opportunity Cost
The UK government's HS2 high-speed rail project had cost estimates exceeding £100 billion before the northern leg was cancelled in 2023, and even the shortened London–Birmingham line is expected to cost tens of billions. Critics argue the opportunity cost is enormous — that money could fund hundreds of new hospitals, thousands of schools, or significant renewable energy investment. Supporters counter that HS2 will boost productivity and regional connectivity, generating returns that outweigh the opportunity cost. This illustrates how opportunity cost involves subjective judgement about the value of alternatives.

1.4 Free Goods vs Economic Goods

  • Free goods: Have no opportunity cost — they are not scarce (e.g., air, sunlight).
  • Economic goods: Have an opportunity cost because resources are used in their production (e.g., cars, healthcare).

2. Enterprise, Adding Value and Specialisation

Enterprise: The factor of production that organises land, labour and capital into a working business and carries the risk of the venture. The person who does this is the entrepreneur, and their reward is profit — which may be negative.

2.1 The Role of the Entrepreneur in the Economy

Spec point 1.2.1 asks you to explain what entrepreneurs actually do. Three activities:

  • Creative destruction — organising the factors of production to create and set up an enterprise. The phrase is Joseph Schumpeter's: new firms and new methods displace older ones, so the same act that creates a business destroys the position of an incumbent. Streaming subscriptions displaced DVD rental; online grocery delivery displaced part of the weekly supermarket shop.
  • Making decisions to operate, expand and develop the business — what to produce, at what scale, in which markets, with whose money.
  • Adding value — selling the output for more than the cost of the inputs used to make it. This is the test of whether the enterprise is worth running at all.
Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials and components.
It is not the same as profit: added value still has to cover wages, rent, energy and overheads before anything is left.
Figure 1: Added value is the gap between the selling price and the cost of bought-in inputs — here £3.20 − £0.45 = £2.75 per cup. It is not profit until staff, premises and overheads are paid.
Worked calculation: A workshop buys leather and fittings for £18 per bag and sells each bag for £65. Added value per bag = £65 − £18 = £47. If it makes 400 bags a month, total added value = 400 × £47 = £18,800. Monthly wages, rent and overheads are £14,500, so monthly profit = £18,800 − £14,500 = £4,300. Showing the two steps separately is what earns the marks.

Case Study: How Many Firms Are Born and How Many Die
ONS Business Demography for 2024 — the most recent full year published — recorded around 317,000 UK business births, a birth rate of 11.1%, and around 280,000 business deaths, a death rate of 9.8%. That death rate was the lowest recorded since 2016. Read the two numbers together and you have creative destruction as a measurable fact: in a single year roughly one business in nine on the register is new and roughly one in ten closes. The churn is the mechanism, not a sign of failure — resources released by closing firms are what new entrants hire.

Exam Tip: Specialisation questions on 9EB0 are usually set in a business context, so answer in business language — unit costs, lead times, staff turnover, supply-chain risk — rather than reciting Adam Smith's pin factory. The strongest evaluation point is that the gains from specialisation depend on the size of the market: a narrow specialist needs enough customers to be worth the loss of flexibility.

3. Demand

Demand: The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price, in a given time period, ceteris paribus (all other things being equal).

3.1 The Law of Demand

There is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded — as price rises, quantity demanded falls, and vice versa. This creates a downward-sloping demand curve. Reasons:

  • Income effect: When price rises, consumers' real income (purchasing power) falls, so they can afford less.
  • Substitution effect: When price rises, consumers switch to cheaper alternatives.
  • Diminishing marginal utility: Each additional unit consumed provides less extra satisfaction, so consumers only buy more at a lower price.

3.2 Individual vs Market Demand

Individual demand is one consumer's demand for a good. Market demand is the horizontal summation of all individual demand curves — at each price, we add together the quantities demanded by all consumers in the market.

Figure 2: The demand curve — downward sloping, showing the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

3.3 Movements Along vs Shifts of the Demand Curve

A movement along the demand curve (contraction or extension) is caused only by a change in the good's own price. A shift of the entire demand curve is caused by a change in a non-price factor.

Case Study: UK Electric Vehicle Demand
Demand for EVs in the UK surged from a few thousand new registrations a year in 2013 to over 300,000 in 2023. Multiple demand shifters explain this: rising environmental awareness (tastes), government grants (the Plug-in Car Grant until 2022 and the Electric Car Grant from July 2025, lowering the effective price), falling battery costs making EVs cheaper, expanding charging infrastructure (complementary goods), and the planned end of new pure petrol/diesel car sales from 2030 (with hybrids allowed until 2035). This represents a significant rightward shift of the demand curve for EVs.

4. Supply

Supply: The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at a given price, in a given time period, ceteris paribus.

4.1 The Law of Supply

There is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied — as price rises, quantity supplied rises. This creates an upward-sloping supply curve. Reasons:

  • Profit motive: Higher prices mean higher potential profits, incentivising firms to produce more.
  • New entrants: Higher prices attract new firms into the market.
  • Covering costs: Higher prices allow higher-cost firms to enter the market profitably.
Figure 4: The supply curve — upward sloping, showing the positive relationship between price and quantity supplied.

5. Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium: The point where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at a given price. There is no tendency for price to change — the market clears with no excess demand or excess supply.
Figure 5: Market equilibrium where D and S intersect at price Pe and quantity Qe.

5.1 Disequilibrium

  • Excess demand (shortage): Price below equilibrium, Qd > Qs. Consumers bid price up towards equilibrium.
  • Excess supply (surplus): Price above equilibrium, Qs > Qd. Firms reduce price towards equilibrium.

5.2 Changes in Equilibrium

  • Increase in demand (D shifts right) → higher equilibrium price and quantity.
  • Decrease in demand (D shifts left) → lower equilibrium price and quantity.
  • Increase in supply (S shifts right) → lower equilibrium price, higher quantity.
  • Decrease in supply (S shifts left) → higher equilibrium price, lower quantity.
Exam Tip: When both demand and supply shift simultaneously, you can determine the direction of change in either price or quantity, but not both — one will be indeterminate. Always state this in your answer.

6. Business Objectives and Stakeholders

Business objective: a target the firm sets itself and against which its decisions can be judged. A firm's objective is a choice, not a law of nature — which is why two firms in the same market can price, invest and hire very differently.

6.1 The Three Objectives the Spec Names

ObjectiveDecision ruleWhat you would observe
Profit maximisationProduce where the last unit adds as much to revenue as it adds to cost (MC = MR)Prices held up even when rivals discount; investment only where the return clears a hurdle rate
Sales maximisationSell the largest volume that still covers costsAggressive discounting, loss-leading lines, heavy promotion to win volume and market share
SatisficingAchieve an outcome good enough to keep every stakeholder group quiet, rather than the best possible outcome for oneSteady dividends, steady pay settlements, no dramatic strategy changes — common where managers, not owners, run the firm

Other objectives on the specification

  • Survival — the overriding objective for a new firm, and for any firm in a downturn. Price may be cut below the level that maximises profit simply to keep cash coming in.
  • Market share — pursued where scale lowers unit costs or where the market is likely to end up with few sellers.
  • Cost efficiency — driving down cost per unit; the objective behind most process investment.
  • Return on investment — profit measured against the capital tied up, which lets a firm compare unlike projects.
  • Employee welfare — pay, conditions, security; raises costs now, but can lower recruitment and training costs later.
  • Customer satisfaction — measured by repeat purchase and retention; the objective that justifies spending on service.
  • Social objectives — emissions, sourcing, community commitments, written into the firm's own targets.
Divorce of ownership from control: in a large company the owners are shareholders but the decisions are taken by salaried managers. Managers are rewarded for growth, security and their own standing, which pulls towards sales maximisation and satisficing rather than profit maximisation. This is the standard explanation of why observed behaviour so often departs from MC = MR.

6.2 Stakeholders and Their Objectives

Stakeholder (economic agent): any group affected by what the firm does, whether or not it owns any part of it.
Figure 6: Stakeholder map. Each group wants something different from the same set of decisions, which is why objectives conflict.

6.3 Stakeholder Conflicts

  • Shareholders v employees: a pay rise is a cost to one group and income to the other. Automating a process cuts unit costs and cuts jobs at the same time.
  • Customers v shareholders: cutting price wins customers and reduces the margin available for dividends.
  • Suppliers v the firm: stretching payment terms improves the firm's cash position and worsens the supplier's — which is why late payment is a standing complaint of small suppliers to large buyers.
  • Community v shareholders: moving production to a cheaper location raises returns and removes local employment.

Conflicts are not always zero-sum. Better pay can cut turnover and training costs; cutting waste can lower costs and emissions together. The examinable judgement is when the interests line up and when they genuinely do not.

6.4 Corporate Social Responsibility

CSR is the idea that a firm should accept responsibilities to stakeholders beyond its legal minimum and its shareholders. In practice it shows up as sourcing standards, emissions targets, pay floors and community spending.

Evaluation: is CSR a cost or an investment?
  • Investment: it can raise price premiums, protect a brand against boycott, cut staff turnover and reduce the risk of future regulation.
  • Cost: it raises unit costs now against benefits that are uncertain and far off, and a firm in a price-driven market may not survive that gap.
  • Credibility: a CSR claim that is not verifiable is cheap to make, so the market discounts it — which is why third-party certification exists.
  • It depends on: how price-sensitive the firm's customers are, whether rivals face the same standards, and how visible the firm is.

7. Elasticity of Demand

7.1 Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

PED: A measure of the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. Always negative (inverse relationship) but we refer to the absolute value.
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
ValueTermMeaningDemand Curve Shape
|PED| > 1ElasticQd highly responsive — %ΔQd > %ΔPRelatively flat
|PED| < 1InelasticQd not very responsive — %ΔQd < %ΔPRelatively steep
|PED| = 1Unit elastic%ΔQd exactly equals %ΔPRectangular hyperbola
PED = 0Perfectly inelasticQd does not change at allVertical line
PED = ∞Perfectly elasticAny price rise → Qd falls to zeroHorizontal line

Determinants of PED

  • Substitutes: More substitutes = more elastic (consumers switch easily).
  • Necessity vs luxury: Necessities are inelastic; luxuries are elastic.
  • Proportion of income: Goods that take a large share of income are more elastic.
  • Time period: Demand is more elastic in the long run (time to adjust).
  • Addictiveness: Addictive goods (cigarettes, coffee) are inelastic.
  • Brand loyalty: Strong loyalty makes demand more inelastic.
  • Market definition: Broad markets (food) are more inelastic; narrow markets (organic avocados) more elastic.

PED and Total Revenue

  • Demand elastic (|PED| > 1): price cut → TR rises (quantity gain outweighs price loss).
  • Demand inelastic (|PED| < 1): price rise → TR rises (quantity loss is proportionally small).
  • TR is maximised where |PED| = 1.
Figure 7: Elastic demand (relatively flat) vs inelastic demand (relatively steep).
Case Study: UK Sugar Tax and PED
In April 2018, the UK introduced the Soft Drinks Industry Levy — 18p/litre for drinks with 5-8g of sugar per 100ml, and 24p/litre above 8g. The amount of sugar sold in soft drinks fell by roughly a third, mainly because many producers reformulated products to fall below the thresholds; household purchases of sugar from soft drinks fell by a smaller amount (around 10% in early studies). Consumers do have substitutes (diet versions, water), but most economists conclude the main impact was supply-side (reformulation) rather than a large demand response. In 2025 the government announced the levy will be extended to sugary milk-based drinks and the lower threshold cut to 4.5g per 100ml from 2028.

7.2 Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

YED: A measure of the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in consumer income.
YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income
ValueType of GoodExample
YED > 1Luxury / superior goodDesigner clothes, overseas holidays, organic food
0 < YED < 1NecessityBread, toothpaste, basic clothing
YED < 0Inferior goodBudget brands, bus travel, instant noodles

Significance of YED for Businesses

  • Luxury goods firms (YED > 1) are vulnerable to recessions — revenue falls sharply when incomes drop.
  • Necessity firms (0 < YED < 1) are relatively recession-resistant — demand is fairly stable.
  • Inferior goods firms (YED < 0) may benefit from recessions (e.g., Aldi and Lidl grew rapidly during 2008-09).
  • Businesses use YED to forecast future demand based on expected economic growth.

8. The Wider Economic Environment and the Firm

Case Study: What UK Firms Faced in 2026
The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% at its decision on 17 September 2026, the sixth consecutive hold since the cut to that level in December 2025, with CPI inflation running at 3.1% in August 2026 against the 2% target. For a firm, those two numbers pull in opposite directions. Borrowing costs have stopped rising, which makes an investment appraisal easier to sign off than it was during the tightening cycle. But prices are still climbing faster than target, so input costs keep moving and any fixed-price contract signed today carries margin risk. A firm reading the same data has to decide whether to lock in a supplier price now or wait — and that decision, not the headline rate, is what a 9EB0 answer should be about.
Evaluation: how much does a macro change actually matter to a given firm?
  • Size of the change relative to the firm's margin — a one-point rate rise is trivial to a debt-free firm and existential to a heavily geared one.
  • Time lag — monetary policy works with a delay, so the firm may have quarters to adjust; an exchange rate move hits the next invoice.
  • Ability to pass costs on — which depends on the price elasticity of demand for the firm's product and on what rivals do.
  • Offsetting effects — a weaker pound raises import costs and export competitiveness at the same time; the net effect depends on the firm's import content.
  • Hedging and contracts — firms that have fixed their energy price or bought currency forward feel the change later, not never.

9. The Price Mechanism

The price mechanism: The system by which changes in price act as signals to producers and consumers, provide incentives for economic agents to change behaviour, and ration scarce resources.

9.1 Three Functions of the Price Mechanism

Signalling

Prices convey information. A rising price signals high demand and encourages firms to allocate more resources to production. A falling price signals reduced demand and triggers reallocation of resources elsewhere. Prices coordinate the decentralised decisions of millions of agents.

Incentive

Prices create incentives for behavioural change. Higher prices incentivise producers to supply more (profit motive) and consumers to reduce consumption. Lower prices incentivise consumption and may push some firms out. This ensures resources flow to where they are most valued.

Rationing

The price mechanism rations scarce goods — those willing and able to pay receive the good; those who are not must go without. This allocates goods to those who value them most (in monetary terms), but raises equity concerns as it depends on ability to pay.

9.2 Adam Smith's "Invisible Hand"

Smith argued that individuals pursuing self-interest, guided by the price mechanism, produce an efficient resource allocation — as if by an "invisible hand." Without central planning, markets coordinate production and consumption to maximise welfare. However, this only holds when markets work well — market failures prevent efficient outcomes.

Evaluation: Limitations of the Price Mechanism
  • Leads to inequality — rationing by price means the poor cannot afford essentials.
  • Fails to account for externalities (e.g., pollution not reflected in prices).
  • Underprovides public goods (non-excludable, non-rivalrous).
  • Information failures mean prices may not reflect true costs and benefits.
  • Monopoly power distorts prices and restricts output.
  • Markets may adjust slowly — price signals take time to work through.

10. Understanding the Consumer (spec 1.3.5)

Spec 1.3.4 ends by asking how firms respond to a change in demand. Spec 1.3.5 asks how a firm finds out what demand looks like before it commits money to a product. On Economics B this is examined as economics, not as marketing coursework: market research is how a firm estimates the demand curve it faces, and the questions ask what the research is worth and where it misleads.

10.1 Primary and Secondary Research

Primary research: data the firm gathers itself, for its own question — questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, test markets, trials.
Secondary research: data already collected by somebody else and reused — ONS releases, trade association reports, competitors' published accounts, the firm's own past sales records, purchased market reports.
PrimarySecondary
Fits the questionExactly — the firm writes the questionsApproximately — it was collected for someone else's purpose

ExclusivityRivals do not have it, so it can be a source of competitive advantageRivals can read the same report

Cutting across that split is a second one the specification names separately:

  • Quantitative data — numbers that can be counted and compared: how many, how often, how much, at what price. It supports a demand forecast and a break-even calculation.
  • Qualitative data — reasons and attitudes: why consumers switched, what they think the brand stands for, what would make them pay more. It explains why the demand curve moved, which is what an analysis mark needs.
Case Study: research in a real 9EB0 question
The June 2023 Paper 1 opened on the UK music streaming market and asked candidates to explain one method the Competition and Markets Authority could use to find out how consumers had been affected by changes in the industry. The mark scheme credited primary methods (questionnaires, focus groups asking listeners directly about the services they use) and secondary methods (ONS data). The lesson for the exam is that a method alone earns the knowledge mark; the application and analysis marks come from saying what this organisation would learn from it in this market.

10.2 Limitations: Sample Size and Bias

Research produces an estimate, not a fact, and the specification asks for its limitations directly.

  • Sample size. A small sample gives a wide margin of error, so a difference the firm acts on may be noise. Doubling the sample does not halve the error — it cuts it by roughly a third — so accuracy gets expensive quickly, and the firm is trading research cost against the cost of getting the launch wrong.
  • Sampling bias. If the sample is not representative of the target population the result is wrong however large it is. Surveying shoppers on a weekday morning in one city centre reaches the retired and the non-working, not commuters.
  • Response bias. People say what sounds reasonable. Asked whether they would pay more for a product made to higher ethical standards, many say yes; far fewer do so at the till. Stated intention is not revealed preference.
  • Question design. A leading question produces the answer it contains. "How much do you like our new packaging?" has already assumed the answer.
  • It is backward-looking. Research describes the market as it is. In a genuinely new market there is nothing yet to survey, which is why entrepreneurs still have to bear risk (spec 1.2.1).

10.3 Categorising Consumers: Market Segmentation

Market segmentation: dividing a market into groups of consumers with similar characteristics and similar buying behaviour, so that the product, the price and the promotion can be aimed at one group rather than averaged across everybody.

The common bases are demographic (age, family stage, income, occupation), geographic (region, urban or rural), psychographic (attitudes, lifestyle, values) and behavioural (how often they buy, how loyal they are, what they use the product for).

The economics of it is straightforward: different segments have different price elasticities of demand. Spotify's student, individual, duo and family tiers are the same service sold at four prices because students have more elastic demand than working adults — exactly the reasoning the June 2023 mark scheme rewarded. Segmenting therefore lets a firm capture demand it would lose at a single price, and lets it design the product for a group instead of for an average consumer who does not exist.

Evaluation: the limits of segmentation
  • Cost. Several product versions mean shorter production runs, more stock lines and more marketing budgets, which pushes average cost up. The extra revenue has to beat that.
  • Segments must be reachable and distinct. If the cheap version leaks to the customers who would have paid full price, the firm has cut its own revenue.
  • Segments move. Tastes and incomes change, so a segmentation built on 2024 research can be aimed at a group that no longer behaves that way.
  • Fairness. A student railcard reads as fair; a price set from an individual's browsing history does not, and the difference is regulatory risk.

11. The Competition (spec 1.3.6)

11.1 Market Positioning and Market Mapping

Market positioning: where a product sits in the consumer's mind relative to rivals.
A market map plots the products in a market against two characteristics that matter to buyers — typically price against quality, but it could be traditional against modern, or basic against feature-rich.

A market map does two jobs. It shows where rivals cluster, which is where competition will be hardest and margins thinnest. And it shows gaps: a combination of characteristics nobody is currently offering. The UK grocery market mapped on price against range shows Aldi and Lidl at low price and narrow range, Waitrose and M&S at high price and high service, and the big four in the middle — and it was the low-price, narrow-range corner that was empty in the 1990s.

Exam Tip: a gap on a market map is not automatically an opportunity. The gap may be empty because there is no profitable demand there, or because the cost of serving it is too high. A good 8 or 10-mark answer makes exactly that point.

11.2 Competitive Advantage and Product Differentiation

Competitive advantage: whatever allows a firm to sell at a higher price, or at a lower cost, than its rivals, and to keep doing so. The two generic routes are cost leadership (produce more cheaply than anyone else and compete on price) and differentiation (make the product distinct enough that buyers will pay more for it).
Product differentiation: making a product different from rivals' — genuinely, through design, quality, technology or service, or in perception, through branding and advertising.

The economic effect of differentiation is the point the exam wants. Successful differentiation does two things to the demand curve the firm faces:

  • It shifts demand to the right — more is bought at every price.
  • It makes demand less price elastic, because the substitutes are now less close. The firm can raise price and lose proportionately less volume, so total revenue rises (see 7.1).

That is why differentiation and added value belong together: differentiation is what lets a firm charge a price well above the cost of its bought-in inputs, and the gap is the added value defined in 2.1.

11.3 How Firms Decide on Price and Output

Spec 1.3.6(e) asks how a firm actually decides. In practice the decision runs: what does the market map say rivals charge; what does research say our segment will pay; how elastic is our demand; what does a unit cost us; and what is our objective (1.1.2)? A firm chasing market share prices below the profit-maximising level; a firm chasing survival prices to keep cash coming in; a differentiated firm prices above the market because it can. The full menu of pricing strategies, and how a firm chooses between them, is developed in Topic 2.1 (spec 2.2.2), and the effect of market structure on that decision in Topic 4.1.

Potential market growth is what makes a market worth entering: a niche that is growing fast can support a firm that a static one cannot, and a market map gap in a shrinking market is not worth filling. Growth is judged from secondary data on volumes, values and household spending, which is exactly where market research (10.1) feeds back into the decision.

11.5 Stable and Dynamic Markets

Stable market: demand, technology, the set of competitors and the product itself change slowly. Examples: bread, cement, domestic water.
Dynamic market: conditions change rapidly — fast technological change, short product life cycles, frequent entry and exit, tastes that move. Examples: streaming, smartphones, fast fashion, food delivery.
For the firmStable marketDynamic market
ForecastingReliable; last year predicts next yearUnreliable; research dates quickly

The distinction matters because it changes what a firm should spend money on. In a stable market, research and development spending may never be recovered; in a dynamic one, refusing to spend it is how an incumbent is displaced — Schumpeter's creative destruction (2.1) is a description of a dynamic market. Topic 2.1 takes this further under spec 2.1.3 and 2.1.4.

Exam Tip — Evaluation: Edexcel B examiners reward evaluation highly. Always consider: (1) short run vs long run effects, (2) the significance of the change, (3) who gains and who loses, (4) the assumptions underlying your analysis, and (5) whether alternative policies might be more effective.
