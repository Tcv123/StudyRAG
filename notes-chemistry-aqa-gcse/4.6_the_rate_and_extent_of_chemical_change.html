4.6 The Rate and Extent of Chemical Change

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 2

Chemical reactions happen at very different speeds — an explosion is over in a fraction of a second, while rusting takes months. This topic explains how fast reactions go (the rate), what we can do to speed them up or slow them down, and why, using collision theory. The second half looks at how far reactions go (the extent): many reactions are reversible and reach a state of dynamic equilibrium, and at Higher tier you must predict how changing the conditions shifts the position of that equilibrium. These ideas are essential for industry, where chemists must make products quickly, cheaply and safely.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. This whole topic is common to GCSE Chemistry and Combined Science; there is no triple-only content in 4.6 (the Haber process in detail is in 4.10).

4.6.1 Rate of Reaction

4.6.1.1 Calculating Rates of Reactions

The rate of a chemical reaction can be found by measuring the quantity of a reactant used or the quantity of product formed over time:

mean rate of reaction = quantity of reactant used ÷ time taken

mean rate of reaction = quantity of product formed ÷ time taken

The quantity of reactant or product can be measured by the mass in grams or by a volume in cm³. The units of rate of reaction may be given as:

Quantity measuredUnit of quantityUnit of rate
Mass of reactant lost / product formedgg/s
Volume of gas producedcm³cm³/s
(HT) Amount of substancemolmol/s

Graphs of Reaction Progress

Plotting the quantity of product formed (or reactant used) against time gives a curve. You should be able to draw and interpret graphs showing quantity against time, and use the slope (gradient) of the graph as a measure of the rate of reaction:

  • The curve is steepest at the start — the rate is greatest because the concentration of reactants is highest.
  • The curve gets less steep as reactants are used up — the rate decreases.
  • The curve becomes horizontal (flat) when the reaction has finished — one reactant (the limiting reactant) has been used up completely.

Worked Example 1 — Mean Rate

Question: Magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid. 60 cm³ of
          hydrogen is collected in the first 30 s. A total of
          75 cm³ is collected after 120 s, when the reaction stops.
          (a) Calculate the mean rate over the first 30 s.
          (b) Calculate the mean rate over the whole reaction.

(a) mean rate = volume of product ÷ time
              = 60 ÷ 30
              = 2.0 cm³/s

(b) mean rate = 75 ÷ 120
              = 0.625 cm³/s ≈ 0.63 cm³/s

Note: the mean rate over the whole reaction is lower because
the reaction slows down as the reactants are used up.

Rate at a Specific Time — Tangents (HT)

Higher-tier students must be able to draw tangents to the curves on these graphs and use the slope of the tangent as a measure of the rate of reaction at a specific time.

  1. Place a ruler so that it just touches the curve at the chosen time without crossing it, with equal angles between the ruler and the curve on either side.
  2. Draw a long straight line (a longer tangent gives a more accurate gradient).
  3. Pick two points far apart on the tangent and read their coordinates.
  4. gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. This is the rate at that time.

Worked Example 2 — Gradient of a Tangent (HT)

Question: A tangent is drawn to the volume–time curve at t = 30 s.
          It passes through (10 s, 20 cm³) and (53 s, 62 cm³).
          Calculate the rate of reaction at 30 s.

gradient = Δy ÷ Δx
         = (62 − 20) ÷ (53 − 10)
         = 42 ÷ 43
         = 0.977 cm³/s

Answer:  ≈ 0.98 cm³/s

Worked Example 3 — Rate in mol/s (HT)

Question: In a reaction, 0.48 g of magnesium (Ar = 24) is used up
          in 40 s. Calculate the mean rate in mol/s.

moles of Mg = mass ÷ Ar = 0.48 ÷ 24 = 0.020 mol
mean rate   = 0.020 ÷ 40 = 0.00050 mol/s  (5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s)
If asked to calculate a rate "at" a particular time, you must draw a tangent — dividing the y-value by the x-value at that point gives the mean rate from time zero, not the rate at that instant. Show your tangent clearly on the graph and show the triangle you used: the examiner will award marks for a correctly placed tangent even if your reading is slightly off. Always give units (cm³/s, g/s, mol/s).

4.6.1.2 Factors Which Affect the Rates of Chemical Reactions

Factors which affect the rates of chemical reactions include:

  • the concentrations of reactants in solution;
  • the pressure of reacting gases;
  • the surface area of solid reactants;
  • the temperature;
  • the presence of catalysts.
When sketching a curve for a faster reaction, make it steeper at the start and level off sooner — but at the same height if the amount of limiting reactant is unchanged. Only changing the amount of the limiting reactant changes the final volume of product. A catalyst, temperature or surface-area change alters how fast you get there, not how much you get.

Required Practical 5 — Rates of Reaction

Aim: to investigate how changes in concentration affect the rates of reactions by a method involving measuring the volume of a gas produced and a method involving a change in colour or turbidity. This should be an investigation involving developing a hypothesis.

Method A — Turbidity: Sodium Thiosulfate and Hydrochloric Acid

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO₂(g) + S(s) + H₂O(l)
                                              ↑
                         yellow sulfur precipitate makes the solution cloudy
  1. Draw a black cross on a piece of white paper and place a conical flask on top.
  2. Measure 10 cm³ of sodium thiosulfate solution and 40 cm³ of water into the flask (for the most dilute run), using measuring cylinders.
  3. Add 10 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid, swirl, and start the stopwatch immediately.
  4. Look down through the flask from above. Stop the clock when the cross can no longer be seen.
  5. Repeat with different concentrations of thiosulfate (e.g. 20 cm³ thiosulfate + 30 cm³ water, 30 + 20, 40 + 10, 50 + 0), keeping the total volume the same.
  6. Repeat each concentration and calculate means. Calculate the rate as 1/time (s⁻¹) and plot against concentration.

Method B — Volume of Gas: Magnesium and Hydrochloric Acid

  1. Set up a conical flask with a bung and delivery tube connected to a gas syringe (or an upturned measuring cylinder filled with water in a trough).
  2. Measure 50 cm³ of hydrochloric acid of a known concentration into the flask.
  3. Add a measured 3 cm length of magnesium ribbon, immediately replace the bung and start the stopwatch.
  4. Record the volume of gas every 10 seconds until no more gas is produced.
  5. Repeat with different concentrations of acid. Plot volume against time for each, and compare initial gradients.

Safety: sulfur dioxide is produced in Method A, which is toxic and can trigger asthma — use a well-ventilated room and pour the mixture into a sodium carbonate solution afterwards. Hydrochloric acid is an irritant — wear eye protection. Hydrogen is flammable — keep away from flames.

In the thiosulfate experiment, the end point is subjective — different people judge "can't see the cross" differently. Improvements: use the same person each time, use the same cross, or replace the eye with a light sensor and data logger. Remember that rate = 1 ÷ time, so a shorter time means a faster rate.

4.6.1.3 Collision Theory and Activation Energy

Collision theory explains how various factors affect rates of reactions. According to this theory, chemical reactions can occur only when reacting particles collide with each other and with sufficient energy.

The minimum amount of energy that particles must have to react is called the activation energy.

Increasing the concentration of reactants in solution, the pressure of reacting gases, and the surface area of solid reactants increases the frequency of collisions and so increases the rate of reaction.

Increasing the temperature increases the frequency of collisions and makes the collisions more energetic, and so increases the rate of reaction.

A collision that has at least the activation energy and leads to a reaction is a successful collision. Most collisions are not successful — the particles simply bounce apart.

Factor increasedEffect on particlesEffect on rate
Concentration (solutions)More reacting particles in the same volume → particles closer together → more frequent collisionsIncreases
Pressure (gases)Same number of gas particles in a smaller volume → more frequent collisionsIncreases
Surface area (solids)Breaking a solid into smaller pieces exposes more particles on the surface → more frequent collisions with the other reactantIncreases
TemperatureParticles move faster → more frequent collisions; AND more particles have energy ≥ activation energy → a greater proportion of collisions are successfulIncreases (significantly)
CatalystProvides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy → more collisions are successfulIncreases

Surface Area to Volume Ratio

Cutting a solid into smaller pieces increases its surface area to volume ratio. You may be asked to calculate this:

Cube of side 2 cm:
  surface area = 6 × (2 × 2) = 24 cm²
  volume       = 2 × 2 × 2   = 8 cm³
  SA : V       = 24 : 8 = 3 : 1

Same cube cut into eight 1 cm cubes:
  surface area = 8 × 6 × (1 × 1) = 48 cm²
  volume       = 8 × 1           = 8 cm³
  SA : V       = 48 : 8 = 6 : 1   → doubled

More surface exposed → more frequent collisions → faster rate.
The most common lost mark in rate explanations is writing "more collisions" instead of "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second". Over the whole reaction the total number of collisions is not the point — it is how often they happen. For temperature, you must give both reasons: particles collide more often and a greater proportion of collisions have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy. The second reason is the more important one.

Temperature and the Energy Distribution of Particles

At any temperature, particles have a spread of energies. Raising the temperature shifts the distribution so that many more particles have energy greater than the activation energy. This is why a rise of only about 10 °C can roughly double the rate of many reactions.

4.6.1.4 Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that changes the rate of a chemical reaction (usually increases it) but is not used up during the reaction. Different reactions need different catalysts.

Catalysts increase the rate of reaction by providing a different pathway for the reaction that has a lower activation energy.

Enzymes act as catalysts in biological systems.

Because the catalyst is not used up, it is not included in the chemical equation for the reaction (though it may be written above the arrow). The same mass of catalyst remains at the end and can be reused. Only a small amount is needed.

CatalystReaction catalysed
Manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
IronHaber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
Vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅Contact process (making sulfuric acid): 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃
Platinum / rhodium / palladiumCatalytic converters in car exhausts
Copper(II) sulfateSpeeds up zinc + sulfuric acid
Enzymes (e.g. amylase, catalase)Digestion of starch; breakdown of hydrogen peroxide in cells; fermentation (yeast enzymes)

You should be able to identify catalysts in reactions from their effect on the rate of reaction and because they are not included in the chemical equation for the reaction. For example, if adding a little copper(II) sulfate makes zinc react much faster with acid and the copper(II) sulfate is still present at the end, it is acting as a catalyst.

Industrial catalysts are valuable because they allow reactions to proceed quickly at lower temperatures, which saves energy and money and reduces CO₂ emissions from burning fuels. They are often used as fine powders or meshes to maximise their surface area.

"Explain how a catalyst increases the rate" — you must say it provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. Saying "it lowers the activation energy" alone may be accepted, but saying "it gives particles more energy" or "it increases the number of collisions" is wrong. A catalyst does not change the energy of the particles; it lowers the energy barrier so that more of the existing collisions are successful.

4.6.2 Reversible Reactions and Dynamic Equilibrium

4.6.2.1 Reversible Reactions

In some chemical reactions, the products of the reaction can react to produce the original reactants. Such reactions are called reversible reactions and are represented with the symbol ⇌ (two half-arrows):

A + B ⇌ C + D

The direction of reversible reactions can be changed by changing the conditions.

The classic example is the thermal decomposition of ammonium chloride:

                       heat
ammonium chloride   ⇌   ammonia + hydrogen chloride
                       cool
NH₄Cl(s)            ⇌   NH₃(g)  + HCl(g)

On heating in a test tube, white solid NH₄Cl decomposes into two
gases. These gases recombine on the cooler upper part of the tube,
forming white NH₄Cl solid again.

4.6.2.2 Energy Changes and Reversible Reactions

If a reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction, it is endothermic in the opposite direction. The same amount of energy is transferred in each case.

For example, the dehydration of hydrated copper(II) sulfate:

                                     endothermic (heat)
hydrated copper(II) sulfate    ⇌    anhydrous copper(II) sulfate + water
      (blue)                         exothermic       (white)
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)                  ⇌    CuSO₄(s)       + 5H₂O(l)

• Heating blue crystals drives off water → white powder (energy taken in).
• Adding water to the white powder turns it blue again and the
  mixture gets HOT (the same amount of energy is given out).
• This is used as a TEST FOR WATER: white anhydrous CuSO₄ turns blue.

You should be able to describe this reaction and explain why the same amount of energy is involved in each direction: the forward and backward reactions involve breaking and forming exactly the same bonds, just the other way round.

4.6.2.3 Equilibrium

When a reversible reaction occurs in apparatus which prevents the escape of reactants and products (a closed system), equilibrium is reached when the forward and reverse reactions occur at exactly the same rate.

At equilibrium, the concentrations of all the reacting substances remain constant (but are not necessarily equal).

This is called dynamic equilibrium because both reactions are still happening — reactants are constantly being turned into products and products back into reactants — but because the rates are equal, there is no overall change.

Two classic errors: (1) "at equilibrium the reaction stops" — wrong, both reactions continue at equal rates; (2) "at equilibrium the amounts of reactants and products are equal" — wrong, the concentrations are constant, but they can be very different from each other. Also remember that equilibrium can only be reached in a closed system, where nothing can enter or leave.

4.6.2.4 The Effect of Changing Conditions on Equilibrium (HT)

The relative amounts of all the reactants and products at equilibrium depend on the conditions of the reaction.

If a system is at equilibrium and a change is made to any of the conditions, then the system responds to counteract the change. This is Le Chatelier's Principle.

The effects of changing conditions on a system at equilibrium can be predicted using Le Chatelier's Principle. When the system "counteracts the change", the position of equilibrium shifts:

  • Shifts to the right (forwards) → more products are formed.
  • Shifts to the left (backwards) → more reactants are formed.

A new equilibrium is then established with different relative amounts of each substance.

4.6.2.5 The Effect of Changing Concentration (HT)

If the concentration of one of the reactants or products is changed, the system is no longer at equilibrium and the concentrations of all the substances will change until equilibrium is reached again.

If the concentration of a reactant is increased, more products will be formed until equilibrium is reached again.
If the concentration of a product is decreased, more reactants will react until equilibrium is reached again.
ChangeShift in position of equilibriumResult
Increase concentration of a reactantRight (forwards) — uses up the added reactantMore product
Decrease concentration of a reactantLeft (backwards) — replaces the reactantLess product
Increase concentration of a productLeft (backwards) — uses up the added productMore reactant
Decrease concentration of a product (e.g. remove it)Right (forwards) — replaces the productMore product

A good illustration in school uses the equilibrium between yellow chromate ions and orange dichromate ions:

2CrO₄²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq)  ⇌  Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)
  yellow                      orange

Add acid (more H⁺)        → equilibrium shifts RIGHT → turns orange
Add alkali (removes H⁺)   → equilibrium shifts LEFT  → turns yellow

4.6.2.6 The Effect of Temperature Changes on Equilibrium (HT)

If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is increased: the relative amount of products at equilibrium increases for an endothermic reaction, and the relative amount of products at equilibrium decreases for an exothermic reaction.

If the temperature of a system at equilibrium is decreased: the relative amount of products at equilibrium increases for an exothermic reaction, and the relative amount of products at equilibrium decreases for an endothermic reaction.

Put simply: increasing the temperature favours the endothermic direction (which takes in the extra energy, counteracting the change); decreasing the temperature favours the exothermic direction (which releases energy).

Example: 2NO₂(g)  ⇌  N₂O₄(g)        forward reaction is EXOTHERMIC
         brown        colourless

• Heat the sealed tube   → favours endothermic (backward) direction
                         → equilibrium shifts LEFT → more NO₂ → DARKER brown
• Cool in iced water     → favours exothermic (forward) direction
                         → equilibrium shifts RIGHT → more N₂O₄ → PALER

4.6.2.7 The Effect of Pressure Changes on Equilibrium (HT)

For gaseous reactions at equilibrium:
An increase in pressure causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the smaller number of molecules, as shown by the symbol equation for that reaction.
A decrease in pressure causes the equilibrium position to shift towards the side with the larger number of molecules.

Count only the molecules of gases, using the balancing numbers in the equation. If both sides have the same number of gas molecules, changing the pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium (though it still changes the rate).

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g)  ⇌  2NH₃(g)
 1    +   3     =  4 molecules on left;  2 molecules on right

Increase pressure → shifts RIGHT (fewer molecules) → MORE ammonia
Decrease pressure → shifts LEFT  (more molecules)  → LESS ammonia

H₂(g) + I₂(g)  ⇌  2HI(g)
 2 molecules   =   2 molecules  → pressure has NO effect on position

Catalysts and Equilibrium

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally. It therefore has no effect on the position of equilibrium or on the yield — but it allows equilibrium to be reached more quickly. This is very valuable industrially because it saves time.

Worked Example 4 — Predicting Equilibrium Shifts (HT)

Question: Sulfur trioxide is made in the Contact process:
          2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)     ΔH = −196 kJ/mol
          Predict and explain the effect on the yield of SO₃ of
          (a) increasing the pressure, (b) increasing the temperature.

(a) Left side: 2 + 1 = 3 gas molecules.  Right side: 2.
    Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side
    with fewer molecules → to the RIGHT → yield of SO₃ INCREASES.

(b) The forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH negative).
    Increasing temperature favours the endothermic (reverse)
    reaction → equilibrium shifts LEFT → yield of SO₃ DECREASES.

Compromise Conditions in Industry

Conditions that give the best yield are not always the best overall. For an exothermic reaction such as the Haber process:

ConditionEffect on yieldEffect on rateCompromise chosen
TemperatureLow temperature gives a higher yield (exothermic forward reaction)Low temperature makes the rate too slowAbout 450 °C — a reasonable yield at an acceptable rate
PressureHigh pressure gives a higher yield (fewer gas molecules on right)High pressure also increases rateAbout 200 atm — higher pressures are too expensive and dangerous (thicker pipes, more energy for compressors)
CatalystNo effect on yieldIncreases rateIron catalyst used

Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled, and ammonia is removed by cooling it until it liquefies — removing the product also helps pull the equilibrium to the right. Full details of the Haber process are covered in 4.10 (Using Resources).

For any Le Chatelier question, use a three-part answer: (1) identify the relevant feature of the equation ("the forward reaction is exothermic" or "there are 4 molecules of gas on the left and 2 on the right"); (2) state the shift ("the position of equilibrium moves to the right"); (3) state the effect on the yield ("so more ammonia is produced"). Missing step (1) often loses the first mark. Never say that pressure affects reactions with no gases, or that a catalyst changes the yield.
