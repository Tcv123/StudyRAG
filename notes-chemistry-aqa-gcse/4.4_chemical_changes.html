4.4 Chemical Changes

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 1

This topic is about three big families of reactions that turn up again and again across the whole course: the reactions of metals (and how we get metals out of their ores), the reactions of acids (and how we make salts and measure concentrations), and electrolysis (using electricity to split ionic compounds). A single idea links them all — oxidation and reduction. Once you can see a reaction in terms of which species loses electrons and which gains them, most of this topic falls into place.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. Content marked (Triple) is only examined in GCSE Chemistry (8462), not in Combined Science.

4.4.1 Reactivity of Metals

4.4.1.1 Metal Oxides — Oxidation and Reduction (Oxygen Definition)

Metals react with oxygen to produce metal oxides. Because oxygen is being added to the metal, these reactions are oxidation reactions.

magnesium + oxygen  →  magnesium oxide        2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
iron      + oxygen  →  iron(III) oxide        4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Fe₂O₃(s)
copper    + oxygen  →  copper(II) oxide       2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s)
Oxidation is the gain of oxygen by a substance.
Reduction is the loss of oxygen from a substance.

Oxidation and reduction always happen together — if one substance gains oxygen, another must have lost it. A reaction in which both happen is called a redox reaction.

For example, when copper(II) oxide is heated with carbon, the copper oxide loses oxygen (it is reduced) and the carbon gains oxygen (it is oxidised):

2CuO(s) + C(s)  →  2Cu(s) + CO₂(g)

  CuO → Cu     loses oxygen  →  REDUCED
  C   → CO₂    gains oxygen  →  OXIDISED

The substance that does the reducing (here, carbon) is called the reducing agent; the substance that provides the oxygen (copper oxide) is the oxidising agent. You do not need these terms for AQA, but they make extended answers clearer.

At GCSE you meet two definitions of oxidation and reduction. At Foundation level you only need the oxygen definition (gain/loss of oxygen). At Higher tier you also need the electron definition (4.4.1.4). Read the question carefully — if it says "in terms of electrons", an answer about oxygen will score zero.

4.4.1.2 The Reactivity Series

When metals react with other substances, the metal atoms lose electrons to form positive ions. The reactivity of a metal is related to its tendency to form positive ions: the more easily a metal loses its outer electrons, the more reactive it is. This is why Group 1 metals (one outer electron, easily lost) are so much more reactive than transition metals such as copper.

Metals can be arranged in order of their reactivity, from their reactions with water and with dilute acids. The non-metals carbon and hydrogen are included in the series as reference points, because they are useful for predicting extraction methods and acid reactions.

Reactions of Metals with Water

Reactive metals react with cold water to form a metal hydroxide and hydrogen:

metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)
Ca(s)  + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Less reactive metals such as magnesium react extremely slowly with cold water but react with steam to form the metal oxide and hydrogen: Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g).

"Hydrogen is produced" is not an observation — you cannot see a gas's identity. The test for hydrogen is a lit splint held at the mouth of the tube, which goes out with a squeaky pop.

Displacement Reactions

A displacement reaction is one in which a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in a compound. The more reactive metal ends up as ions in the compound; the less reactive metal is released as the element. 
magnesium + copper(II) sulfate → magnesium sulfate + copper
Mg(s)     + CuSO₄(aq)          → MgSO₄(aq)         + Cu(s)

Observations: blue solution fades to colourless; a pink-brown
coating of copper forms on the magnesium; the mixture warms up.

copper + magnesium sulfate → NO REACTION
(copper is less reactive than magnesium, so it cannot displace it)

Displacement experiments are used to deduce an order of reactivity. Each metal is placed in solutions of the salts of the other metals, and a reaction is recorded as a tick. The metal that displaces the most others is the most reactive. Displacement reactions are also exothermic — the bigger the gap in reactivity between the two metals, the bigger the temperature rise.

4.4.1.3 Extraction of Metals and Reduction

Unreactive metals such as gold (and some silver and platinum) are found in the Earth's crust as the uncombined element — they are said to occur "native". They can be separated from the surrounding rock by physical methods such as panning. Most metals, however, are found as compounds (usually oxides, sulfides or carbonates) in rocks called ores. A chemical reaction is needed to extract the metal.

An ore is a rock that contains enough of a metal compound to make it economically worthwhile to extract the metal.

Metals less reactive than carbon can be extracted from their oxides by reduction with carbon: the carbon removes the oxygen from the metal oxide.
Metals more reactive than carbon must be extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound, which is much more expensive because of the large amount of electricity needed. 
Extraction of iron in a blast furnace (simplified)

  iron(III) oxide + carbon → iron + carbon dioxide
  2Fe₂O₃(s)       + 3C(s)  → 4Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g)

  In practice the main reducing agent is carbon monoxide:
  Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g)

  Fe₂O₃ → Fe   loses oxygen → reduced
  C / CO → CO₂ gains oxygen → oxidised

Extraction of zinc
  2ZnO(s) + C(s) → 2Zn(s) + CO₂(g)

The question "Explain why aluminium cannot be extracted by heating its oxide with carbon" needs the comparison: "Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot remove the oxygen from (reduce) aluminium oxide." Just saying "aluminium is very reactive" is not enough — you must compare it with carbon.

4.4.1.4 Oxidation and Reduction in Terms of Electrons (HT)

Oxidation is the loss of electrons.
Reduction is the gain of electrons.

Remember: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Higher-tier students must be able to write ionic equations for displacement reactions and identify in a symbol equation which species are oxidised and which are reduced. The trick is to remove spectator ions — ions that are present on both sides unchanged and take no part in the reaction.

Full equation:   Mg(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Split into ions: Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + Cu(s)

SO₄²⁻ appears unchanged on both sides → spectator ion → cancel it.

Ionic equation:  Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)

Half equations:
  Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻         Mg LOSES electrons  → OXIDISED
  Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu         Cu²⁺ GAINS electrons → REDUCED

Worked Example 1 — Ionic Equation and Half Equations (HT)

Question: Zinc reacts with iron(II) sulfate solution.
          (a) Write the ionic equation.
          (b) Write half equations and identify the species
              oxidised and reduced.

(a) Zn(s) + FeSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Fe(s)
    SO₄²⁻ is a spectator ion.
    Ionic: Zn(s) + Fe²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Fe(s)

(b) Zn  → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻    zinc atoms lose electrons: OXIDISED
    Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe     iron(II) ions gain electrons: REDUCED

Check: charges balance on both sides of each half equation
       (0 = +2 − 2 and +2 − 2 = 0).
In half equations, electrons are written as e⁻. Always check that the total charge on each side is the same — this is the quickest way to spot a missing electron. You may also see oxidation written as Mg − 2e⁻ → Mg²⁺; AQA accepts this form, but adding electrons to the right-hand side is clearer.

4.4.2 Reactions of Acids

4.4.2.1 Reactions of Acids with Metals

Acids react with metals that are above hydrogen in the reactivity series to produce a salt and hydrogen:

metal + acid → salt + hydrogen 
magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen
Mg(s)     + 2HCl(aq)          → MgCl₂(aq)          + H₂(g)

zinc + sulfuric acid → zinc sulfate + hydrogen
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq)    → ZnSO₄(aq)    + H₂(g)

iron + hydrochloric acid → iron(II) chloride + hydrogen
Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq)         → FeCl₂(aq)         + H₂(g)

Knowledge of reactions limited to magnesium, zinc and iron with hydrochloric and sulfuric acids is expected. Copper does not react because it is below hydrogen.

Acid–Metal Reactions as Redox (HT)

Higher-tier students should be able to explain in terms of gain or loss of electrons that these are redox reactions, and identify which species are oxidised and which are reduced.

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Ions:   Mg + 2H⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ + H₂
Cl⁻ is a spectator ion.

Ionic:  Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g)

Mg   → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻     magnesium is OXIDISED (loses electrons)
2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂        hydrogen ions are REDUCED (gain electrons)

4.4.2.2 Neutralisation of Acids and Salt Production

Acids are neutralised by alkalis (soluble metal hydroxides) and bases (insoluble metal hydroxides and metal oxides) to produce a salt and water, and by metal carbonates to produce a salt, water and carbon dioxide.

A base is a substance that neutralises an acid to form a salt and water (e.g. metal oxides and metal hydroxides).
An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, producing hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq) (e.g. sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide).
A salt is the compound formed when the hydrogen in an acid is replaced by a metal (or by the ammonium ion).

The test for carbon dioxide is to bubble the gas through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution), which turns milky / cloudy. If a question describes "fizzing" when an acid reacts with a solid that is not a metal, the solid is almost certainly a carbonate and the gas is CO₂.

4.4.2.3 Soluble Salts

Soluble salts can be made from acids by reacting them with solid insoluble substances such as metals, metal oxides, hydroxides or carbonates. The solid is added to the acid until no more reacts (the solid is in excess). The excess solid is filtered off to leave a solution of the salt only. The salt solution is then crystallised to produce solid salt.

Required Practical 1 — Preparing a Pure, Dry Sample of a Soluble Salt

Aim: to prepare a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt (e.g. copper(II) sulfate) from an insoluble oxide or carbonate (e.g. copper(II) oxide), using a Bunsen burner to heat dilute acid and a water bath or electric heater to evaporate the solution.

CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
black     colourless     blue

Crystals: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue, hydrated copper(II) sulfate)
  1. Measure about 40 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker using a measuring cylinder.
  2. Warm the acid gently with a Bunsen burner (do not boil) — this speeds up the reaction.
  3. Add copper(II) oxide a spatula at a time, stirring with a glass rod, until no more dissolves and some black solid remains. This shows the copper oxide is in excess, so all the acid has been used up.
  4. Filter the warm mixture through filter paper in a funnel into an evaporating basin, to remove the unreacted copper oxide. The filtrate is a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate.
  5. Heat the solution gently over a water bath (or with an electric heater) to evaporate some of the water until the crystallisation point is reached — when a drop placed on a cold glass rod forms crystals, or crystals start to form at the edge of the solution. Do not boil to dryness.
  6. Leave the solution in a cool place to crystallise slowly (giving larger crystals).
  7. Pour off any remaining liquid, then pat the crystals dry between sheets of filter paper (or leave them in a warm place / desiccator).
StepReason
Warm the acidIncreases the rate of reaction so the base dissolves faster.
Add base in excessEnsures all the acid reacts, so the final salt is not contaminated with acid.
FilterRemoves the unreacted (insoluble) excess base.
Evaporate only to the crystallisation pointBoiling to dryness can decompose the salt, drive off water of crystallisation (giving white anhydrous powder), and causes spitting.
Cool slowlyProduces larger, purer crystals.

Hazards: sulfuric acid is an irritant/corrosive — wear eye protection. Copper(II) oxide and copper(II) sulfate are harmful and dangerous to the environment — do not pour down the sink. Hot apparatus can burn — allow to cool before handling.

Six-mark method questions on this practical are marked on a logical sequence: warm acid → add solid until in excess → filter → heat filtrate to crystallisation point → leave to cool → dry crystals. Name the specific apparatus (filter funnel and paper, evaporating basin, water bath) and give the reason for adding the solid in excess. Students often lose marks by evaporating "until all the water has gone".

4.4.2.4 The pH Scale and Neutralisation

Acids produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), in aqueous solutions.
Aqueous solutions of alkalis contain hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq).

The pH scale, from 0 to 14, is a measure of the acidity or alkalinity of a solution. A solution with pH 7 is neutral; pH less than 7 is acidic; pH greater than 7 is alkaline.

pH can be measured using universal indicator (which gives a colour that is compared with a chart) or a pH probe / pH meter (which gives a numerical reading, often to 1 or 2 decimal places, and is more accurate and less subjective).

In neutralisation reactions between an acid and an alkali, hydrogen ions react with hydroxide ions to produce water. This is the ionic equation for every acid–alkali neutralisation:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

The metal ion from the alkali and the non-metal ion from the acid are spectator ions; they remain in solution as the salt.

You should be able to describe the use of universal indicator or a wide-range indicator to measure the approximate pH of a solution, and to use the pH scale to identify acidic or alkaline solutions. In a neutralisation curve, adding alkali to acid causes the pH to rise slowly at first, then very sharply around pH 7 (the end point), then level off.

4.4.2.5 Titrations (Triple)

The volumes of acid and alkali solutions that react with each other can be measured by titration using a suitable indicator. If the concentration of one solution is known, the concentration of the other can be calculated.

A titration is a technique used to find the exact volume of one solution that reacts completely with a known volume of another solution. The point at which the reaction is just complete is called the end point, shown by a sharp colour change of the indicator.

Required Practical 2 — Titration (Triple)

Aim: to determine the reacting volumes of solutions of a strong acid and a strong alkali by titration, and (HT) to determine the concentration of one of the solutions in mol/dm³ and g/dm³ from the reacting volumes and the known concentration of the other solution.

  1. Rinse a 25.0 cm³ volumetric pipette with the alkali, then use it (with a pipette filler) to transfer exactly 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution into a clean conical flask.
  2. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator — e.g. phenolphthalein (pink in alkali, colourless in acid) or methyl orange (yellow in alkali, red in acid).
  3. Rinse a burette with the acid, then fill it with acid using a small funnel. Remove the funnel. Run a little acid through the tap to fill the jet. Record the initial reading to the nearest 0.05 cm³, reading from the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  4. Place the flask on a white tile (so the colour change is easy to see). Add acid from the burette, swirling the flask constantly.
  5. As the end point approaches (the colour lingers longer where the acid enters), add the acid dropwise until the indicator changes colour permanently. Record the final burette reading.
  6. The first run is a rough titration. Repeat accurately until you have concordant results — titres within 0.10 cm³ of each other.
  7. Calculate the mean titre using only the concordant results.
 RoughRun 1Run 2Run 3
Final reading / cm³23.2045.6522.5045.00
Initial reading / cm³0.0023.200.0022.50
Titre / cm³23.2022.4522.5022.50

Titration Calculations (HT, Triple)

moles (mol) = concentration (mol/dm³) × volume (dm³)

concentration (g/dm³) = concentration (mol/dm³) × Mr

To convert cm³ to dm³, divide by 1000 (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³).

The four-step method for any titration calculation:

  1. Calculate the moles of the substance whose concentration and volume you know.
  2. Use the balanced equation (mole ratio) to find the moles of the other substance.
  3. Calculate the concentration of the unknown in mol/dm³ = moles ÷ volume in dm³.
  4. If asked, convert to g/dm³ by multiplying by Mr.

Question: 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution was neutralised by

Question: 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide was neutralised

The most common error in titration calculations is forgetting the mole ratio. Sulfuric acid is diprotic — one H₂SO₄ releases two H⁺ ions and reacts with two NaOH. Always write the balanced equation first. The second most common error is forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³.

4.4.2.6 Strong and Weak Acids (HT)

A strong acid is completely ionised in aqueous solution. Examples: hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids.
A weak acid is only partially ionised in aqueous solution. Examples: ethanoic, citric and carbonic acids. 
Strong:  HCl(aq)          → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)            (every molecule ionises)
Weak:    CH₃COOH(aq)      ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)        (only a small fraction ionises;
                                                          reversible reaction)

Strength vs Concentration

"Strong/weak" and "concentrated/dilute" describe two completely different things:

TermMeaningExample
Strong / weakThe proportion of acid molecules that ionise in water.HCl is strong at any concentration; ethanoic acid is weak at any concentration.
Concentrated / diluteThe amount of acid (in moles or grams) dissolved in a given volume of solution.2.0 mol/dm³ is more concentrated than 0.1 mol/dm³.

So it is perfectly possible to have a dilute solution of a strong acid (e.g. 0.01 mol/dm³ HCl) or a concentrated solution of a weak acid (e.g. 5 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid).

For a given concentration of aqueous solutions, the stronger the acid, the lower the pH.

If a question asks you to explain why two acids of the same concentration have different pH values, the answer must mention ionisation: "Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid and is fully ionised, whereas ethanoic acid is a weak acid and is only partially ionised. The same idea explains why a weak acid reacts more slowly with magnesium than a strong acid of the same concentration.

4.4.3 Electrolysis

4.4.3.1 The Process of Electrolysis

When an ionic compound is melted or dissolved in water, the ions are free to move about within the liquid or solution. These liquids and solutions are able to conduct electricity and are called electrolytes. In the solid state, the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct.

Electrolysis is the process of using electricity to decompose an ionic compound (the electrolyte) into its elements, by passing a direct current through it when molten or in solution.

Cathode = the negative electrode. Positive ions (cations) move towards it.
Anode = the positive electrode. Negative ions (anions) move towards it.

At the electrodes, the ions are discharged — they gain or lose electrons to become neutral atoms or molecules:

  • At the cathode, positive ions gain electrons — this is reduction.
  • At the anode, negative ions lose electrons — this is oxidation.

Electrodes are usually made of an inert material such as graphite or platinum, so they conduct electricity but do not take part in the reaction.

Use the memory aid PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode. And OIL RIG links to it: oxidation at the anode (An Ox), reduction at the cathode (Red Cat). The electrons flow round the external circuit from the anode to the cathode — never through the electrolyte, where the current is carried by moving ions.

4.4.3.2 Electrolysis of Molten Ionic Compounds

When a simple ionic compound (e.g. lead bromide) is electrolysed in the molten state using inert electrodes, the metal (lead) is produced at the cathode and the non-metal (bromine) is produced at the anode. You should be able to predict the products of electrolysis of any binary ionic compound in the molten state.

4.4.3.3 Using Electrolysis to Extract Metals

Metals can be extracted from molten compounds using electrolysis. Electrolysis is used if the metal is too reactive to be extracted by reduction with carbon, or if the metal reacts with carbon. Large amounts of energy are used in the extraction process to melt the compounds and to produce the electrical current, which makes it expensive.

Aluminium is manufactured by the electrolysis of a molten mixture of aluminium oxide (from the ore bauxite) and cryolite, using carbon as the positive electrode (anode).

FeatureExplanation
Why electrolysis?Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so its oxide cannot be reduced by carbon.
Why cryolite?Pure aluminium oxide has a very high melting point (over 2000 °C). Dissolving it in molten cryolite lowers the operating temperature to about 950 °C, saving energy and reducing costs. The molten mixture also conducts better.
Cathode reactionAl³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (reduction) — molten aluminium sinks and is tapped off.
Anode reaction2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (oxidation).
Why must the anode be replaced?At the high temperature, the oxygen produced reacts with the carbon anode to form carbon dioxide (C + O₂ → CO₂). The anodes gradually burn away and must be replaced regularly.
Overall reaction2Al₂O₃(l) → 4Al(l) + 3O₂(g)
"Explain why the positive electrodes must be replaced" is a classic 3-mark question: (1) oxygen is produced at the positive electrode; (2) the oxygen reacts with the carbon; (3) forming carbon dioxide, so the electrode is used up. Also be ready to explain why aluminium is expensive: lots of electricity is needed both to keep the electrolyte molten and to provide the current.

4.4.3.4 Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

An aqueous solution contains the ions from the dissolved compound and a small number of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions from the partial ionisation of water: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. The ions discharged at each electrode depend on the relative reactivity of the elements involved.

At the negative electrode (cathode): hydrogen is produced if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen. Otherwise, the metal is produced (e.g. copper, silver).

At the positive electrode (anode): oxygen is produced unless the solution contains halide ions (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻), in which case the halogen is produced. With sulfate, nitrate or carbonate solutions, oxygen is formed from hydroxide ions.

The electrolysis of brine (concentrated sodium chloride solution) is a major industrial process. All three products are useful: chlorine (for bleach, PVC and water treatment), hydrogen (for making ammonia and margarine, and as a fuel) and sodium hydroxide (for soap, paper and bleach manufacture).

Required Practical 3 — Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

Aim: to investigate what happens when aqueous solutions are electrolysed using inert electrodes. This should be an investigation involving developing a hypothesis.

  1. Pour about 50 cm³ of the solution (e.g. copper(II) sulfate, sodium chloride) into a beaker.
  2. Place a lid (e.g. a Petri dish with two holes) on the beaker and push two graphite rod electrodes through the holes so that they do not touch each other.
  3. Attach the electrodes to a low-voltage DC power supply (4–6 V) using crocodile clips and leads.
  4. Turn on the supply and observe each electrode: is a gas produced, or a solid deposited?
  5. Identify any gas using the appropriate test. Gases can be collected in small test tubes filled with the solution and inverted over the electrodes.
  6. Repeat with other solutions and compare the products with your predictions.

In theory, equal volumes of hydrogen and chlorine are produced from brine. In practice, the volume of chlorine collected is less than hydrogen because chlorine is slightly soluble in water (and reacts with it). In the electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid, the volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen — 4 electrons release 2 H₂ but only 1 O₂.

4.4.3.5 Representation of Reactions at Electrodes as Half Equations (HT)

During electrolysis, at the cathode (negative electrode), positively charged ions gain electrons and so the reactions are reductions. At the anode (positive electrode), negatively charged ions lose electrons and so the reactions are oxidations. These reactions can be represented by half equations.

Worked Example 5 — Writing Half Equations (HT)

Question: Aqueous copper(II) chloride is electrolysed with inert
          electrodes. Write half equations for the reactions at
          each electrode and state which is oxidation.

Ions present:  Cu²⁺, Cl⁻ (from CuCl₂);  H⁺, OH⁻ (from water)

Cathode (−):  copper is less reactive than hydrogen → copper forms
              Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu            (gain of e⁻ = REDUCTION)

Anode (+):    halide present → chlorine forms
              2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻           (loss of e⁻ = OXIDATION)

Check balance: each half equation has equal atoms and equal
               charge on both sides (e.g. 2 × −1 = −2 = 0 − 2).

Worked Example 6 — Balancing the Hydroxide Half Equation (HT)

Start:    OH⁻ → O₂            need 2 O on the left... but H must go somewhere

Step 1:   4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O    atoms: 4 O and 4 H each side ✓
Step 2:   charge left = −4,  charge right = 0
          add 4 electrons to the right to balance charge
Result:   4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻

Alternative form accepted by AQA:  4OH⁻ − 4e⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O
When asked "why is the reaction at the cathode a reduction?" the answer is "because the positive ions gain electrons". Do not say "because the ion gains oxygen" or "because it is at the negative electrode". The electron definition is the one rewarded in electrolysis questions.
