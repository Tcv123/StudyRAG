4.5 Energy Changes

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 1

Every chemical reaction involves an energy change. Bonds in the reactants must be broken and new bonds in the products must be made, and the balance between these two processes decides whether a reaction gives out energy to the surroundings or takes it in. This topic covers how we recognise and measure these energy changes, how we show them on reaction profiles, how we calculate them from bond energies (HT), and how chemical reactions are used to generate electricity in cells, batteries and fuel cells (Triple).

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. Content marked (Triple) is only examined in GCSE Chemistry (8462), not in Combined Science.

4.5.1 Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

4.5.1.1 Energy Transfer During Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Energy is conserved in chemical reactions. The amount of energy in the universe at the end of a chemical reaction is the same as before the reaction takes place. If a reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, the product molecules must have less energy than the reactants, by the amount transferred.

To talk about energy changes clearly we separate the system (the reacting chemicals) from the surroundings (everything else — the solvent water, the test tube, the thermometer, the air). A thermometer dipped into a reaction mixture measures the temperature of the surroundings, so it tells us which way energy is flowing.

An exothermic reaction is one that transfers energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases.

An endothermic reaction is one that takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases.

Required Practical 4 — Temperature Changes

Aim: to investigate the variables that affect temperature changes in reacting solutions, such as acid plus metals, acid plus carbonates, neutralisations, and displacement of metals.

A typical version: investigate how the volume of sodium hydroxide added to a fixed volume of hydrochloric acid affects the temperature change.

  1. Using a measuring cylinder, put 30 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid into a polystyrene cup. Stand the cup inside a beaker so it does not fall over.
  2. Measure and record the initial temperature of the acid with a thermometer.
  3. Measure 5 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution and add it to the cup. Fit a lid with a hole for the thermometer.
  4. Stir the solution with the thermometer and record the highest temperature reached.
  5. Repeat, adding further 5 cm³ portions of NaOH, recording the highest temperature after each addition, up to about 40 cm³ in total.
  6. Repeat the whole experiment to get a second set of results and calculate mean temperatures. Plot a graph of highest temperature against volume of NaOH added.
VariableIn this investigation
IndependentVolume of sodium hydroxide added (or: type of metal, concentration of acid, mass of solid…)
DependentHighest temperature reached / temperature change
ControlVolume and concentration of acid; concentration of alkali; same cup and lid; same starting temperature; stirring

The temperature rises as more acid is neutralised. Once all the acid has reacted, adding more (cooler) alkali just dilutes the mixture and absorbs energy, so the temperature falls. Drawing two straight lines of best fit and finding where they cross gives the volume of alkali needed for exact neutralisation and the true maximum temperature.

The main source of error in this practical is energy lost to the surroundings, which makes measured temperature changes smaller than the true value. Improvements to suggest: use a lid, use a polystyrene cup (or add more insulation), stir so the temperature is even, and read the highest temperature quickly. A temperature probe and data logger reduces reading errors. A polystyrene cup is used rather than a glass beaker because polystyrene is a poor conductor of heat.

4.5.1.2 Reaction Profiles

Chemical reactions can only occur when reacting particles collide with each other and with sufficient energy. The minimum amount of energy that particles must have to react is called the activation energy (Ea).

Reaction profiles (energy level diagrams) can be used to show the relative energies of reactants and products, the activation energy and the overall energy change of a reaction. You must be able to draw simple reaction profiles for exothermic and endothermic reactions, showing the relative energies of reactants and products, the activation energy and the overall energy change, with a curved line to show the energy as the reaction proceeds.

FeatureExothermicEndothermic
Products vs reactantsProducts lower in energy than reactantsProducts higher in energy than reactants
Overall energy change arrowPoints down (energy released)Points up (energy absorbed)
Sign of energy change, ΔHNegative (e.g. −890 kJ/mol)Positive (e.g. +178 kJ/mol)
Temperature of surroundingsIncreasesDecreases
Activation energyAlways measured from the reactants up to the peak of the curve. Both types need activation energy.
The two most common mistakes on reaction profiles are (1) drawing the activation energy arrow from the products to the peak, or from the axis — it must go from the reactant level to the top of the hump; and (2) forgetting arrowheads, or putting them on both ends. Label reactants and products, and label each arrow. A catalyst lowers the hump (see 4.6.1.4) but does not change the reactant or product levels.

4.5.1.3 The Energy Change of Reactions (HT)

During a chemical reaction:

  • Energy must be supplied to break bonds in the reactants — bond breaking is endothermic.
  • Energy is released when bonds are formed in the products — bond making is exothermic.
The energy needed to break bonds and the energy released when bonds are formed can be calculated from bond energies (the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond, in kJ/mol).

overall energy change = energy needed to break bonds in reactants − energy released making bonds in products

In an exothermic reaction, the energy released from forming new bonds is greater than the energy needed to break existing bonds → ΔH is negative.
In an endothermic reaction, the energy needed to break existing bonds is greater than the energy released from forming new bonds → ΔH is positive.

Worked Example 2 — Hydrogen and Chlorine (HT)

Question: Calculate the energy change for:  H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl
          (H–H = 436, Cl–Cl = 243, H–Cl = 432 kJ/mol)

Bonds broken (reactants):  1 × H–H   = 436
                           1 × Cl–Cl = 243
                           Total     = 679 kJ

Bonds made (products):     2 × H–Cl  = 2 × 432 = 864 kJ

Energy change = broken − made
              = 679 − 864
              = −185 kJ/mol

Answer: −185 kJ/mol. Negative, so the reaction is EXOTHERMIC.

Worked Example 4 — An Endothermic Reaction (HT)

Question: Calculate the energy change for:  2HBr → H₂ + Br₂
          (H–Br = 366, H–H = 436, Br–Br = 193 kJ/mol)

Bonds broken:  2 × H–Br = 2 × 366 = 732 kJ
Bonds made:    1 × H–H  = 436
               1 × Br–Br = 193
               Total     = 629 kJ

Energy change = 732 − 629 = +103 kJ/mol

Answer: +103 kJ/mol. Positive, so the reaction is ENDOTHERMIC.

Worked Example 5 — Finding an Unknown Bond Energy (HT)

Question: For  N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃   the energy change is −93 kJ/mol.
          N≡N = 945 kJ/mol, H–H = 436 kJ/mol.
          Calculate the N–H bond energy.

Bonds broken:  1 × N≡N = 945
               3 × H–H = 1308
               Total   = 2253 kJ

Bonds made:    2 NH₃ contain 6 × N–H = 6x

Energy change = broken − made
        −93   = 2253 − 6x
         6x   = 2253 + 93 = 2346
          x   = 391 kJ/mol

Answer: N–H = 391 kJ/mol
Draw out the displayed formula of every molecule before counting bonds — it is very easy to miss that CO₂ has two C=O double bonds, or that 2H₂O contains four O–H bonds. Remember to multiply by the balancing numbers. Always write "bonds broken − bonds made" and keep the sign in your answer: a positive value without a sign may be marked wrong. Bond-energy answers often differ slightly from measured values because bond energies are averages taken across many different compounds.

Common Misconceptions

MisconceptionCorrect idea
"Breaking bonds releases energy."Breaking bonds always requires energy (endothermic). Energy is released only when new bonds form.
"An endothermic reaction feels hot because it takes in heat."It takes energy from the surroundings, so the surroundings (including your hand) get colder.
"Exothermic reactions do not need activation energy."All reactions need activation energy. Burning methane needs a spark or flame to start; once started, the energy released keeps it going.
"A catalyst changes the overall energy change."A catalyst lowers the activation energy only; the reactant and product energy levels, and therefore ΔH, are unchanged.
"Bond energy calculations give exact answers."Bond energies are mean values averaged over many compounds, so calculated energy changes are approximate.

4.5.2 Chemical Cells and Fuel Cells (Triple)

4.5.2.1 Cells and Batteries

A cell contains chemicals which react to produce electricity. A simple cell can be made by connecting two different metals in contact with an electrolyte.

A battery consists of two or more cells connected together in series to provide a greater voltage.

The voltage produced by a cell is dependent upon a number of factors, including:

  • the type of electrode (which two metals are used) — the bigger the difference in reactivity between the two metals, the larger the voltage;
  • the electrolyte used (its type and concentration).

In a simple cell, the more reactive metal loses electrons more readily and becomes the negative terminal. Electrons flow through the external circuit from the more reactive metal to the less reactive metal. If both electrodes are the same metal, the voltage is zero.

Interpreting Cell Data

You should be able to interpret data for relative reactivity of different metals and evaluate the use of cells. The table below shows voltages measured with a copper electrode and a second metal in the same electrolyte:

Rechargeable and Non-Rechargeable Cells

TypeExplanationExamples
Non-rechargeableThe chemical reactions stop when one of the reactants has been used up. The cell is then "flat" and must be replaced.Alkaline batteries (zinc and manganese dioxide), zinc–carbon cells
RechargeableThe chemical reactions are reversed when an external electrical current is supplied, regenerating the original reactants.Lithium-ion (phones, laptops, electric cars), nickel–metal hydride, lead–acid car batteries
If asked why a non-rechargeable battery eventually stops working, say "one of the reactants is used up, so the chemical reaction stops". If asked why a rechargeable battery can be recharged, say "the reaction is reversed when an external current is supplied". Vague answers like "it runs out of energy" or "the electricity is used up" do not score.

4.5.2.2 Fuel Cells

Fuel cells are supplied by an external source of fuel (e.g. hydrogen) and oxygen or air. The fuel is oxidised electrochemically within the fuel cell to produce a potential difference. Unlike a battery, a fuel cell keeps producing electricity for as long as fuel and oxygen are supplied.

The overall reaction in a hydrogen fuel cell involves the oxidation of hydrogen to produce water:

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

This is the same overall reaction as burning hydrogen, but in a fuel cell the energy is released as electrical energy rather than mostly as heat, and there is no flame.

Half Equations in the Hydrogen Fuel Cell (HT)

Acidic electrolyte (the version used by AQA):

Negative electrode:   2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻          hydrogen is OXIDISED
                                               (loses electrons)

Positive electrode:   O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O    oxygen is REDUCED
                                               (gains electrons)

Add them (4e⁻ and 4H⁺ cancel):
Overall:              2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Alkaline electrolyte (for interest – e.g. KOH fuel cells):
Negative electrode:   2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻
Positive electrode:   O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
Overall:              2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O   (same)

Evaluating Hydrogen Fuel Cells vs Rechargeable Batteries

Hydrogen fuel cells — advantagesHydrogen fuel cells — disadvantages
  • The only product is water — no CO₂ or other pollutants at the point of use.
  • Do not need to be electrically recharged; refuelling takes minutes rather than hours.
  • Keep working as long as fuel is supplied — longer range for vehicles.
  • Fewer parts to wear out; do not lose efficiency over repeated charge cycles.
  • Less toxic waste at the end of life than batteries (which contain toxic metal compounds).
  • Hydrogen is a gas — it takes up a large volume and must be stored under high pressure or at very low temperature.
  • Hydrogen is highly flammable — risk of explosion if it leaks.
  • Hydrogen is usually made from fossil fuels (e.g. methane) or by electrolysis using electricity that may come from fossil fuels — so it is not necessarily carbon-free overall.
  • Few hydrogen refuelling stations; fuel cells are expensive (platinum catalysts).
Rechargeable batteries — advantagesRechargeable batteries — disadvantages
  • Can be recharged from the mains anywhere there is a socket.
  • No flammable gas storage needed.
  • Well-developed technology; widely available.
  • Recharging takes a long time.
  • Store less energy per kg — limited range in vehicles.
  • Lose capacity after many charge cycles; contain toxic/scarce metals that are hard to dispose of.
  • Electricity used for charging may be generated from fossil fuels.
Evaluation questions ("Evaluate the use of hydrogen fuel cells compared with rechargeable cells for powering vehicles") need points on both sides and a justified conclusion. The key chemistry point is that a fuel cell's only product is water, but the hydrogen itself may have been produced using fossil fuels. Do not say that fuel cells are "100% clean" without qualifying it.
