4.2 Bonding, Structure and the Properties of Matter

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 1

4.2.1 Chemical Bonds: Ionic, Covalent and Metallic

Chemists use theories of structure and bonding to explain the physical and chemical properties of materials. Analysing structures shows that atoms can be arranged in a variety of ways, some of which are molecular while others are giant structures. Understanding bonding is the key to explaining why salt has a high melting point, why oxygen is a gas, and why copper conducts electricity.

You should be able to explain chemical bonding in terms of electrostatic forces and the transfer or sharing of electrons. A quick way to decide the bond type from a formula: metal + non-metal (e.g. NaCl, MgO, CaF₂) → ionic; only non-metals (e.g. H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, Cl₂) → covalent; pure metal or alloy (e.g. Cu, brass) → metallic.

4.2.1.2 Ionic Bonding

When a metal atom reacts with a non-metal atom, electrons in the outer shell of the metal atom are transferred to the non-metal atom. Metal atoms lose electrons to become positively charged ions; non-metal atoms gain electrons to become negatively charged ions. The ions produced have the electronic structure of a noble gas (Group 0) — a full outer shell.

The charge on an ion formed by an element in Groups 1, 2, 6 and 7 relates directly to its group number:

Dot and Cross Diagrams for Ionic Compounds

The electron transfer during the formation of an ionic compound can be represented by a dot and cross diagram. Electrons from one atom are drawn as dots and from the other as crosses, so you can see where each electron came from. The final ions are drawn in square brackets with the charge at the top right. Often only the outer shell is drawn.

Working Out Formulae of Ionic Compounds

An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Combine ions in the ratio that makes the charges cancel.

Some ions contain more than one element (compound ions). You should know: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻ and ammonium NH₄⁺. Use brackets when more than one compound ion is needed: calcium hydroxide is Ca(OH)₂; ammonium sulfate is (NH₄)₂SO₄.

In ionic dot and cross diagrams, examiners look for: (1) the correct number of electrons in the outer shell of each ion (usually 8); (2) square brackets around each ion; (3) the correct charge on each ion; (4) dots and crosses making clear which electrons were transferred. For CaCl₂ or Na₂O you must draw two of the ion that appears twice (or write "2" in front of it).

4.2.1.3 Ionic Compounds

An ionic compound is a giant structure of ions. Ionic compounds are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions. These forces act in all directions in the lattice, and this is called ionic bonding.

The structure of sodium chloride is a regular, repeating cubic lattice. Each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion is surrounded by six Na⁺ ions. The lattice extends in three dimensions and contains a huge number of ions — there is no such thing as a single "molecule" of sodium chloride. The formula NaCl simply tells us the ratio of ions (1 : 1).

Deducing the Empirical Formula from a Model

You should be able to work out the empirical formula of an ionic compound from a diagram of its structure: count the ions of each type (or use their charges) and simplify to the lowest whole-number ratio. For example, a 2D diagram showing 8 Mg²⁺ ions and 8 O²⁻ ions gives MgO; a diagram showing 6 K⁺ and 3 O²⁻ gives K₂O.

4.2.1.4 Covalent Bonding

When atoms share pairs of electrons, they form covalent bonds. These bonds between atoms are strong. Covalent bonding occurs in most non-metallic elements and in compounds of non-metals. Each shared pair of electrons is one covalent bond; the shared electrons are attracted to the nuclei of both atoms.

Covalently bonded substances may consist of:

  • small molecules — e.g. H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂, HCl, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄;
  • large molecules — such as polymers (e.g. poly(ethene));
  • giant covalent structures (macromolecules) — such as diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide.

You must be able to draw dot and cross diagrams for the molecules H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂, HCl, H₂O, NH₃ and CH₄. The shared electrons are drawn in the overlap of the two atoms' outer shells.

You should be able to deduce the molecular formula of a substance from a given model or diagram in these forms, by counting the atoms of each element. For a giant covalent structure, the formula is an empirical (ratio) formula, e.g. SiO₂.

In covalent dot and cross diagrams, each atom must end up with a full outer shell (8 electrons, or 2 for hydrogen). Shared pairs go in the overlap. A common error is forgetting lone pairs — e.g. drawing NH₃ with only 6 electrons around nitrogen. Count every shell after drawing. For N₂, there are 3 shared pairs (6 shared electrons) plus 1 lone pair on each N.

4.2.1.5 Metallic Bonding

Metals consist of giant structures of atoms arranged in a regular pattern. The electrons in the outer shell of metal atoms are delocalised and so are free to move through the whole structure. The sharing of delocalised electrons gives rise to strong metallic bonds — the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the "sea" of delocalised electrons.

4.2.2 How Bonding and Structure are Related to the Properties of Substances

4.2.2.1 The Three States of Matter

The three states of matter are solid, liquid and gas. Melting and freezing take place at the melting point; boiling and condensing take place at the boiling point. The three states can be represented by a simple model in which particles are represented by small solid spheres. Particle theory can help to explain melting, boiling, freezing and condensing.

The amount of energy needed to change state from solid to liquid and from liquid to gas depends on the strength of the forces between the particles of the substance. The nature of the particles involved depends on the type of bonding and the structure of the substance. The stronger the forces between the particles, the higher the melting point and boiling point of the substance.

Predicting States from Data

To predict the state of a substance at a given temperature:

  • Temperature below the melting point → solid.
  • Temperature between the melting point and boiling point → liquid.
  • Temperature above the boiling point → gas.

Worked Example 1 — Predicting the State

Question: Substance X has a melting point of −7 °C and a boiling
          point of 59 °C. What is its state at 25 °C and at 80 °C?

At 25 °C:  −7 < 25 < 59   → between m.p. and b.p. → LIQUID
At 80 °C:  80 > 59         → above b.p.              → GAS

(Substance X is bromine.)

Limitations of the Simple Particle Model (Higher)

The particle model represents particles as small, solid, inelastic spheres. This is useful but has limitations:

  • There are no forces shown between the spheres, so the model cannot explain why different substances have different melting and boiling points.
  • All particles are represented as spheres, but real particles may be atoms, ions or molecules of many different shapes.
  • The particles are shown as solid — real atoms are mostly empty space.
  • The spheres are inelastic and the model does not show the movement or energy of particles, nor the actual distances between them (gas particles are much further apart than can be drawn).

4.2.2.2 State Symbols

In chemical equations, the three states of matter are shown as (s) solid, (l) liquid and (g) gas, with (aq) for aqueous solutions (substances dissolved in water). 
Example:  CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Water is (l) not (aq) — (aq) means "dissolved in water", and water cannot be dissolved in itself. A precipitate formed in a reaction is (s). Gases given off are (g).

Ionic compounds conduct when molten or in solution because the ions move — not electrons. Saying "electrons move" for an ionic compound is a classic error that loses the mark. Equally, saying "ionic bonds are broken when NaCl melts" is acceptable, but saying "intermolecular forces are broken" is wrong — there are no molecules in an ionic compound.

4.2.2.4 Properties of Small Molecules

Substances that consist of small molecules are usually gases or liquids that have relatively low melting points and boiling points. These substances have only weak forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces). It is these intermolecular forces that are overcome, not the covalent bonds, when the substance melts or boils.
  • The intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecules, so larger molecules have higher melting and boiling points. This explains the trend in the halogens (F₂ gas → I₂ solid) and in alkanes in crude oil.
  • Substances that consist of small molecules do not conduct electricity, because the molecules do not have an overall electric charge and there are no free electrons or ions.

This is the single most-tested misconception in the topic. When a simple molecular substance boils, the weak intermolecular forces between molecules are overcome; the strong covalent bonds inside each molecule are not broken. If you write "the covalent bonds are weak" or "covalent bonds break when water boils", you will lose the marks. Water vapour is still H₂O molecules.

4.2.2.5 Polymers

Polymers have very large molecules. The atoms in the polymer molecules are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds. The intermolecular forces between polymer molecules are relatively strong, and so these substances are solids at room temperature.

Polymers are made by joining together thousands of small molecules called monomers. For example, poly(ethene) is made from ethene (C₂H₄) monomers. A polymer can be represented by its repeating unit in brackets with "n" after it, showing it repeats many times.

Repeating unit of poly(ethene):

        ⎛  H   H  ⎞
        ⎜  |   |  ⎟
      ──⎜──C───C──⎟──
        ⎜  |   |  ⎟
        ⎝  H   H  ⎠n

Molecular formula of the repeating unit: C₂H₄
The bonds crossing the brackets show the chain continues on both sides.

You should be able to recognise polymers from diagrams showing their bonding and structure. Because the molecules are long, tangled chains, the intermolecular forces add up along their length and are much stronger than those between small molecules — this is why plastics are solid, while ethene itself is a gas.

4.2.2.6 Giant Covalent Structures

Substances that consist of giant covalent structures are solids with very high melting points. All of the atoms in these structures are linked to other atoms by strong covalent bonds. These bonds must be overcome to melt or boil these substances. Diamond and graphite (forms of carbon) and silicon dioxide (silica) are examples of giant covalent structures.

Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) is the main compound in sand. Each silicon atom is covalently bonded to four oxygen atoms, and each oxygen atom is bonded to two silicon atoms, in a giant 3D lattice. Like diamond, it is hard, has a very high melting point (about 1700 °C) and does not conduct electricity.

4.2.2.7 Properties of Metals and Alloys

Metals have giant structures of atoms with strong metallic bonding. This means that most metals have high melting and boiling points — a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons.

In pure metals, atoms are arranged in layers, which allows metals to be bent and shaped (malleable) — the layers can slide over each other while the delocalised electrons keep holding the structure together. Pure metals are often too soft for many uses, so they are mixed with other metals to make alloys, which are harder.

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with at least one other element (usually another metal, or carbon in steel). The different sizes of atoms in an alloy distort the layers in the structure, making it more difficult for them to slide over each other, so alloys are harder than pure metals.

Common alloys include steel (iron + carbon, and sometimes other metals), brass (copper + zinc), bronze (copper + tin), and gold alloys used for jewellery (pure gold is 24 carat; 18 carat gold is 75% gold). Alloys are covered further in topic 4.10 Using Resources.

4.2.2.8 Metals as Conductors

Metals are good conductors of electricity because the delocalised electrons in the metal carry electrical charge through the metal. Metals are good conductors of thermal energy because energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons.
When explaining why metals conduct, use the word delocalised and say what the electrons do: "the delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge". Simply saying "metals have free electrons" may score only 1 of 2 marks. For thermal conduction, say "energy is transferred by the delocalised electrons".

4.2.3 Structure and Bonding of Carbon

Carbon can form several different structures (called allotropes) because each carbon atom can form up to four covalent bonds. The properties of each form depend on how the atoms are bonded together.

4.2.3.1 Diamond

In diamond, each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in a giant covalent structure, so diamond is very hard, has a very high melting point and does not conduct electricity.
  • Very hard — every atom is held by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D tetrahedral network. Used in cutting tools and drill bits.
  • Very high melting point (above 3500 °C) — a huge amount of energy is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds.
  • Does not conduct electricity — all four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonding; there are no delocalised electrons or ions.

4.2.3.2 Graphite

In graphite, each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds with three other carbon atoms, forming layers of hexagonal rings which have no covalent bonds between the layers. In graphite, one electron from each carbon atom is delocalised.
  • Soft and slippery — there are only weak intermolecular forces between the layers, so the layers can slide over each other. Used in pencils and as a lubricant.
  • High melting point — the strong covalent bonds within the layers must be broken to melt it.
  • Conducts electricity and thermal energy — the delocalised electrons can move along the layers. Used for electrodes. You should be able to explain the properties of graphite in terms of its structure and bonding, and know that graphite is similar to metals in that it has delocalised electrons.

4.2.3.3 Graphene and Fullerenes

Graphene is a single layer of graphite — one atom thick. It has properties that make it useful in electronics and composites. You should be able to explain the properties of graphene in terms of its structure and bonding.
  • Very strong — held by strong covalent bonds in the layer (about 100 times stronger than steel of the same thickness).
  • Conducts electricity very well — delocalised electrons move freely across the layer.
  • Transparent and extremely light — being only one atom thick.
Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms with hollow shapes. The structure of fullerenes is based on hexagonal rings of carbon atoms but they may also contain rings with five or seven carbon atoms. The first fullerene to be discovered was Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀), which has a spherical shape.

Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes with very high length to diameter ratios. Their properties make them useful for nanotechnology, electronics and materials:

  • high tensile strength (resist being pulled apart) — used to reinforce materials such as tennis rackets and bicycle frames;
  • high electrical and thermal conductivity — used in electronics.

So C₆₀ has a much lower melting point than diamond — when it melts, only the weak intermolecular forces between C₆₀ molecules are overcome. Questions often give you data about an unfamiliar form of carbon and ask you to explain its properties — always link each property to the bonding (number of covalent bonds per atom, delocalised electrons, forces between layers or molecules).

4.2.4 Bulk and Surface Properties of Matter including Nanoparticles (Triple Only)

4.2.4.1 Sizes of Particles and their Properties

Nanoscience refers to structures that are 1–100 nm in size, of the order of a few hundred atoms. Nanoparticles are smaller than fine particles (PM2.5), which have diameters between 100 and 2500 nm (1 × 10⁻⁷ m and 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m). Coarse particles (PM10) have diameters between 1 × 10⁻⁵ m and 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m. Coarse particles are often referred to as dust.

As the side of a cube decreases by a factor of 10, the surface area to volume ratio increases by a factor of 10. Nanoparticles may have properties different from those for the same materials in bulk because of their high surface area to volume ratio — a much larger proportion of the atoms are at the surface. It may also mean that smaller quantities are needed to be effective than for materials with normal particle sizes.

Worked Example 2 — Surface Area to Volume Ratio

Question: A cube-shaped nanoparticle has sides of 20 nm.
          Calculate its surface area to volume ratio.

Surface area = 6 × (side)²  = 6 × 20²  = 6 × 400  = 2400 nm²
Volume       = (side)³      = 20³                 = 8000 nm³
SA : V       = 2400 ÷ 8000  = 0.3

Answer:  SA : V = 0.3 : 1  (or 0.3 nm⁻¹)

If the side were 2 nm (10 times smaller):
  SA = 6 × 4 = 24 nm²,  V = 8 nm³,  SA : V = 3 : 1
  → ratio has increased by a factor of 10 ✓

Worked Example 3 — Comparing Particle Sizes

Question: A nanoparticle has a diameter of 25 nm. A fine particle
          of soot has a diameter of 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m. How many times
          larger is the soot particle's diameter?

Convert:  25 nm = 25 × 10⁻⁹ m = 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ m
Ratio:    (2.5 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ (2.5 × 10⁻⁸) = 1 × 10² = 100

Answer:  The soot particle is 100 times larger in diameter.

Nanoparticles have many applications in medicine, electronics, cosmetics and sun creams, as deodorants, and as catalysts. New applications for nanoparticulate materials are an important area of research.

Risks of Nanoparticles

You should be able to evaluate the benefits and disadvantages of nanoparticles:

  • Their effects on health are not yet fully understood — they are a relatively new technology with little long-term data.
  • Because they are so small, they may be absorbed through the skin or breathed into the lungs and enter cells, where they might cause damage (possibly being toxic or catalysing harmful reactions).
  • Nanoparticles washed into the environment (e.g. silver from clothing) might harm useful bacteria in water treatment or ecosystems.
  • Many argue that products containing nanoparticles should be tested more thoroughly and clearly labelled.
For 6-mark "evaluate the use of nanoparticles in sun cream" questions, give a balanced answer: benefits (better UV protection, better coverage, no white marks, so may reduce skin cancer risk) and risks (may be absorbed into cells, long-term effects unknown, may damage the environment when washed off), then a justified conclusion. Always explain the benefit using the high surface area to volume ratio where relevant.
