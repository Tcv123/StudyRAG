4.1 Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 1

4.1.1 Atoms, Elements and Compounds

An atom is the smallest part of an element that can exist. All substances are made of atoms.

An element is a substance made of only one type of atom. There are about 100 different elements, and each is shown in the periodic table.

A compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions.

Every element has a chemical symbol of one or two letters — for example O for oxygen, Na for sodium (from the Latin natrium) and Fe for iron (ferrum). The first letter is always a capital and the second is always lower case: Co is cobalt, but CO is carbon monoxide (a compound of carbon and oxygen). You need to know the names and symbols of the first 20 elements, Groups 1 and 7, and other common elements named in the specification.

Compounds and Chemical Reactions

Compounds are formed from elements by chemical reactions. A chemical reaction always involves the formation of one or more new substances, and often involves a detectable energy change. Because the atoms in a compound are held together by chemical bonds, a compound can only be separated into its elements by further chemical reactions — not by physical methods such as filtration or distillation.

Compounds have properties that are completely different from the elements that made them. Sodium is a soft, highly reactive metal that fizzes in water; chlorine is a toxic green gas. Combined chemically they form sodium chloride — table salt — a white crystalline solid you can safely eat.

Word Equations and Symbol Equations

Chemical reactions can be represented by word equations or by symbol equations. Reactants go on the left of the arrow; products on the right. You should be able to write word equations for reactions in the specification and write formulae and balanced chemical equations for them. Higher-tier students should also be able to write balanced half equations and ionic equations where appropriate.

Word equation:    magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
Symbol equation:  2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)

Word equation:    methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Symbol equation:  CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. You balance by changing the large numbers in front of formulae (coefficients) — never by changing the small subscript numbers inside a formula, as that would change the substance itself.

Worked Example 1 — Balancing an Equation

Question: Balance the equation for sodium reacting with water.
          Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂

Step 1:  Count atoms.
         Left:  Na 1, H 2, O 1      Right: Na 1, H 3, O 1
Step 2:  H is unbalanced. Put 2 in front of H₂O and NaOH:
         Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
         Left:  Na 1, H 4, O 2      Right: Na 2, H 4, O 2
Step 3:  Na now unbalanced. Put 2 in front of Na:
         2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
         Left:  Na 2, H 4, O 2      Right: Na 2, H 4, O 2  ✓

Answer:  2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)

Half Equations and Ionic Equations (Higher)

A half equation shows what happens to one species when it gains or loses electrons (e⁻). An ionic equation shows only the ions that actually take part, leaving out spectator ions that are unchanged.

Half equations:
  Na → Na⁺ + e⁻            (sodium atom loses one electron)
  Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻         (chlorine molecule gains two electrons)

Ionic equation — chlorine displacing bromide ions:
  Full:   Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)
  Ionic:  Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq)
          (K⁺ ions are spectator ions — they do not change)
When balancing, check charges as well as atoms in half equations and ionic equations: the total charge must be the same on both sides. In Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻, the left has a total charge of 2− and the right has 2 × (1−) = 2−. Never change the formula of a substance to make an equation balance — H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, not water.

4.1.1.2 Mixtures and Separation Techniques

A mixture consists of two or more elements or compounds not chemically combined together. The chemical properties of each substance in the mixture are unchanged, and the substances can be separated by physical processes — no chemical reactions are needed.

Air (mainly nitrogen, oxygen and argon), sea water (water + dissolved salts) and crude oil (many hydrocarbons) are all mixtures. Because the components are not bonded together, a mixture has no fixed composition and does not have a single sharp melting or boiling point.

When asked to describe a separation, name the technique and the property it relies on. Filtration relies on particle size / solubility; distillation relies on different boiling points; chromatography relies on different solubility in the solvent and attraction to the paper. Draw the chromatography start line in pencil (ink would dissolve and run) and keep it above the solvent level (otherwise the spots dissolve into the solvent in the beaker).

4.1.1.3 The Development of the Model of the Atom

Ideas about atoms have changed many times as new experimental evidence was discovered. This is a classic example of how scientific models are tested and replaced. You need to know the sequence and what evidence caused each change.

DateScientistModel / discoveryEvidence / reason
Early 1800sJohn DaltonAtoms are tiny solid spheres that cannot be divided. Each element has its own kind of atom.Elements combine in fixed ratios by mass.
1897J.J. ThomsonDiscovered the electron. Proposed the plum pudding model: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.Cathode-ray experiments showed atoms contain tiny negative particles, so atoms can be divided.
1909–1911Ernest Rutherford (with Geiger and Marsden)Nuclear model: mass concentrated in a tiny, positively charged nucleus, surrounded mostly by empty space containing electrons.The alpha particle scattering experiment.
1913Niels BohrElectrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances in shells (energy levels).Theoretical calculations agreed with experimental observations (e.g. emission spectra).
c. 1920Rutherford and othersThe positive charge of the nucleus is divided into smaller particles, each with the same amount of positive charge: protons.Later experiments.
1932James ChadwickProvided evidence for neutrons in the nucleus.About 20 years after the nucleus became an accepted idea; explained why atomic masses were larger than proton counts alone.

The Alpha Particle Scattering Experiment

Rutherford's team fired positively charged alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil and recorded where they went. If the plum pudding model were right, the positive charge would be spread thinly through the atom, and all the alpha particles should pass through with only tiny deflections.

ObservationConclusion
Most alpha particles passed straight through.Most of the atom is empty space.
Some were deflected through small angles.The centre of the atom has a positive charge that repels the positive alpha particles.
A very few (about 1 in 8000) bounced back.The positive charge and almost all the mass are concentrated in a very small nucleus.
A favourite question: "Compare the plum pudding model with the nuclear model." Give differences in pairs: in the plum pudding model, positive charge is spread throughout the atom, whereas in the nuclear model it is concentrated in the nucleus; the plum pudding model has no empty space, whereas the nuclear model is mostly empty space; the plum pudding model has electrons embedded in the positive charge, whereas the nuclear model has electrons orbiting the nucleus (in shells, after Bohr).

4.1.1.4–4.1.1.5 Subatomic Particles, Size and Mass of Atoms

Atoms contain three subatomic particles. The nucleus contains protons and neutrons; the electrons are arranged in shells (energy levels) around the nucleus.
ParticleRelative chargeRelative massLocation
Proton+11Nucleus
Neutron01Nucleus
Electron−1Very small (about 1/1840)Shells around nucleus

In an atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons in the nucleus, so atoms have no overall electrical charge. The number of protons in an atom of an element is its atomic number; all atoms of a particular element have the same number of protons, and atoms of different elements have different numbers of protons.

Size of Atoms

  • Atoms are very small, having a radius of about 0.1 nm (1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m).
  • The radius of a nucleus is less than 1/10 000 of that of the atom (about 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m).
  • Almost all of the mass of an atom is in the nucleus.

To picture this: if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot. You should be able to relate size and scale of atoms to objects in the physical world, and use standard form and prefixes (1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m).

Mass Number, Atomic Number and Isotopes

Atomic number = number of protons (also the number of electrons in a neutral atom).
Mass number = total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number.

Atoms are often shown in nuclide notation, with the mass number written at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left of the symbol: for sodium, ²³₁₁Na has 11 protons, 11 electrons and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons (so different mass numbers). Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties because they have the same electronic structure.

Ions

Atoms can gain or lose electrons to form charged particles called ions. The number of protons never changes. For example, ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺ has 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 13 − 3 = 10 electrons; ³²₁₆S²⁻ has 16 protons, 16 neutrons and 16 + 2 = 18 electrons.

When calculating subatomic particles in an ion, work out protons first (= atomic number), then neutrons (mass number − atomic number), then electrons last: subtract the charge for a positive ion, add it for a negative ion. A very common mistake is to change the number of protons when an ion forms — protons never change in a chemical reaction.

4.1.1.6 Relative Atomic Mass

The relative atomic mass (Ar) of an element is an average value that takes account of the abundance of the isotopes of the element. It is measured relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, so it has no units.

Most elements exist naturally as a mixture of isotopes. This is why many Ar values on the periodic table are not whole numbers (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5).

Ar = Σ(isotope mass × abundance) ÷ total abundance
If abundances are given as percentages, the total abundance is 100.

Worked Example 2 — Relative Atomic Mass of Chlorine

Question: Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37.
          Calculate the relative atomic mass of chlorine.

Formula: Ar = (mass₁ × abundance₁ + mass₂ × abundance₂) ÷ 100
Working: Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100
            = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100
            = 3550 ÷ 100
            = 35.5

Answer:  Ar(Cl) = 35.5

Worked Example 3 — Relative Atomic Mass of Copper

Question: A sample of copper contains 69.2% copper-63 and 30.8%
          copper-65. Calculate the relative atomic mass to 1 d.p.

Working: Ar = (63 × 69.2 + 65 × 30.8) ÷ 100
            = (4359.6 + 2002.0) ÷ 100
            = 6361.6 ÷ 100
            = 63.616

Answer:  Ar(Cu) = 63.6 (to 1 d.p.)

Sense check: the answer must lie between 63 and 65, and closer
to 63 because copper-63 is the more abundant isotope. ✓

Worked Example 4 — Finding an Unknown Abundance

Question: Boron has two isotopes, boron-10 and boron-11, and
          Ar = 10.8. Calculate the percentage of boron-10.

Let % of boron-10 = x, so % of boron-11 = 100 − x
         10.8 = (10x + 11(100 − x)) ÷ 100
         1080 = 10x + 1100 − 11x
         1080 = 1100 − x
            x = 20

Answer:  20% boron-10, 80% boron-11
Always sanity-check an Ar answer: it must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses, and nearer the most abundant one. If the abundances are given as numbers that do not add up to 100 (e.g. from a mass spectrum with relative heights 3 and 1), divide by their total, not by 100.

4.1.1.7 Electronic Structure

Electrons occupy the lowest available energy levels (innermost available shells) first. For the first 20 elements:
• the first shell holds up to 2 electrons
• the second shell holds up to 8 electrons
• the third shell holds 8 electrons before the fourth shell starts to fill (for elements 1–20)

The electronic structure of an atom can be written as numbers separated by commas (e.g. sodium is 2,8,1) or drawn as a diagram of concentric circles with electrons shown as dots or crosses.

Link to the periodic table: the number of shells containing electrons equals the period (row) number. The number of electrons in the outer shell equals the group number (for Groups 1–7; Group 0 elements have a full outer shell).
For the first 20 elements, the fourth shell starts filling at potassium (2,8,8,1) even though the third shell could, in theory, hold more. Do not write potassium as 2,8,9. Draw electrons in pairs around the outer shell when possible — it makes bonding diagrams much easier later.

4.1.2 The Periodic Table

4.1.2.1 The Periodic Table

The elements in the periodic table are arranged in order of atomic (proton) number so that elements with similar properties are in columns, known as groups. The horizontal rows are called periods. The table is called a periodic table because similar properties occur at regular intervals.

Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell, and this gives them similar chemical properties — chemical reactions only involve the outer electrons. You should be able to explain how the position of an element in the periodic table is related to the arrangement of electrons in its atoms, and hence to its atomic number, and to predict possible reactions and probable reactivity of elements from their positions.

4.1.2.2 Development of the Periodic Table

Before the discovery of protons, neutrons and electrons, scientists attempted to classify the elements by arranging them in order of their atomic weights.

  • Early periodic tables were incomplete. Some elements were placed in inappropriate groups if the strict order of atomic weights was followed. John Newlands' "law of octaves" (1864) noticed that every eighth element had similar properties, but his table broke down after calcium and grouped some very different elements together (e.g. metals with non-metals), so it was not widely accepted.
  • Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) overcame some of these problems by:
    • leaving gaps for elements that he thought had not yet been discovered;
    • in some places changing the order based on atomic weights so that elements lined up with others of similar properties (e.g. he placed iodine after tellurium even though tellurium has the higher atomic weight).
  • Elements with properties predicted by Mendeleev were later discovered and filled the gaps — for example gallium (1875) and germanium (1886), whose properties closely matched his predictions for "eka-aluminium" and "eka-silicon". This strongly supported his table.
  • Knowledge of isotopes made it possible to explain why the order based on atomic weights was not always correct: atomic weight depends on the number of neutrons, but an element's chemistry depends on its proton number. Ordering by atomic number places every element correctly.
A common 4–6 mark question asks you to compare Mendeleev's table with the modern periodic table. Key points: Mendeleev ordered by atomic weight (modern: atomic number); he left gaps (modern table has no gaps for elements up to 118); he had no Group 0 (noble gases were undiscovered because they are so unreactive); and he swapped some pairs (Te/I) — later justified by the discovery of isotopes and atomic number.

4.1.2.3 Metals and Non-metals

Metals are elements that react to form positive ions. They are found to the left and towards the bottom of the periodic table. The majority of elements are metals.

Non-metals are elements that do not form positive ions. They are found towards the right and top of the periodic table.

Whether an element forms positive ions depends on its electronic structure. Metal atoms have only a few outer electrons (1, 2 or 3), so it takes relatively little energy for them to lose these electrons and achieve the stable electronic structure of a noble gas. Non-metal atoms have nearly full outer shells, so they tend to gain electrons (forming negative ions) or share electrons (covalent bonds).

4.1.2.4 Group 0 — The Noble Gases

The elements in Group 0 are called the noble gases: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon. They are unreactive and do not easily form molecules because their atoms have stable arrangements of electrons. The noble gases have eight electrons in their outer shell, except for helium, which has only two.

Because noble gases exist as single atoms (they are monatomic), the only forces between them are very weak intermolecular forces, so their boiling points are very low. The boiling points of the noble gases increase with increasing relative atomic mass (going down the group), because the atoms get larger and the forces between them become stronger.

Explain unreactivity in terms of full outer shells — noble gases have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons.

4.1.2.5 Group 1 — The Alkali Metals

The elements in Group 1 are known as the alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium). They have characteristic properties because of the single electron in their outer shell. They react with water to form alkaline hydroxide solutions.

Alkali metals are soft (they can be cut with a knife), have low densities (Li, Na and K float on water) and have relatively low melting points for metals. They are stored under oil because they react rapidly with oxygen and water vapour in the air. When freshly cut they are shiny, but they quickly tarnish as a layer of oxide forms.

Explaining the Trend in Reactivity

Reactivity increases going down Group 1. In all their reactions, Group 1 atoms lose their single outer electron to form a 1+ ion (e.g. Na → Na⁺ + e⁻). Going down the group:
• the atoms get larger — more shells of electrons;
• the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner shells;
• so there is weaker electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron;
• so the outer electron is lost more easily.
For a full-mark explanation of the Group 1 trend you need four linked points: more shells → outer electron further from nucleus (plus more shielding) → weaker attraction to the nucleus → outer electron lost more easily. Do not say "the outer electron is easier to gain" — Group 1 metals lose electrons. Avoid saying "the bonds are weaker" — it is the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron that is weaker.

4.1.2.6 Group 7 — The Halogens

The elements in Group 7 are known as the halogens (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine). They have similar reactions because they all have seven electrons in their outer shell. The halogens are non-metals and consist of diatomic molecules made of pairs of atoms (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂).

Going down Group 7, the halogens have higher relative molecular masses, so the melting and boiling points increase: the larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, which need more energy to overcome. The colours also become darker.

Reactions of the Halogens

  • With metals: halogens react to form ionic compounds (metal halides) in which the halide ions carry a 1− charge. e.g. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl; 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃.
  • With non-metals: halogens form covalent molecular compounds, e.g. H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (hydrogen chloride gas, which dissolves in water to form hydrochloric acid).
  • Displacement reactions: a more reactive halogen can displace a less reactive halogen from an aqueous solution of its salt.

Explaining the Trend in Reactivity

Reactivity decreases going down Group 7. Halogen atoms react by gaining one electron to form a 1− ion with a full outer shell. Going down the group:
• the atoms get larger — more shells of electrons;
• the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded;
• so there is weaker attraction from the nucleus for an incoming electron;
• so an electron is gained less easily.

Students often reverse the Group 1 and Group 7 trends. Remember: Group 1 reactivity increases down the group (electron lost more easily); Group 7 reactivity decreases down the group (electron gained less easily). The underlying reason is the same — larger atoms, weaker attraction between nucleus and outer shell — but the effect is opposite because one group loses and the other gains electrons. In displacement questions, use halide (bromide, iodide) for the ions and halogen (bromine, iodine) for the element.

4.1.3 Properties of Transition Metals (Triple Only)

4.1.3.1 Comparison with Group 1 Elements

The transition elements are metals found in the central block of the periodic table, between Groups 2 and 3. They have similar properties to each other and some special properties, but they are quite different from the Group 1 metals. You should be able to describe the difference compared with Group 1 in melting points, densities, strength, hardness and reactivity with oxygen, water and halogens. Examples include Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu.

If asked to "give two properties of transition metals that Group 1 metals do not have", choose from: form coloured compounds, form ions with different charges, act as catalysts, have higher melting points, have higher densities, are harder/stronger. Be specific — "they are stronger" scores, "they are better" does not. Always name a correct example of a transition-metal catalyst with its process (iron → Haber process; nickel → hydrogenation).
