4.7 Organic Chemistry

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 2

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Carbon atoms can form four covalent bonds and can join to each other to make chains and rings of almost any length, so there are millions of organic compounds — far more than the compounds of every other element put together. This topic covers crude oil and the fuels made from it, cracking, the homologous series of alkenes, alcohols and carboxylic acids, and both synthetic and natural polymers. Sections marked (Chemistry only) are not in Combined Science; content marked (HT only) is Higher Tier.

4.7.1 Carbon Compounds as Fuels and Feedstock

4.7.1.1 Crude Oil, Hydrocarbons and Alkanes

Crude oil is a finite resource found in rocks. It is the remains of an ancient biomass consisting mainly of plankton that was buried in mud millions of years ago and subjected to heat and pressure.

Crude oil is a mixture of a very large number of compounds. Most of the compounds in crude oil are hydrocarbons.
A hydrocarbon is a compound made of hydrogen and carbon atoms only.

Because crude oil is a mixture (not a compound), the substances in it are not chemically bonded to one another. Each keeps its own properties — including its own boiling point — which is why the mixture can be separated by a physical process (fractional distillation).

Most of the hydrocarbons in crude oil are alkanes. The alkanes are a homologous series.

A homologous series is a family of compounds that:
  • have the same general formula;
  • differ from the next member by a CH2 unit;
  • have the same functional group and so similar chemical reactions;
  • show a gradual trend in physical properties (e.g. boiling point rises as the chain gets longer).
Alkanes have the general formula CnH2n+2.

They are saturated hydrocarbons — every carbon atom is joined to four other atoms by single covalent bonds; there are no C=C double bonds. This makes alkanes fairly unreactive (apart from combustion).

4.7.1.2 Fractional Distillation and Petrochemicals

The many hydrocarbons in crude oil can be separated into fractions, each of which contains molecules with a similar number of carbon atoms and therefore similar boiling points. This is done by fractional distillation.

How Fractional Distillation Works

  1. Crude oil is heated to about 350–400 °C so that most of it evaporates (vaporises).
  2. The vapours enter the bottom of a tall fractionating column, which has a temperature gradient — it is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top.
  3. The vapours rise up the column through trays fitted with bubble caps.
  4. As the vapours rise, they cool. Each hydrocarbon condenses when it reaches a level where the temperature is below its boiling point; the liquid is collected from that tray.
  5. Long-chain hydrocarbons have high boiling points, so they condense low down in the column (or never evaporate and are drained from the bottom as a residue).
  6. Short-chain hydrocarbons have low boiling points, so they rise to the top and leave as gases.

Uses of Fractions — Fuels and Feedstock

Many of the fractions are used as fuels. The modern way of life depends on fuels from crude oil — petrol, diesel oil, kerosene, heavy fuel oil and liquefied petroleum gases.

Fractions are also processed in the petrochemical industry to produce feedstock (raw materials) for making many other useful materials, such as:

  • solvents (e.g. for paints and glues);
  • lubricants (engine oils, greases);
  • polymers (plastics such as poly(ethene) and poly(propene));
  • detergents.

The vast array of natural and synthetic carbon compounds exists because carbon atoms can form families of similar compounds — chains, branched chains and rings.

When asked to explain how fractional distillation works, you must mention all of: heat/evaporate the crude oil; temperature gradient (hot at the bottom, cool at the top); vapours rise and cool; each fraction condenses at its boiling point and is collected at a different height. "The oil is separated by boiling points" on its own scores very little.

4.7.1.3 Properties of Hydrocarbons

Some properties of hydrocarbons depend on the size of their molecules (the length of the carbon chain). These properties influence how hydrocarbons are used as fuels.

PropertyTrend as chain length increasesReason
Boiling pointIncreasesLarger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces between them, so more energy is needed to separate the molecules.
Viscosity (how thick / slow-flowing a liquid is)IncreasesLong chains have stronger intermolecular forces and can tangle, so they flow less easily.
Flammability (how easily it ignites)DecreasesLong-chain molecules are less volatile — fewer molecules evaporate to mix with oxygen and catch fire.
Volatility (how readily it evaporates)DecreasesHigher boiling point means fewer molecules escape from the liquid at a given temperature.

So short-chain hydrocarbons make the best fuels — they are runny, volatile and very flammable with clean flames. Long-chain fractions are thick, hard to ignite and burn with a smoky flame, which is why there is a much greater demand for short-chain fractions than for long-chain ones.

Do not say that boiling point increases because "the covalent bonds get stronger" — boiling a hydrocarbon does not break any covalent bonds. It overcomes the weak intermolecular forces between molecules, and these get stronger as the molecules get bigger. This is one of the most frequently penalised errors in the whole of GCSE Chemistry.

Combustion of Hydrocarbon Fuels

The combustion of hydrocarbon fuels releases energy (it is exothermic). During combustion, the carbon and hydrogen in the fuel are oxidised.

Complete combustion (plenty of oxygen) of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water:

hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Examples you should be able to balance:

methane:   CH₄  + 2O₂   →  CO₂  + 2H₂O
propane:   C₃H₈ + 5O₂   →  3CO₂ + 4H₂O
butane:   2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ →  8CO₂ + 10H₂O
octane:   2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O

Worked Example 1 — Balancing a Combustion Equation

Question: Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion
          of pentane, C₅H₁₂.

Step 1:  Carbon  — 5 C on the left, so 5CO₂ on the right.
Step 2:  Hydrogen — 12 H on the left, so 6H₂O on the right.
Step 3:  Oxygen on the right = (5 × 2) + (6 × 1) = 16 O atoms
         → 8 O₂ molecules needed on the left.

Answer:  C₅H₁₂ + 8O₂ → 5CO₂ + 6H₂O

Shortcut for alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: O₂ needed = n + (n+1)/2.
If this is a half number, double everything (as for butane above).

Incomplete combustion (when oxygen is limited) produces carbon monoxide and/or soot (carbon particles) as well as water — this is covered in 4.9 Chemistry of the Atmosphere.

4.7.1.4 Cracking and Alkenes

Fractional distillation produces far more long-chain hydrocarbons than are needed, and not enough of the short-chain fractions (such as petrol) that are in high demand. Cracking solves this problem.

Cracking is the breaking down of long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful molecules. It is a thermal decomposition reaction. The products of cracking include alkanes and another type of hydrocarbon called alkenes.

Two Methods of Cracking

MethodConditions
Catalytic crackingThe heavy fraction is vaporised and the vapour is passed over a hot catalyst (a powdered zeolite or aluminium oxide) at around 550 °C.
Steam crackingThe vaporised hydrocarbons are mixed with steam and heated to a very high temperature (around 800–850 °C). No catalyst is needed.

Writing Cracking Equations

In any cracking equation, the number of carbon atoms and hydrogen atoms must balance. Typically one product is a shorter alkane (CnH2n+2) and at least one is an alkene (CnH2n).

C₁₀H₂₂   →   C₈H₁₈    +   C₂H₄
decane       octane       ethene
(alkane)     (alkane,     (alkene, used to
             for petrol)   make poly(ethene))

C₁₂H₂₆   →   C₆H₁₄  +  C₄H₈  +  C₂H₄
                       butene   ethene

Worked Example 2 — Completing a Cracking Equation

Question: Hexadecane, C₁₆H₃₄, is cracked to give octane, C₈H₁₈,
          and one other product. Identify the other product.

Carbon:    16 − 8  = 8
Hydrogen:  34 − 18 = 16
Other product = C₈H₁₆

C₈H₁₆ fits CₙH₂ₙ (16 = 2 × 8), so it is an ALKENE — octene.

Equation: C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₈H₁₆

Alkenes and the Test for Alkenes

Alkenes are more reactive than alkanes because they contain a carbon–carbon double bond. This allows them to be identified using bromine water.

Test for alkenes: shake the substance with bromine water.
• Alkene present: bromine water turns from orange to colourless (it is decolourised).
• Alkane: bromine water stays orange (no reaction).
The colour change is orange to colourless. Never write "clear" — clear means transparent, and orange bromine water is already clear. Writing "clear" loses the mark every time.

Why Cracking Is Useful

  • It converts low-demand long-chain fractions into high-demand shorter alkanes such as petrol — matching supply to demand.
  • The alkenes produced are used to make polymers (e.g. ethene → poly(ethene); propene → poly(propene)) and as starting materials for the production of many other chemicals (e.g. ethanol by hydration of ethene).
  • Students should be able to use data on supply and demand to explain why cracking is carried out.

Worked Example 3 — Supply and Demand

Fraction        Supply from crude oil (%)    Demand (%)
Gases                    2                       4
Petrol                  16                      27
Kerosene                13                       8
Diesel                  19                      23
Fuel oil & bitumen      50                      38

Question: Use the data to explain why cracking is needed.

Answer: Demand for petrol (27%) is greater than the supply from
        fractional distillation (16%). Supply of fuel oil and
        bitumen (50%) exceeds demand (38%). Cracking converts the
        surplus long-chain fuel oil into shorter-chain molecules
        such as petrol, so supply better matches demand. It also
        produces alkenes needed to make polymers.

4.7.2 Reactions of Alkenes and Alcohols (Chemistry only)

4.7.2.1 Structure and Formulae of Alkenes

Alkenes are hydrocarbons with a double carbon–carbon bond (C=C). Their general formula is CnH2n.

Alkene molecules are unsaturated because they contain two fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane with the same number of carbon atoms. The C=C double bond is the functional group of the alkenes.
NameMolecular formulaCondensed structural formula
EtheneC2H4CH2=CH2
PropeneC3H6CH2=CHCH3
ButeneC4H8CH2=CHCH2CH3 (but-1-ene) or CH3CH=CHCH3 (but-2-ene)
PenteneC5H10CH2=CHCH2CH2CH3 (pent-1-ene)

There is no "methene" — you need at least two carbon atoms to make a C=C double bond.

4.7.2.2 Reactions of Alkenes

Alkenes are hydrocarbons, so they burn in oxygen; and because they contain the reactive C=C double bond they undergo addition reactions.

Combustion of Alkenes

C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O       (complete combustion of ethene)

Alkenes tend to burn in air with smoky (sooty) yellow flames because they usually undergo incomplete combustion — they have a higher proportion of carbon than the corresponding alkanes, so there is often not enough oxygen to oxidise all the carbon.

Addition Reactions

In an addition reaction, atoms are added across the C=C double bond. The double bond opens up to become a single bond, and one new atom (or group) joins to each of the two carbon atoms. Only one product is formed.

Uses of Alcohols

  • Fuels — ethanol is blended with petrol (e.g. E10 petrol is up to 10% ethanol); methanol is used as a fuel in some racing cars.
  • Solvents — ethanol dissolves many substances that water cannot (e.g. in perfumes, aftershaves, mouthwashes, inks); methanol is in some industrial solvents.
  • Alcoholic drinks — the main alcohol in alcoholic drinks is ethanol.

Making Ethanol by Fermentation

Aqueous solutions of ethanol are produced when sugar solutions are fermented using yeast.

glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2

Conditions needed for fermentation:

  • Yeast (contains the enzymes that catalyse the reaction).
  • Warm temperature of about 30–35 °C — too cold and the reaction is slow; too hot (above ~40 °C) and the yeast enzymes are denatured.
  • Anaerobic conditions (no air) — if oxygen is present, the ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid (vinegar).
  • Aqueous solution of sugar, at a suitable pH.

Fermentation stops when the ethanol concentration reaches about 14–15%, because the ethanol becomes toxic to the yeast. A more concentrated solution can be obtained by fractional distillation.

The carbon in –COOH is counted in the name. Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH) has two carbons in total, so it comes from ethanol (also two carbons). A common error is to name CH3COOH "methanoic acid" because you see "CH3".

Reactions of Carboxylic Acids

1. Dissolve in water to form acidic solutions (pH below 7), because they release H+ ions.

2. React with carbonates like other acids, to give a salt, water and carbon dioxide (fizzing):

ethanoic acid + sodium carbonate → sodium ethanoate + water + carbon dioxide
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

The salts of ethanoic acid are called ethanoates (e.g. sodium ethanoate, CH3COONa).

3. React with alcohols in the presence of an acid catalyst (a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid) to produce esters and water:

carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester + water

ethanoic acid + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate + water
CH3COOH + C2H5OH → CH3COOC2H5 + H2O

Esters have the functional group –COO–. They are volatile compounds with distinctive, often fruity smells, and are used as flavourings and in perfumes. Ethyl ethanoate is also used as a solvent (e.g. in nail-varnish remover and glues).

Naming esters: the part from the alcohol comes first and ends in -yl; the part from the acid comes second and ends in -oate. So ethanol + ethanoic acid → ethyl ethanoate; methanol + propanoic acid → methyl propanoate. Only ethyl ethanoate is required by name, but the pattern is useful.

Carboxylic Acids Are Weak Acids (HT only)

Carboxylic acids are weak acids: they only partially ionise in aqueous solution. Most of the molecules stay as un-ionised CH3COOH, and a reversible reaction sets up an equilibrium:

CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq)

A strong acid such as hydrochloric acid fully ionises: HCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq).

So, for aqueous solutions of the same concentration, a carboxylic acid has a lower concentration of H+ ions and therefore a higher pH than a strong acid. It also reacts more slowly with metals and carbonates (less vigorous fizzing).

Do not confuse weak with dilute. "Weak" describes the degree of ionisation; "dilute" describes the amount of acid per unit volume. You can have a concentrated solution of a weak acid (e.g. glacial ethanoic acid) and a dilute solution of a strong acid.

4.7.3 Synthetic and Naturally Occurring Polymers (Chemistry only)

4.7.3.1 Addition Polymerisation

Polymers are very large molecules made by joining together many small molecules called monomers.

In addition polymerisation, many alkene monomers join together. The C=C double bond in each monomer opens up and the monomers link into a long chain. The polymer is the only product.

In an addition polymer, the repeating unit has the same atoms as the monomer because no other molecule is formed in the reaction.

4.7.3.2 Condensation Polymerisation (HT only)

Condensation polymerisation involves monomers with two functional groups. When these monomers react, they join together and lose small molecules such as water (or HCl). So there are two products: the polymer and the small molecule.

The simplest condensation polymers are made from two different monomers, each with two of the same functional group. For example, a diol (two –OH groups) reacts with a dicarboxylic acid (two –COOH groups) to form a polyester. Each link is an ester link (–COO–), and one molecule of water is lost each time a link forms.

Exam questions use a simplified representation in which the carbon chain between the functional groups is shown as a box:

4.7.3.3 Amino Acids (HT only)

Amino acids have two different functional groups in the same molecule: an amine group (–NH2) and a carboxylic acid group (–COOH).

Amino acids react by condensation polymerisation to produce polypeptides. The –NH2 of one amino acid reacts with the –COOH of the next, eliminating water.

The simplest amino acid is glycine, H2NCH2COOH:

glycine:          H   H   O
                   \  |  //
                    N—C—C
                   /  |  \
                  H   H   O—H

Polymerisation of glycine:

n H₂NCH₂COOH   →   —(— HNCH₂COO —)—ₙ   +   n H₂O
                       (shown as —(—NH—CH₂—C(=O)—)—ₙ)

Because one amino acid molecule has both functional groups, only one type of monomer is needed, and n water molecules are lost for n monomers. Different amino acids can be combined in the same chain to produce proteins. There are about 20 different naturally occurring amino acids; the order in which they are joined determines the protein's shape and function (e.g. enzymes, haemoglobin, keratin).

Higher-tier questions often give you an unfamiliar monomer and ask you to say whether it forms an addition or a condensation polymer. Look for a C=C (addition) or for two reactive groups such as –OH, –COOH or –NH2 (condensation, with loss of water).

4.7.3.4 DNA and Other Naturally Occurring Polymers

Many important biological molecules are polymers. You should be able to name the types of monomers from which these naturally occurring polymers are made.

Natural polymerMonomersNotes
DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)NucleotidesEncodes genetic instructions for the development and functioning of living organisms and viruses.
ProteinsAmino acidsCondensation polymers (polypeptides); enzymes, antibodies, muscle, hair.
StarchGlucose (a sugar)Energy store in plants.
CelluloseGlucose (a sugar)Structural material in plant cell walls.

The Structure of DNA

  • DNA is a large molecule essential for life. It encodes the genetic instructions for the development and functioning of living organisms and viruses.
  • Most DNA molecules are two polymer chains, made from four different monomers called nucleotides, in the form of a double helix.
  • Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group and one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G).
  • The two strands are held together by weak bonds between complementary bases: A pairs with T and C pairs with G.
Exam questions on natural polymers are usually short recall: "Name the monomer of starch" (glucose / sugar), "Name the monomers in DNA" (nucleotides), "Name the monomers in proteins" (amino acids). Make sure you know which is which — and that DNA has two strands in a double helix.
