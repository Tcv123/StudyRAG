4.10 Using Resources

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 2

Industries use the Earth's natural resources to manufacture useful products. In order to operate sustainably, chemists seek to minimise the use of limited resources, the use of energy, waste and environmental impact in the manufacture of these products. Chemists also aim to develop ways of disposing of products at the end of their useful life in ways that ensure that materials and stored energy are utilised. Pollution, disposal of waste products and changing land use have a significant effect on the environment, and environmental chemists study how human activity has affected the Earth's natural cycles. Sections marked (Chemistry only) are not in Combined Science; content marked (HT only) is Higher Tier.

4.10.1 Using the Earth's Resources and Obtaining Potable Water

4.10.1.1 Using the Earth's Resources and Sustainable Development

Humans use the Earth's resources to provide warmth, shelter, food and transport.

  • Natural resources, supplemented by agriculture, provide food, timber, clothing and fuels (e.g. cotton, wool, wood, rubber).
  • Finite resources from the Earth, oceans and atmosphere are processed to provide energy and materials (e.g. crude oil, metal ores, limestone).
  • Chemistry plays an important role in improving agricultural and industrial processes to provide new products and in sustainable development.
  • In some cases, synthetic products can replace or supplement natural products (e.g. synthetic rubber for natural rubber, polyester and nylon for wool and cotton, synthetic fertilisers for manure).
Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
Finite (non-renewable) resources will run out because they are used much faster than they are formed — e.g. fossil fuels, metal ores.
Renewable resources can be replaced at the same rate as they are used — e.g. timber, crops, fresh water.

Students should be able to state examples of natural products that are supplemented or replaced by agricultural and synthetic products, and distinguish between finite and renewable resources given appropriate information. They should also be able to extract and interpret information about resources from charts, graphs and tables, and use orders of magnitude to evaluate the significance of data.

The AQA definition of sustainable development is examined word-for-word. Learn it exactly: "meeting the needs of current generations without compromising the potential of future generations to meet their own needs."

4.10.1.2 Potable Water

Potable water is water that is safe to drink. It is not pure water in the chemical sense because it contains dissolved substances.

Water of appropriate quality is essential for life. For humans, drinking water should have sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and microbes.
"Potable water is pure water" is wrong. Potable water contains dissolved salts (e.g. calcium and magnesium ions, small amounts of chlorine) — it is a mixture. Pure water in chemistry means only H2O molecules.

Methods to Produce Potable Water

Methods used depend on available supplies of water and local conditions.

In the United Kingdom (UK), rain provides water with low levels of dissolved substances (fresh water) that collects in the ground and in lakes and rivers. Most potable water is produced by:

  1. Choosing an appropriate source of fresh water — ground water from aquifers (porous rock), reservoirs, lakes or rivers.
  2. Passing the water through filter beds — to remove any solids (leaves, twigs, sand, grit).
  3. Sterilising — to kill microbes. Sterilising agents used for potable water include chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light.

Desalination of Sea Water

If supplies of fresh water are limited (e.g. in dry countries such as those in the Middle East), desalination of salty water or sea water may be required. Desalination can be done by:

MethodHow it worksDisadvantages
DistillationSea water is boiled; the water evaporates leaving the salts behind; the steam is condensed to give pure water.Requires large amounts of energy to boil the water — expensive; usually uses fossil fuels.
Reverse osmosisSea water is forced under high pressure through a membrane that lets water molecules pass through but holds back the dissolved salts.Requires large amounts of energy to create the high pressure; membranes are expensive and must be replaced.

Both desalination processes are therefore expensive and are only used where fresh water is scarce. The water produced may need minerals added back and sterilisation before drinking.

Required Practical — Analysis and Purification of Water Samples

Aim: analyse and purify water samples from different sources, including pH, dissolved solids and distillation.

  1. pH: test each sample with universal indicator (compare with colour chart) or a calibrated pH meter.
  2. Mass of dissolved solids: weigh an empty evaporating basin; add a measured volume (e.g. 10 cm3) of the water; heat gently to evaporate the water; reweigh. The increase in mass is the mass of dissolved solids.
  3. Distillation: heat salty water in a flask; the steam passes into a condenser (cold water flowing in at the bottom and out at the top) and the condensed pure water is collected. Test the distillate: its pH should be 7, it should leave no residue when evaporated, and it should boil at 100 °C.
In the distillation set-up, cold water enters the condenser at the bottom (lower end) and leaves at the top, so the condenser jacket stays completely full of cold water. The thermometer bulb is at the side-arm, measuring the temperature of the vapour — 100 °C confirms the distillate is water.

Worked Example 1 — Dissolved Solids

Question: 25.0 cm³ of a water sample was evaporated to dryness.
          Mass of empty basin = 41.62 g
          Mass of basin + residue = 41.67 g
          Calculate the concentration of dissolved solids in g/dm³.

Mass of solids = 41.67 − 41.62 = 0.05 g
Volume         = 25.0 cm³ = 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm³
Concentration  = mass ÷ volume = 0.05 ÷ 0.0250 = 2.0 g/dm³

Answer:  2.0 g/dm³
(Note: such a small mass difference has a large percentage
 uncertainty — using a larger volume would improve accuracy.)

4.10.1.3 Waste Water Treatment

Urban lifestyles and industrial processes produce large amounts of waste water that require treatment before being released into the environment.

  • Sewage and agricultural waste water require removal of organic matter and harmful microbes.
  • Industrial waste water may require removal of organic matter and harmful chemicals (e.g. heavy metals, detergents).

Sewage treatment includes:

  1. Screening and grit removal — large objects (rags, plastics, twigs) and grit/sand are removed.
  2. Sedimentation — in large settlement tanks, heavier solids sink to produce sewage sludge; the lighter liquid on top is called effluent.
  3. Anaerobic digestion of sewage sludge — bacteria break down the organic matter in the sludge without oxygen. This produces biogas (mainly methane) that can be burned to generate electricity, and digested waste that can be used as fertiliser.
  4. Aerobic biological treatment of effluent — air is bubbled through the effluent so that aerobic bacteria break down the remaining organic matter and microbes. The clean water can then be released into rivers or the sea.

Comparing Sources of Potable Water

SourceProcesses neededEnergy / cost
Ground water / fresh waterFiltration and sterilisationLowest — the easiest to treat
Waste waterScreening, sedimentation, biological treatment, then further treatment and sterilisationMedium — more stages; public may be reluctant to drink recycled sewage water
Salt water (sea water)Desalination (distillation or reverse osmosis), then treatmentHighest — huge energy demand
Students should be able to comment on the relative ease of obtaining potable water from waste, ground and salt water. The usual ranking is ground water easiest, then waste water, then salt water (most energy-intensive). Always justify with energy and number of processes.

4.10.1.4 Alternative Methods of Extracting Metals (HT only)

The Earth's resources of metal ores are limited. Copper ores are becoming scarce, and new ways of extracting copper from low-grade ores (ores containing only small amounts of copper) are being researched to limit the environmental impacts of traditional mining (digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock).

Copper can be extracted from low-grade ores using biological methods:

MethodHow it works
PhytominingUses plants to absorb metal compounds from the soil. The plants are harvested and then burned to produce ash that contains metal compounds. The metal compounds are then processed to obtain the metal.
BioleachingUses bacteria to produce leachate solutions that contain metal compounds (e.g. copper ions in solution).

The metal compounds obtained by phytomining and bioleaching can then be processed to obtain the metal. For example, copper can be obtained from solutions of copper compounds by:

  • Displacement using scrap iron (iron is more reactive than copper): Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu
    Ionic: Fe(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Fe2+(aq) + Cu(s)
  • Electrolysis — copper is deposited at the negative electrode (cathode): Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu
AdvantagesDisadvantages
PhytominingExtracts metal from low-grade ores and contaminated land; less damage than mining; less waste rock; plants are renewable; can clean up polluted soilVery slow — plants take time to grow; depends on weather/seasons
BioleachingUses low-grade ores; less energy than smelting; no need to dig/move large amounts of rockSlow; leachate can be toxic and acidic if it escapes into the environment
The examiner wants you to say these methods avoid traditional mining, which involves "digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock". Both methods are slow, so their main justification is that high-grade copper ores are running out. Remember the final step: scrap iron displacement or electrolysis.

4.10.2 Life Cycle Assessment and Recycling

4.10.2.1 Life Cycle Assessment

A life cycle assessment (LCA) is carried out to assess the environmental impact of products in each of these stages:
• extracting and processing raw materials
• manufacturing and packaging
• use and operation during its lifetime
• disposal at the end of its useful life, including transport and distribution at each stage.

Limitations of LCAs

  • Use of water, resources, energy sources and production of some wastes can be fairly easily quantified (measured with numbers).
  • Allocating numerical values to pollutant effects is less straightforward and requires value judgements, so LCA is not a purely objective process.
  • Selective or abbreviated LCAs can be devised to evaluate a product, but these can be misused to reach pre-determined conclusions, e.g. in support of claims for advertising purposes.

Comparing Shopping Bags — an Example LCA

StagePlastic (polythene) carrier bagPaper carrier bag
Raw materialsCrude oil (finite) — fractional distillation, cracking, polymerisationTimber (renewable) — but trees must be felled and pulped
ManufactureLower energy; little waste; bags are lightPulping and processing use lots of energy and water; produce lots of waste
UseStrong; can be reused many times; waterproofUsually used only once; weak when wet
DisposalNot biodegradable — persists in landfill for decades; litter harms wildlife; can be recycledBiodegradable; non-toxic; easily recycled

Data per bag:                        Plastic bag    Paper bag

Question: Use the data to compare the two bags.

Answer:  The paper bag produces over 3 times more solid waste

LCA questions nearly always give you a data table. Make direct numerical comparisons ("x is three times higher than y"), cover all four stages, and finish with a conclusion. For "evaluate" questions, also mention a limitation — e.g. the values for pollution involve value judgements, or the LCA may have been commissioned by a company with a vested interest.

4.10.2.2 Ways of Reducing the Use of Resources

The reduction in use, reuse and recycling of materials by end users reduces the use of limited resources, use of energy sources, waste and environmental impacts.

  • Metals, glass, building materials, clay ceramics and most plastics are produced from limited raw materials.
  • Much of the energy for the processes comes from limited resources (fossil fuels).
  • Obtaining raw materials from the Earth by quarrying and mining causes environmental impacts — noise, dust, destruction of habitats, visual scarring, waste heaps.
MaterialReuse / recycling method
GlassGlass bottles can be reused (washed and refilled). Glass that is not reused is crushed and melted to make different glass products. Products that do not need clear glass (e.g. green bottles) can be made from mixed glass.
MetalsRecycled by melting and recasting or reforming into different products. The amount of separation required depends on the metal and the product — e.g. scrap steel can be added to iron from a blast furnace to reduce the amount of iron that needs to be extracted from iron ore.
PlasticsSorted by type, washed, shredded, melted and remoulded (thermosoftening plastics only).

Advantages and Disadvantages of Recycling

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Conserves finite raw materials (ores, crude oil, sand)Collection and transport of waste uses energy and costs money
Uses less energy than extraction (recycling aluminium uses about 5% of the energy needed to extract it from bauxite)Sorting materials is labour-intensive and can be expensive
Less mining/quarrying — less environmental damageQuality of recycled material may be lower (e.g. some plastics degrade)
Less waste going to landfillSome materials (e.g. mixed composites) are hard to separate

4.10.3 Using Materials (Chemistry only)

4.10.3.1 Corrosion and Its Prevention

Corrosion is the destruction of materials by chemical reactions with substances in the environment.

Rusting is an example of corrosion. Both air (oxygen) and water are necessary for iron to rust. 
iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)
4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) + 2xH₂O(l) → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O(s)

Rusting is an oxidation reaction — iron atoms lose electrons:
Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻

Experiment to Show That Air and Water Are Needed

Boiling the water in tube B removes dissolved air; the oil layer stops air re-dissolving. Calcium chloride in tube C absorbs water vapour from the air. Only nail A, with both air and water, rusts.

Preventing Corrosion

Corrosion can be prevented by applying a coating that acts as a barrier, or by sacrificial protection.

MethodHow it worksExamples
Barrier methods — greasing, painting, electroplating, oiling, plastic coatingStop air and/or water reaching the metal surface. If the barrier is scratched, the exposed metal will corrode.Painting car bodies and bridges; greasing bicycle chains; electroplating tin onto steel cans; chromium plating
Sacrificial protectionA more reactive metal is attached to or coats the iron. The more reactive metal loses electrons (oxidises) in preference to iron, so it corrodes instead of the iron. Works even if the surface is scratched.Blocks of magnesium or zinc bolted to ships' hulls, pipelines and oil rigs
GalvanisingCoating iron/steel with a layer of zinc. Acts as both a barrier and sacrificial protection — if scratched, the zinc still corrodes in preference to the iron.Buckets, nails, fences, car bodies, lamp posts

Aluminium is a reactive metal but does not corrode significantly, because it has a thin oxide coating (Al2O3) that forms naturally and protects the metal from further corrosion.

To explain sacrificial protection you must use reactivity and electrons: "Zinc is more reactive than iron, so zinc loses electrons / is oxidised in preference to iron." Saying "zinc rusts instead" is wrong — only iron rusts; other metals corrode.

4.10.3.2 Alloys as Useful Materials

Most metals in everyday use are alloys — mixtures of a metal with other elements (usually other metals or carbon). Pure metals are often too soft for many uses because their layers of atoms slide over each other easily. In an alloy, the different-sized atoms distort the layers, making it harder for them to slide, so alloys are harder and stronger.

AlloyCompositionPropertiesUses
BronzeCopper and tinHard, strong, resistant to corrosionStatues, medals, bells, ship propellers
BrassCopper and zincHarder than copper, workable, gold-colouredWater taps, door fittings, musical instruments, electrical plugs
Gold alloysGold with silver, copper and zincHarder than pure goldJewellery

Low carbon steel (mild steel)Iron with a low proportion of carbonSofter and more easily shapedCar bodies, girders, wire, food cans
Stainless steelIron with chromium and nickel (and carbon)Hard and resistant to corrosionCutlery, sinks, surgical instruments, cookware
Aluminium alloysAluminium with e.g. copper, magnesiumLow density, strongAircraft, bicycles, high-performance cars

Carats — Measuring the Purity of Gold

The proportion of gold in an alloy is measured in carats. 24 carat is 100% (pure) gold.

percentage of gold = (carat number ÷ 24) × 100
So 18 carat gold is 75% gold (18 ÷ 24 = 0.75).

Worked Example 3 — Carats

Question: (a) What percentage of a 9 carat gold ring is gold?
          (b) The ring has a mass of 4.8 g. What mass of gold
              does it contain?

(a) % gold = (9 ÷ 24) × 100 = 37.5%

(b) mass of gold = 4.8 × 0.375 = 1.8 g

Answer:  (a) 37.5%    (b) 1.8 g
You will not be asked to recall the composition of every alloy, but you should know bronze = copper + tin, brass = copper + zinc, stainless steel contains chromium and nickel, and the difference between high and low carbon steels. Questions often give you data on unfamiliar alloys and ask you to choose one for a given use — match a property to the use every time.

4.10.3.3 Ceramics, Polymers and Composites

Glass

Most of the glass we use is soda-lime glass, made by heating a mixture of sand, sodium carbonate and limestone. Borosilicate glass, made from sand and boron trioxide, melts at higher temperatures than soda-lime glass, so it is used for laboratory glassware and ovenware (e.g. Pyrex).

Clay Ceramics

Clay ceramics, including pottery and bricks, are made by shaping wet clay and then heating it in a furnace (a kiln). They are hard, brittle, resistant to heat and electrical insulators.

Polymers

The properties of polymers depend on what monomers they are made from and the conditions under which they are made.

In a "suggest which material" question, quote the relevant data from the table you are given (e.g. density, strength, cost) and link each property to a requirement of the use.

4.10.4 The Haber Process and the Use of NPK Fertilisers (Chemistry only)

4.10.4.1 The Haber Process

The Haber process is used to manufacture ammonia, which can be used to produce nitrogen-based fertilisers.

nitrogen + hydrogen ⇌ ammonia
N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)

The reaction is reversible. The forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH = −92 kJ/mol).

Raw Materials

  • Nitrogen is obtained from the air (fractional distillation of liquefied air).
  • Hydrogen may be obtained from natural gas (methane) or other sources, e.g. by reacting methane with steam: CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2.

The Process

  1. The purified gases are passed over a catalyst of iron at a high temperature (about 450 °C) and a high pressure (about 200 atmospheres).
  2. Some of the hydrogen and nitrogen reacts to form ammonia. The reaction is reversible, so some of the ammonia produced breaks down into nitrogen and hydrogen.
  3. On cooling, the ammonia liquefies and is removed (ammonia has a much higher boiling point than N2 and H2).
  4. The remaining unreacted hydrogen and nitrogen are recycled back into the reactor.

Choosing the Conditions (HT only)

Students should be able to interpret graphs of reaction conditions versus rate and apply the principles of dynamic equilibrium (from 4.6.2) to explain how the commercially used conditions are a compromise between rate, equilibrium position (yield) and cost/energy.

ConditionEffect on equilibrium yieldEffect on rateOther considerationsCompromise chosen
TemperatureForward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right → higher yieldLower temperature → slower rate (fewer successful collisions; catalyst less effective)Too slow at low temperature to be economic~450 °C — reasonable yield at an acceptable rate
Pressure4 molecules of gas on the left, 2 on the right, so higher pressure shifts equilibrium to the side with fewer molecules (right) → higher yieldHigher pressure → gas particles closer together → more frequent collisions → fasterVery high pressures are expensive (strong pipes and vessels, energy for compressors) and dangerous~200 atm — high yield and rate without excessive cost or risk
Catalyst (iron)No effect on the position of equilibrium or the yieldIncreases the rate of both forward and reverse reactions equally — equilibrium is reached fasterAllows a lower temperature to be used economicallyIron catalyst used
For a 6-mark Haber question, structure your answer as temperature → pressure → catalyst, and for each one state the effect on yield (with the equilibrium reason) and on rate, then explain the compromise. Key phrases: "exothermic forward reaction", "fewer moles/molecules of gas on the right", "a catalyst does not change the yield". Mention that recycling unreacted gases gives an overall conversion of about 97% even though each pass converts only about 15%.

4.10.4.2 Production and Uses of NPK Fertilisers

Compounds of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are used as fertilisers to improve agricultural productivity. Plants need these elements for healthy growth, and crops remove them from the soil each year.

NPK fertilisers contain compounds of all three elements — Nitrogen, Phosphorus and K (potassium) — in appropriate percentages. They are formulations of various salts containing appropriate percentages of the elements.
ElementNeeded forSource used in fertilisers
Nitrogen (N)Making proteins; healthy leaf growthAmmonia from the Haber process → ammonium salts and nitrates (e.g. ammonium nitrate, ammonium sulfate)
Phosphorus (P)Root growth; DNA and energy transferPhosphate rock, which is mined and then treated with acids
Potassium (K)Fruit and flower growth; resistance to diseasePotassium chloride and potassium sulfate, which are mined and can be used directly

Industrial Production of Fertiliser Salts

Ammonia can be used to manufacture ammonium salts and nitric acid:

Nitric acid is made by oxidising ammonia (several stages):
   ammonia + oxygen → ... → nitric acid

Ammonium nitrate (nitrogen fertiliser):
   ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate
   NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃

Ammonium sulfate:
   ammonia + sulfuric acid → ammonium sulfate
   2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

Ammonium phosphate:
   ammonia + phosphoric acid → ammonium phosphate
   3NH₃ + H₃PO₄ → (NH₄)₃PO₄

Phosphate rock is insoluble, so it cannot be used directly as a fertiliser — plants can only absorb soluble compounds. It is treated with acids:

Phosphate rock treated with…Products
Nitric acidPhosphoric acid and calcium nitrate
Sulfuric acidSingle superphosphate — a mixture of calcium phosphate and calcium sulfate
Phosphoric acidTriple superphosphate (calcium phosphate)

Laboratory vs Industrial Production of Ammonium Sulfate

Students should be able to compare the industrial production of fertilisers with laboratory preparations of the same compounds.

FeatureLaboratory preparationIndustrial production
Raw materialsDilute ammonia solution and dilute sulfuric acid bought from suppliersRaw materials made on site: ammonia from the Haber process; sulfuric acid from sulfur (Contact process)
MethodTitration to find exact volumes, then mix, evaporate some water and leave to crystalliseReactants react in a large reaction vessel; the hot concentrated solution is sprayed into a tower to dry and crystallise

Process typeBatchContinuous
Number of stagesFew (one reaction)Several stages (making ammonia, making sulfuric acid, then neutralisation)
Concentration of reactantsDiluteConcentrated

Worked Example 4 — Percentage of Nitrogen in a Fertiliser

Question: Compare the percentage by mass of nitrogen in
          ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, and ammonium sulfate,
          (NH₄)₂SO₄.  (Ar: H = 1, N = 14, O = 16, S = 32)

NH₄NO₃:   Mr = 14 + 4(1) + 14 + 3(16) = 80
          mass of N = 2 × 14 = 28
          % N = (28 ÷ 80) × 100 = 35%

(NH₄)₂SO₄: Mr = 2(14 + 4) + 32 + 4(16) = 132
          mass of N = 2 × 14 = 28
          % N = (28 ÷ 132) × 100 = 21.2%

Answer:  Ammonium nitrate contains a much higher percentage of
         nitrogen (35%) than ammonium sulfate (21.2%), so less
         mass is needed to supply the same amount of nitrogen.
Remember which raw material provides which element: N from the Haber process (ammonia), P from phosphate rock (treated with acid because the rock is insoluble), K from mined potassium chloride and potassium sulfate. The three phosphate-rock reactions are classic 1-mark recall questions — especially "single superphosphate = calcium phosphate + calcium sulfate".
