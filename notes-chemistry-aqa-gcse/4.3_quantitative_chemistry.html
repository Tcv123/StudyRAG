4.3 Quantitative Chemistry

AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) — Paper 1

Chemists use quantitative analysis to determine the formulae of compounds and the equations for reactions. Given this information, they can work out the quantities of reactants needed to make a certain amount of product, which matters enormously in industry where wasted chemicals cost money and harm the environment. This topic is calculation-heavy — roughly 15–20% of the marks on Paper 1 involve the mathematics here, and it underpins energy changes, rates and electrolysis questions too.

4.3.1 Conservation of Mass and the Quantitative Interpretation of Chemical Equations

4.3.1.1 Conservation of Mass and Balanced Chemical Equations

The law of conservation of mass states that no atoms are lost or made during a chemical reaction, so the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants.

This means that chemical reactions can be represented by symbol equations which are balanced in terms of the numbers of atoms of each element involved on both sides of the equation. Atoms are only rearranged into new substances; the total number of each type of atom stays the same.

Example:   2Mg(s)  +  O₂(g)  →  2MgO(s)
Atoms:     Mg: 2      O: 2      Mg: 2, O: 2   ✓ balanced

Masses:    2 × 24  +  2 × 16  =  2 × (24 + 16)
           48 g    +  32 g    =  80 g          ✓ mass conserved

4.3.1.2 Relative Formula Mass

The relative formula mass (Mr) of a compound is the sum of the relative atomic masses of the atoms in the numbers shown in the formula. It has no units.

In a balanced chemical equation, the sum of the relative formula masses of the reactants in the quantities shown equals the sum of the relative formula masses of the products in the quantities shown.

Relative atomic masses (Ar) are read from the periodic table. Values used in this topic:

ElementHCNONaMgAlSClKCaFeCu
Ar11214162324273235.539405663.5

Worked Example 1 — Calculating Mr

(a) Carbon dioxide, CO₂
    Mr = 12 + (2 × 16) = 12 + 32 = 44

(b) Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃
    Mr = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100

(c) Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂
    The bracket multiplies everything inside by 2:
    Mr = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74

(d) Ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄
    Mr = 2 × (14 + 4 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16)
       = 2 × 18 + 32 + 64
       = 36 + 32 + 64 = 132

(e) Hydrated copper sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O
    Mr = 63.5 + 32 + 64 + 5 × 18 = 159.5 + 90 = 249.5

Percentage by Mass of an Element in a Compound

% by mass of element = (Ar × number of atoms of that element) ÷ Mr of compound × 100 
Question: Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in
          ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (a fertiliser).

Mr of NH₄NO₃ = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80
Mass of N    = 2 × 14 = 28
% N          = 28 ÷ 80 × 100 = 35%

Answer:  35%
The most common error with Mr is mishandling brackets — in Ca(OH)₂, the 2 multiplies both O and H. Another is forgetting that the big number in front of a formula in an equation (e.g. 2H₂O) is not part of the Mr: Mr of H₂O is always 18. Only multiply by the coefficient when you are checking that total masses balance.

4.3.1.3 Mass Changes when a Reactant or Product is a Gas

Some reactions appear to involve a change in mass, but this can usually be explained because a reactant or product is a gas and its mass has not been taken into account. If the reaction were carried out in a sealed (closed) container, the mass would not change.

SituationExampleObserved mass change (open container)Explanation
A gas is a reactantMetal + oxygen → metal oxide
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)		IncreasesOxygen atoms from the air combine with the metal; the mass of the oxygen is now included in the solid product.
A gas is a productThermal decomposition of a metal carbonate
CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)		DecreasesCarbon dioxide escapes into the atmosphere, so its mass is no longer measured.
A gas is a productAcid + carbonate
CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂		DecreasesCO₂ escapes from the open flask.
No gas involvedPrecipitation
BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)		No changeAll products remain in the container.

Worked Example 2 — Interpreting a Mass-Loss Graph

When marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with hydrochloric acid in an open flask on a balance, the mass falls as carbon dioxide escapes. The graph levels off when the reaction stops — when the limiting reactant has been used up. The total mass lost equals the mass of CO₂ produced.

In a sealed flask (e.g. with a balloon over the neck to trap the gas) the balance reading would stay constant throughout — the clearest demonstration that mass is conserved.

When explaining an apparent mass change, always name the gas and say whether it entered or left the open container. Then state that mass is still conserved: "the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants, but the gas was not weighed". In 6-mark practical questions about the crucible experiment, mention lifting the lid occasionally to let oxygen in while preventing magnesium oxide smoke from escaping.

4.3.1.4 Chemical Measurements and Uncertainty

Whenever a measurement is made there is always some uncertainty about the result obtained. The range of a set of repeat measurements about the mean can be used as a measure of uncertainty:

uncertainty = ± range ÷ 2    where    range = highest value − lowest value

Worked Example 3 — Mean and Uncertainty

Question: A student measures the volume of gas produced in four
          repeats: 42.0, 44.5, 43.0, 42.5 cm³. Calculate the mean
          and the uncertainty.

Mean        = (42.0 + 44.5 + 43.0 + 42.5) ÷ 4
            = 172.0 ÷ 4 = 43.0 cm³
Range       = 44.5 − 42.0 = 2.5 cm³
Uncertainty = ± 2.5 ÷ 2 = ± 1.25 cm³ (≈ ± 1.3 cm³)

Answer:  43.0 ± 1.3 cm³

Sources of uncertainty include the resolution of the measuring instrument (a balance reading to 0.01 g, a burette to 0.05 cm³), random errors in reading scales, and variations in conditions. Any obvious anomalous results should be identified and left out of the mean. You should also be able to represent the distribution of results and make estimations of uncertainty.

4.3.2 Use of Amount of Substance in Relation to Masses of Pure Substances

4.3.2.1 Moles (Higher)

Chemical amounts are measured in moles. The symbol for the unit mole is mol.

The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its relative formula mass (or relative atomic mass for an element).

One mole of a substance contains the same number of the stated particles — atoms, molecules or ions — as one mole of any other substance. The number of atoms, molecules or ions in a mole of a given substance is the Avogadro constant, which has the value 6.02 × 10²³ per mole.
number of moles (mol) = mass (g) ÷ Mr

number of particles = number of moles × 6.02 × 10²³

Worked Example 4 — Mass to Moles to Particles (Higher)

Question: (a) How many moles are in 22 g of carbon dioxide?
          (b) How many molecules is this?
          (c) How many atoms in total?

(a) Mr(CO₂) = 12 + 2 × 16 = 44
    moles   = mass ÷ Mr = 22 ÷ 44 = 0.50 mol

(b) molecules = 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules

(c) Each CO₂ molecule contains 3 atoms:
    atoms = 3 × 3.01 × 10²³ = 9.03 × 10²³ atoms

Worked Example 5 — Mass of a Single Atom (Higher)

Question: Calculate the mass in grams of one atom of carbon-12.

1 mol of carbon-12 has a mass of 12 g and contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms.
mass of one atom = 12 ÷ (6.02 × 10²³)
                 = 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g

Answer:  1.99 × 10⁻²³ g (to 3 s.f.)
Read particle questions carefully: "molecules", "atoms" and "ions" give different answers. One mole of Cl₂ contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules but 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms. One mole of CaCl₂ contains 3 moles of ions (1 Ca²⁺ + 2 Cl⁻). Give answers in standard form to the requested significant figures.

4.3.2.2 Amounts of Substances in Equations (Higher)

The masses of reactants and products can be calculated from balanced symbol equations. Chemical equations can be interpreted in terms of moles: for example, Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ shows that 1 mol of magnesium reacts with 2 mol of hydrochloric acid to produce 1 mol of magnesium chloride and 1 mol of hydrogen gas.

Reacting-mass method (three steps):
1. Convert the known mass into moles (moles = mass ÷ Mr).
2. Use the balanced equation ratio to find moles of the substance you want.
3. Convert those moles back to mass (mass = moles × Mr).

Worked Example 6 — Reacting Masses (Higher)

Question: Calculate the mass of calcium oxide produced when 50 g of
          calcium carbonate is completely decomposed by heating.
          CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Step 1: Mr(CaCO₃) = 100        moles CaCO₃ = 50 ÷ 100 = 0.50 mol
Step 2: Ratio CaCO₃ : CaO = 1 : 1        moles CaO = 0.50 mol
Step 3: Mr(CaO) = 40 + 16 = 56  mass CaO = 0.50 × 56 = 28 g

Answer:  28 g of calcium oxide

Worked Example 7 — Reacting Masses with a 2 : 1 Ratio (Higher)

Question: Iron(III) oxide is reduced by carbon monoxide in a blast
          furnace:  Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
          What mass of iron can be made from 800 kg of Fe₂O₃?

Step 1: Mr(Fe₂O₃) = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 = 160
        800 kg = 800 000 g
        moles Fe₂O₃ = 800 000 ÷ 160 = 5000 mol
Step 2: Ratio Fe₂O₃ : Fe = 1 : 2    moles Fe = 10 000 mol
Step 3: mass Fe = 10 000 × 56 = 560 000 g = 560 kg

Answer:  560 kg of iron

Shortcut: masses can be kept in kg (or tonnes) throughout,
because the units cancel — but be consistent.

4.3.2.3 Using Moles to Balance Equations (Higher)

The balancing numbers in a symbol equation can be calculated from the masses of reactants and products by converting the masses in grams to amounts in moles and converting the numbers of moles to simple whole number ratios.

Worked Example 8 — Balancing from Masses (Higher)

Question: 4.8 g of magnesium reacts with 3.2 g of oxygen (O₂) to
          produce 8.0 g of magnesium oxide. Use these masses to
          balance the equation  __Mg + __O₂ → __MgO

                 Mg              O₂               MgO
Mass (g)         4.8             3.2              8.0
Mr               24              32               40
Moles            4.8÷24 = 0.20   3.2÷32 = 0.10    8.0÷40 = 0.20
÷ smallest       0.20÷0.10 = 2   0.10÷0.10 = 1    0.20÷0.10 = 2

Answer:  2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

4.3.2.4 Limiting Reactants (Higher)

In a chemical reaction involving two reactants, it is common to use an excess of one of the reactants to ensure that all of the other reactant is used. The reactant that is completely used up is called the limiting reactant because it limits the amount of products.

You should be able to explain the effect of a limiting quantity of a reactant on the amount of products in terms of amounts in moles or masses in grams. If you double the amount of the limiting reactant (keeping the other in excess), you double the amount of product; adding more of the reactant already in excess makes no difference.

Worked Example 9 — Identifying the Limiting Reactant (Higher)

Question: 2.4 g of magnesium is added to a solution containing
          0.15 mol of hydrochloric acid.
          Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
          (a) Which reactant is limiting?
          (b) What mass of hydrogen is produced?

(a) moles Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol
    From the equation, 0.10 mol Mg needs 2 × 0.10 = 0.20 mol HCl.
    Only 0.15 mol HCl is available → HCl runs out first.
    HCl is the LIMITING reactant; magnesium is in excess.

(b) Ratio HCl : H₂ = 2 : 1
    moles H₂ = 0.15 ÷ 2 = 0.075 mol
    Mr(H₂) = 2       mass H₂ = 0.075 × 2 = 0.15 g

Answer:  (a) hydrochloric acid   (b) 0.15 g of hydrogen
To find the limiting reactant, do not just compare the numbers of moles of each reactant — you must take the ratio in the equation into account. In Example 8 there are more moles of HCl (0.15) than Mg (0.10), yet HCl is limiting because twice as much is needed. Always base the product calculation on the limiting reactant.

4.3.2.5 Concentration of Solutions

Many chemical reactions take place in solutions. The concentration of a solution can be measured in mass per given volume of solution, e.g. grams per dm³ (g/dm³).

concentration (g/dm³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)

Volume conversions: 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ (= 1 litre). To convert cm³ to dm³, divide by 1000. You should be able to explain how the mass of a solute and the volume of a solution are related to the concentration: a larger mass of solute in the same volume gives a higher concentration; the same mass in a larger volume gives a lower concentration.

Worked Example 10 — Concentration in g/dm³

Question: 25 g of sodium chloride is dissolved in water to make
          500 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration in g/dm³.

Convert: 500 cm³ = 500 ÷ 1000 = 0.500 dm³
Formula: concentration = mass ÷ volume
Working: concentration = 25 ÷ 0.500 = 50 g/dm³

Answer:  50 g/dm³

Worked Example 11 — Mass of Solute from Concentration

Question: What mass of copper sulfate is dissolved in 75 cm³ of a
          solution of concentration 40 g/dm³?

Convert:  75 cm³ = 0.075 dm³
Rearrange: mass = concentration × volume
Working:   mass = 40 × 0.075 = 3.0 g

Answer:  3.0 g
Nearly every concentration error comes from forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³. If your concentration seems 1000 times too small or too large, check this first. Show the conversion as a separate line — examiners award a method mark for it.

4.3.3 Yield and Atom Economy of Chemical Reactions (Triple Only)

4.3.3.1 Percentage Yield

Even though no atoms are gained or lost in a chemical reaction, it is not always possible to obtain the calculated amount of a product. The amount of product obtained is known as the yield. When compared with the maximum theoretical amount as a percentage, it is called the percentage yield.

% yield = (mass of product actually made ÷ maximum theoretical mass of product) × 100

Reasons why the actual yield is less than the theoretical yield:

  • The reaction may not go to completion because it is reversible (an equilibrium is reached — e.g. the Haber process).
  • Some of the product may be lost when it is separated from the reaction mixture (e.g. left on filter paper, lost in transferring between containers, lost during evaporation).
  • Some of the reactants may react in ways different to the expected reaction (side reactions forming unwanted products).
  • The reactants may be impure.

Worked Example 12 — Percentage Yield

Question: A student heats 10.0 g of calcium carbonate and obtains
          4.2 g of calcium oxide.  CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
          Calculate the percentage yield.

Step 1 (theoretical yield, Higher method):
        moles CaCO₃ = 10.0 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol
        moles CaO   = 0.100 mol (1 : 1)
        max mass CaO = 0.100 × 56 = 5.6 g

   (Foundation method: 100 g CaCO₃ → 56 g CaO,
    so 10.0 g → 5.6 g by scaling.)

Step 2: % yield = 4.2 ÷ 5.6 × 100 = 75%

Answer:  75%

Higher-tier students should be able to calculate the theoretical mass of a product from a given mass of reactant and the balanced equation for the reaction, as in step 1 above.

Worked Example 13 — Percentage Yield in Industry (Higher)

Question: In a test run of the Haber process, 28 tonnes of nitrogen
          react with excess hydrogen and 5.1 tonnes of ammonia are
          collected.  N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
          Calculate the percentage yield.

Mr(N₂) = 28        Mr(NH₃) = 14 + 3 = 17
Ratio N₂ : NH₃ = 1 : 2
28 t N₂ is 1 "tonne-mole"  →  2 "tonne-moles" NH₃
Theoretical mass NH₃ = 2 × 17 = 34 tonnes
% yield = 5.1 ÷ 34 × 100 = 15%

The yield is low mainly because the reaction is reversible —
unreacted N₂ and H₂ are recycled, so little is actually wasted.

4.3.3.2 Atom Economy

The atom economy (atom utilisation) is a measure of the amount of starting materials that end up as useful products. It is calculated using the balanced equation:

atom economy = (Mr of desired product from equation ÷ sum of Mr of all reactants from equation) × 100

It is important for sustainable development and for economic reasons to use reactions with high atom economy: less waste is produced, fewer raw materials are used, and less money is spent dealing with by-products. Remember to multiply each Mr by its coefficient in the balanced equation.

Worked Example 14 — Atom Economy

Question: Calculate the atom economy for making calcium oxide by
          heating calcium carbonate.  CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Mr of reactants = Mr(CaCO₃) = 100
Mr of desired product = Mr(CaO) = 56
Atom economy = 56 ÷ 100 × 100 = 56%

The other 44% ends up as CO₂ — a waste product (and a
greenhouse gas) unless it can be captured and sold.

Worked Example 15 — Comparing Two Routes to Ethanol

Route A — fermentation:
   C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂
   Mr(glucose) = 6×12 + 12×1 + 6×16 = 180
   Mr of 2C₂H₅OH = 2 × 46 = 92
   Atom economy = 92 ÷ 180 × 100 = 51.1%

Route B — hydration of ethene:
   C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH
   Sum of Mr reactants = 28 + 18 = 46
   Mr(C₂H₅OH) = 46
   Atom economy = 46 ÷ 46 × 100 = 100%
FactorFermentationHydration of ethene
Atom economy51% (CO₂ by-product)100% (only one product)
Raw materialSugar from crops — renewableEthene from crude oil — non-renewable
Conditions~30–35 °C, yeast, batch process~300 °C, 60–70 atm, phosphoric acid catalyst, continuous
Rate / puritySlow; dilute ethanol needing distillationFast; purer product

When choosing a reaction pathway, industrial chemists consider atom economy, yield, rate, equilibrium position and the usefulness of by-products. A reaction with high atom economy but very slow rate or low yield may not be the best choice.

Do not confuse percentage yield (based on experimental masses — how much you actually got) with atom economy (based on the balanced equation — the theoretical fraction of atoms that could end up in the product). A reaction can have 100% atom economy but a low percentage yield, e.g. if it is reversible. Addition reactions always have 100% atom economy because there is only one product.

4.3.4 Using Concentrations of Solutions in mol/dm³ (Triple Only, Higher)

The concentration of a solution can be measured in mol/dm³.

concentration (mol/dm³) = amount of solute (mol) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)

The amount in moles of solute or the mass in grams of solute in a given volume of solution can be calculated from its concentration in mol/dm³. To convert: concentration (g/dm³) = concentration (mol/dm³) × Mr.

Titrations

If the volumes of two solutions that react completely are known and the concentration of one solution is known, the concentration of the other solution can be calculated. The volumes are found by titration — this is a required practical for Chemistry (separate science) students.

Titration Method (Required Practical)

  1. Use a volumetric pipette and pipette filler to measure exactly 25.0 cm³ of the alkali into a clean conical flask.
  2. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein: pink in alkali, colourless in acid; or methyl orange: yellow in alkali, red in acid). Place the flask on a white tile.
  3. Fill the burette with acid using a funnel (remove the funnel afterwards); record the initial reading at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus.
  4. Add the acid, swirling the flask. As the end-point approaches (colour starts to change temporarily), add the acid drop by drop.
  5. Stop when the indicator just changes colour permanently (the end-point). Record the final burette reading. The titre = final − initial reading.
  6. The first run is a rough titration. Repeat until you have concordant results (within 0.10 cm³ of each other), then calculate the mean of the concordant titres.

Universal indicator is not used in titrations because it changes colour gradually through many colours and gives no sharp end-point.

Worked Example 16 — Concentration from a Titration (1 : 1)

Question: 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution was neutralised by
          20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid.
          NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
          Calculate the concentration of the NaOH in (a) mol/dm³
          and (b) g/dm³.

Step 1 — moles of the known solution (HCl):
         volume = 20.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0200 dm³
         moles HCl = 0.100 × 0.0200 = 0.00200 mol
Step 2 — use the ratio (NaOH : HCl = 1 : 1):
         moles NaOH = 0.00200 mol
Step 3 — concentration of NaOH:
         volume = 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm³
         conc = 0.00200 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0800 mol/dm³
(b)      Mr(NaOH) = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40
         conc = 0.0800 × 40 = 3.20 g/dm³

Answer:  (a) 0.0800 mol/dm³   (b) 3.20 g/dm³

Worked Example 17 — Titration with a 1 : 2 Ratio

Question: 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide was neutralised
          by a mean titre of 12.50 cm³ of sulfuric acid.
          H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
          Calculate the concentration of the sulfuric acid.

Step 1: moles NaOH = 0.100 × (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00250 mol
Step 2: ratio H₂SO₄ : NaOH = 1 : 2
        moles H₂SO₄ = 0.00250 ÷ 2 = 0.00125 mol
Step 3: conc H₂SO₄ = 0.00125 ÷ (12.50 ÷ 1000)
                   = 0.00125 ÷ 0.01250
                   = 0.100 mol/dm³

Answer:  0.100 mol/dm³

Worked Example 18 — Choosing Concordant Titres

Titration          Rough    1       2       3
Final (cm³)        23.10    45.20   22.75   45.05
Initial (cm³)       0.00    22.65    0.10   22.50
Titre (cm³)        23.10    22.55   22.65   22.55

Concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³): 22.55, 22.65, 22.55
Mean titre = (22.55 + 22.65 + 22.55) ÷ 3 = 22.58 cm³
(the rough titre is ignored)

Worked Example 19 — Making a Solution of Known Concentration

Question: What mass of anhydrous sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, is needed
          to make 250 cm³ of a 0.200 mol/dm³ solution?

Step 1: moles needed = concentration × volume
                     = 0.200 × (250 ÷ 1000) = 0.0500 mol
Step 2: Mr(Na₂CO₃) = 2 × 23 + 12 + 3 × 16 = 106
Step 3: mass = 0.0500 × 106 = 5.30 g

Method: weigh 5.30 g accurately, dissolve in a little distilled
water in a beaker, transfer with rinsings to a 250 cm³ volumetric
flask, make up to the mark and invert to mix.
Titration calculations always follow the same three steps: moles of the solution you know everything about → use the equation ratio → concentration of the unknown. Write down the ratio explicitly. Convert both volumes to dm³. Give burette readings to 2 decimal places (ending in 0 or 5) and never include the rough titre in the mean.

4.3.5 Use of Amount of Substance in Relation to Volumes of Gases (Triple Only, Higher)

Equal amounts in moles of gases occupy the same volume under the same conditions of temperature and pressure. The volume of one mole of any gas at room temperature and pressure (RTP: 20 °C and 1 atmosphere pressure) is 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³).

volume of gas at RTP (dm³) = moles × 24     moles = volume (dm³) ÷ 24

This is true whatever the gas — 1 mol of hydrogen (2 g) and 1 mol of carbon dioxide (44 g) both occupy 24 dm³ at RTP, because the gas particles are so far apart that their own size is negligible compared with the space between them.

Worked Example 20 — Gas Volume from a Mass

Question: Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide, at RTP, produced
          when 5.0 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess acid.
          CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Step 1: moles CaCO₃ = 5.0 ÷ 100 = 0.050 mol
Step 2: ratio CaCO₃ : CO₂ = 1 : 1      moles CO₂ = 0.050 mol
Step 3: volume = 0.050 × 24 = 1.2 dm³  (= 1200 cm³)

Answer:  1.2 dm³

Worked Example 21 — Mass from a Gas Volume

Question: What mass of oxygen gas occupies 600 cm³ at RTP?

Convert: 600 cm³ = 0.600 dm³
moles O₂ = 0.600 ÷ 24 = 0.025 mol
mass O₂  = 0.025 × 32 = 0.80 g

Answer:  0.80 g

Reacting Volumes of Gases

Because equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of moles, the ratio of gas volumes in a reaction is the same as the mole ratio in the balanced equation. You should be able to use the volumes of gaseous reactants and products to deduce the balancing numbers in an equation, and calculate the volumes of gases involved in reactions using the molar gas volume at RTP.

Worked Example 22 — Reacting Gas Volumes

Question: Hydrogen burns in oxygen:  2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
          What volume of oxygen is needed to react completely
          with 50 cm³ of hydrogen? (Same temperature and pressure.)

Ratio H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1
Volume O₂ = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 cm³

Answer:  25 cm³ (no need to use 24 dm³ — the ratio is enough)

Question 2: 20 cm³ of a hydrocarbon gas reacts with exactly
            60 cm³ of oxygen to form 40 cm³ of carbon dioxide.
            Deduce the balancing numbers.

Ratio hydrocarbon : O₂ : CO₂ = 20 : 60 : 40 = 1 : 3 : 2
Equation:  C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O    (ethene fits this ratio)
The 24 dm³ value only applies at RTP (20 °C, 1 atm) and only to gases — never use it for solids, liquids or solutions. It is given on the exam paper when needed, but you should know it. If a question involves only gas volumes, you can use the volume ratio directly without converting to moles at all.
