Topic 4 — Extracting Metals and Equilibria

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 1

This topic has two halves. The first half is about metals: how reactive they are, why some are found as the pure element while most are locked up in ores, and how the position of a metal in the reactivity series decides how we get it out of the ground. It finishes with the bigger picture — biological extraction, recycling and life cycle assessments. The second half introduces reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium, using the Haber process for ammonia as the main example, and (at Higher tier) shows how changing the conditions shifts the position of an equilibrium.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. All of Topic 4 is examined in both GCSE Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science; the chemistry-only extension of the Haber process, fertilisers and yield/atom economy is in Topic 5 (Separate Chemistry 1).

4.1 The Reactivity Series

The reactivity series is a list of metals arranged in order of how vigorously they react. A metal high in the series reacts readily because its atoms have a strong tendency to lose electrons and form positive ions. Carbon and hydrogen are non-metals but are included as reference points.

The order you need for Edexcel is:

A popular mnemonic: Please Stop Calling Me A Careless Zebra, Instead Help Count Some Giraffes — K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, C, Zn, Fe, H, Cu, Ag, Au. Lithium is not in the Edexcel list but, if it appears, it sits between sodium and calcium.

Deducing an order of reactivity from experiments

You need to be able to put metals in order using three kinds of evidence: their reactions with water, with dilute acid, and displacement reactions with solutions of other metal salts. The more vigorous the reaction (more bubbles, faster temperature rise, metal disappears more quickly), the more reactive the metal.

Reactions with water

Reactive metals react with cold water to form a metal hydroxide and hydrogen:

metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen 2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g) Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Less reactive metals such as magnesium, zinc and iron react only very slowly with cold water but react with steam to form a metal oxide and hydrogen:

Mg(s) + H₂O(g) → MgO(s) + H₂(g)

Copper, silver and gold do not react with water or steam.

Reactions with dilute acids

Metals above hydrogen react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid to produce a salt and hydrogen. Metals below hydrogen (copper, silver, gold) do not react with dilute acids.

metal + acid → salt + hydrogen Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

The hydrogen can be confirmed with a lighted splint, which burns with a squeaky pop.

Aluminium often seems less reactive than its position suggests because it is covered by a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide that stops water and acid reaching the metal. If you are asked why, say the oxide layer is protective — not that aluminium is unreactive.

Making it a fair test

When comparing metals with acid, keep the following the same: the concentration and volume of acid, the temperature, and the mass and surface area of metal (e.g. use powders of similar particle size, or same-length strips). Measure something objective, such as the temperature rise in a fixed time or the volume of hydrogen collected in a gas syringe in the first minute.

A student adds 0.5 g of four metal powders, W, X, Y and Z, to 25 cm³ of 1 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid in turn and records the temperature rise.

W: +2 °C   X: +0 °C   Y: +18 °C   Z: +7 °C

The greater the temperature rise, the more energy released and the more vigorous the reaction. Order (most → least reactive): Y > Z > W > X. X may be copper (below hydrogen, no reaction). This is consistent with Y = magnesium, Z = zinc, W = iron.

4.2 Displacement Reactions

In a displacement reaction a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in one of its compounds. A metal will displace any metal below it in the reactivity series from a solution of its salt (or from its oxide when heated).
magnesium + copper sulfate → magnesium sulfate + copper Mg(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Observations: the blue colour of the copper(II) sulfate solution fades, a red-brown/pink coating of copper forms on the magnesium, and the mixture warms up (the reaction is exothermic). If the metal added is less reactive than the one in solution (e.g. copper in magnesium sulfate) there is no reaction.

A grid of results is a quick way to deduce a reactivity order: the metal that displaces the most others is the most reactive.

The thermite reaction

Displacement also works with solid oxides. Aluminium displaces iron from iron(III) oxide in a very exothermic reaction that produces molten iron — used to weld railway lines:

2Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → Al₂O₃(s) + 2Fe(l)

Displacement as a redox reaction (HT)

In terms of electrons, the more reactive metal loses electrons (is oxidised) and the ion of the less reactive metal gains electrons (is reduced). Because oxidation and reduction happen together, displacement reactions are redox reactions.

Oxidation is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons — OIL RIG.

The sulfate ions do not change; they are spectator ions. Leaving them out gives the ionic equation, which can be split into two half equations:

Ionic: Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) Oxidation: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻    |    Reduction: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
(HT) Zinc powder is added to silver nitrate solution. Write the ionic equation and identify what is oxidised and reduced.

Full equation: Zn(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)
Nitrate ions are spectators. Ionic equation: Zn(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)
Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻   (zinc atoms lose electrons → zinc is oxidised)
2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag   (silver ions gain electrons → silver ions are reduced)
Each zinc atom loses 2 electrons but each silver ion gains only 1, so two Ag⁺ ions are needed per Zn atom — the electrons must balance.
When you say what is oxidised or reduced, name the exact particle: "zinc atoms are oxidised" and "silver ions are reduced". "Silver nitrate is reduced" usually loses the mark.

4.3 Why Metals Differ in Reactivity

Metals react by losing their outer electrons to form positive ions. The reactivity series is really a list of how easily each metal does this:

  • Potassium forms K⁺ very easily, so it reacts violently with water.
  • Copper has only a weak tendency to form Cu²⁺, so it does not react with water or dilute acids.
  • Gold has almost no tendency to form ions, which is why it stays shiny for thousands of years.

Resistance to oxidation

Because the less reactive metals have a low tendency to form positive ions, they are more resistant to oxidation. This explains:

  • Gold and silver are used for jewellery and electrical contacts: they do not tarnish (or tarnish only slowly).
  • Copper is used for water pipes: it does not react with water.
  • Iron rusts (is oxidised) quite easily and must be protected.
  • Potassium and sodium oxidise in air within seconds and must be stored under oil.

4.4 Oxidation and Reduction — the Oxygen Definition

Oxidation is the gain of oxygen by a substance.
Reduction is the loss of oxygen from a substance.
A substance that removes oxygen from another is a reducing agent (e.g. carbon, carbon monoxide).
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)    (magnesium is oxidised) 2CuO(s) + C(s) → 2Cu(s) + CO₂(g)    (copper oxide is reduced; carbon is oxidised)

In the second reaction, copper(II) oxide loses oxygen (reduced) while carbon gains oxygen (oxidised). Oxidation and reduction always occur together.

At Foundation you only need the oxygen definition. At Higher you may be asked to use either definition — read the question carefully. For reactions involving ions (displacement in solution, electrolysis) the electron definition is the one that earns marks.

4.5 Ores and the Extraction of Metals

An ore is a rock containing enough of a metal or metal compound to make it economically worthwhile to extract the metal. Most metals are found in the Earth's crust combined with other elements, typically as oxides, sulfides or carbonates.

Very unreactive metals such as gold (and some silver, copper and platinum) are found in the Earth's crust as the uncombined element ("native" metal), because they are too unreactive to have formed compounds. These can be separated from rock physically, e.g. by panning.

The method depends on reactivity

Extracting a metal from its oxide means reducing it (removing oxygen). The method chosen depends on where the metal is in the reactivity series relative to carbon:

PositionMethodExamplesWhy
Above carbonElectrolysis of the molten ore (or molten compound)K, Na, Ca, Mg, AlCarbon is less reactive, so it cannot remove the oxygen. Electrolysis is needed but uses large amounts of electricity — expensive.
Below carbonHeating with carbon (carbon reduction)Zn, Fe, CuCarbon is more reactive, so it displaces the metal from its oxide. Carbon (coke) is cheap and also provides heat when it burns.

Extracting iron — reduction with carbon

Iron is extracted from haematite in a blast furnace. Coke (carbon) burns in hot air to form carbon dioxide; this reacts with more coke to form carbon monoxide, which acts as the main reducing agent. The overall idea is simply that carbon removes oxygen from iron(III) oxide:

2Fe₂O₃(s) + 3C(s) → 4Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g) Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g)

Iron(III) oxide is reduced (loses oxygen); carbon/carbon monoxide is oxidised (gains oxygen). Limestone is added to remove acidic impurities (mainly silica) as slag.

The specification states that knowledge of the blast furnace is not required — you only need to know that iron is extracted by heating its oxide with carbon, because iron is below carbon in the reactivity series. The detail here is background.

Extracting aluminium — electrolysis

Aluminium is above carbon, so it is extracted by electrolysis of aluminium oxide (purified from bauxite). Key points:

  • Aluminium oxide has a very high melting point (over 2000 °C). It is dissolved in molten cryolite, which lowers the operating temperature to about 950 °C — saving energy and cost.
  • The ions must be free to move, so the electrolyte must be molten (or dissolved).
  • At the cathode (negative electrode) aluminium ions gain electrons ((HT) Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al). Molten aluminium collects at the bottom.
  • At the anode (positive electrode) oxide ions lose electrons ((HT) 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻).
  • The anodes are made of carbon (graphite). The hot oxygen reacts with them to form carbon dioxide (C + O₂ → CO₂), so the anodes burn away and must be replaced regularly.
Overall: 2Al₂O₃(l) → 4Al(l) + 3O₂(g)
Why is aluminium expensive to extract? Give two reasons: (1) electrolysis needs a very large amount of electricity, and energy is also needed to keep the electrolyte molten; (2) the carbon anodes must be replaced because they react with oxygen. Cryolite is used to reduce the energy cost, not to make the ions move.
Explain why zinc can be extracted by heating zinc oxide with carbon, but magnesium cannot be extracted from magnesium oxide this way.

Carbon can only remove oxygen from metals that are less reactive than carbon. Zinc is below carbon, so carbon reduces zinc oxide: ZnO + C → Zn + CO. Magnesium is above carbon — it holds on to oxygen more strongly than carbon does — so carbon cannot reduce magnesium oxide, and magnesium must be extracted by electrolysis of a molten compound. In general: metal below carbon → carbon reduction; metal above carbon → electrolysis.

4.6 Biological Methods of Extraction (HT)

The world's supply of high-grade ores (especially copper) is running out. Low-grade ores contain only a small percentage of metal, so traditional mining and smelting would be uneconomic. Two biological methods can be used:

Bacterial extraction (bioleaching)

  • Certain bacteria feed on low-grade ores (e.g. copper sulfide) and produce a leachate — a solution containing metal ions such as Cu²⁺.
  • The metal is then recovered from the leachate, for example by displacement with scrap iron (Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu) or by electrolysis.

Phytoextraction (phytomining)

  • Plants are grown on soil containing low concentrations of the metal; they absorb metal ions through their roots and concentrate them in their tissues.
  • The plants are harvested and burned; the ash contains a high concentration of metal compounds.
  • The metal is extracted from the ash (e.g. dissolved in acid then electrolysed or displaced).

"Evaluate" questions need both sides and a judgement. A good finishing line: "Biological methods are better for low-grade ores where traditional extraction is uneconomic, even though they are slow."

4.7 Recycling Metals

Metal ores are finite resources. Recycling metals means collecting used items, sorting them, and melting them down to make new products.

Advantages of recyclingDisadvantages / issues
Conserves finite metal ores, so supplies last longerCollection and transport of scrap uses energy and costs money
Usually uses much less energy than extracting from ore (e.g. recycling aluminium uses about 5% of the energy of electrolysis)Sorting mixed metals and removing coatings is labour-intensive
Less mining → less damage to habitats, less noise, dust and waste rockSome items are hard to separate (e.g. alloys, metals bonded to plastics)
Less waste going to landfillThe quality can be lower if metals are contaminated
Economic benefits: saves money (cheaper energy, fewer imports), creates jobs in collection and processingMay not be economic if the metal is cheap and abundant or the price of scrap is low
Recycling aluminium is a favourite example because aluminium extraction by electrolysis uses so much electricity. Link the saving in energy to both lower cost and lower CO₂ emissions (if electricity is generated from fossil fuels).

4.8 Life Cycle Assessments

A life cycle assessment (LCA) considers the effect on the environment of a product at each stage of its life: (1) obtaining the raw materials, (2) manufacturing the product, (3) using the product, and (4) disposing of the product when it is no longer useful.

At each stage an LCA looks at the use of energy, water and other resources, and the pollution and waste produced (CO₂, other gases, solid waste, effluent). Some factors, such as the amount of energy used or the mass of CO₂ released, are easy to quantify; others, such as the visual impact of a quarry or the harm from litter, need value judgements, so LCAs can be subjective or even biased (e.g. selective LCAs written to support advertising claims).

The plastic bag uses a finite resource and persists in the environment, but has lower manufacturing impacts and can be re-used many times. In LCA questions always (1) quote numbers from the data and compare them (e.g. "twice as much"), (2) consider every stage, and (3) end with a conclusion that is supported by the data. Mention that not all effects can be measured objectively.

4.9 Reversible Reactions

A reversible reaction is one in which the products can react to re-form the original reactants. It is shown by the symbol ⇌. The direction of some reversible reactions can be changed by altering the conditions (e.g. temperature).
A + B ⇌ C + D

Examples

  • Ammonium chloride: heating the white solid decomposes it into ammonia and hydrogen chloride gases; at the cooler top of the tube these recombine to form white solid again. NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g)
  • Hydrated copper(II) sulfate: heating blue crystals drives off the water of crystallisation, leaving white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate; adding water turns it blue again (and releases heat). This is also used as a test for water. CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) ⇌ CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) blue              white
  • The Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)

If a reversible reaction is exothermic in one direction, it is endothermic in the other, and the same amount of energy is transferred each way.

4.10 Dynamic Equilibrium

A reversible reaction in a closed system (nothing can get in or out) reaches dynamic equilibrium when the forward and backward reactions are happening at the same rate. The reactions have not stopped, but the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant.

At the start only reactants are present, so the forward reaction is fast and the backward reaction is zero. As reactants are used up, the forward rate falls; as products build up, the backward rate rises. Eventually the two rates become equal.

Two common errors: (1) saying "the reaction has stopped" at equilibrium — it hasn't, both reactions continue (that is what dynamic means); (2) saying "the concentrations of reactants and products are equal" — they are constant, not necessarily equal.

4.11 The Haber Process

Ammonia is made by the Haber process, a reversible reaction between nitrogen and hydrogen that reaches a dynamic equilibrium:

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    (forward reaction exothermic)

Raw materials

  • Nitrogen is extracted from the air (about 78% nitrogen) by fractional distillation of liquefied air.
  • Hydrogen is obtained from natural gas (methane), by reacting it with steam.

Conditions you must recall

ConditionValue
Temperature450 °C
Pressure200 atmospheres
CatalystIron

The gases leaving the reactor are cooled. Ammonia has a much higher boiling point than nitrogen and hydrogen, so it liquefies and is removed; the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled back into the reactor, so very little is wasted even though only a fraction reacts on each pass.

Ammonia is used mainly to make fertilisers (e.g. ammonium nitrate). The detail of why 450 °C and 200 atm are compromise conditions, and how fertilisers are made, is chemistry-only content in Topic 5 — but the equilibrium reasoning below is needed for both.

4.12 Changing the Position of Equilibrium (HT)

When a system at equilibrium is changed, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose the change. ("Position of equilibrium" just means the relative amounts of reactants and products.) This lets you predict the effect of changing temperature, pressure and concentration.

(HT) Hydrogen and iodine react in a sealed container: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). The forward reaction is exothermic. Predict the effect on the amount of HI of (a) raising the temperature, (b) raising the pressure.

(a) Raising the temperature favours the endothermic direction, which is the backward reaction. The equilibrium shifts left, so the amount of HI decreases.
(b) There are 2 molecules of gas on the left (1 H₂ + 1 I₂) and 2 on the right. Because the number of gas molecules is the same on both sides, changing pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium (although the rate increases).
(HT) Nitrogen dioxide (brown) and dinitrogen tetroxide (colourless) are in equilibrium: 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g). When a sealed syringe of the mixture is placed in ice, the colour becomes paler. Deduce whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic.

Paler colour → less NO₂ → equilibrium has shifted right on cooling. Lowering the temperature favours the exothermic direction, so the forward reaction is exothermic.
Structure every equilibrium answer in three steps: (1) state the change; (2) state which direction is favoured and why (endothermic/exothermic, fewer/more gas molecules, use up added substance); (3) state the effect on the yield of the named product. Always count gas molecules using the balancing numbers and ignore solids and liquids.
