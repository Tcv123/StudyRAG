Topic 7 — Rates of Reaction and Energy Changes

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 2

Some reactions are over in a fraction of a second (an explosion, a precipitation), others take weeks (rusting) or years (weathering of limestone). This topic is about how fast reactions go and why, using collision theory, and about the energy changes that go with every chemical reaction — why some reactions warm their surroundings and others cool them, and how we can use bond energies and reaction profiles to explain this.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. Topic 7 is common to GCSE Chemistry and Combined Science — there is no chemistry-only content in this topic. It contains one core practical: investigating the effect of changing conditions on the rate of a reaction.

7.1 What Is the Rate of a Reaction?

The rate of a reaction is how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. It is measured as the change in the amount (mass, volume or concentration) of a reactant or product per unit time.
mean rate of reaction = amount of reactant used ÷ time taken mean rate of reaction = amount of product formed ÷ time taken

(Illustrative data.) Magnesium ribbon reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid. 48 cm³ of hydrogen is collected in the first 30 s. Calculate the mean rate of reaction over this time.
mean rate = volume of product ÷ time = 48 ÷ 30 = 1.6 cm³/s.
(Illustrative data.) A conical flask containing marble chips and acid loses 0.66 g in 2 minutes. Mean rate = 0.66 ÷ 120 = 0.0055 g/s (5.5 × 10⁻³ g/s). If asked for g/min: 0.66 ÷ 2 = 0.33 g/min.
Always check the time units in the question. If the data are in minutes but the answer is asked "in g/s", convert minutes to seconds first (× 60). Rates are almost always quoted to 2 or 3 significant figures.

7.3 Core Practical — Investigating Rates of Reaction

The specification requires you to investigate the effects of changing the conditions of a reaction on the rate by (a) measuring the production of a gas (hydrochloric acid and marble chips) and (b) observing a colour change / cloudiness (sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid).

Part (a): marble chips and hydrochloric acid — effect of surface area

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
  1. Measure 50 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid (e.g. 1 mol/dm³) into a conical flask using a measuring cylinder.
  2. Set up a gas syringe (or upturned measuring cylinder full of water in a trough) connected to a bung and delivery tube.
  3. Weigh out a fixed mass of large marble chips (e.g. 5.0 g), add them to the flask, quickly insert the bung and start the stopwatch.
  4. Record the volume of CO₂ every 30 s until the reaction stops (no more gas collected).
  5. Repeat with the same mass of medium and then small chips (or powder), keeping everything else the same.
  6. Plot volume of gas (y) against time (x) for each size; compare the initial gradients.

Variables: independent — size of marble chips (surface area); dependent — volume of CO₂ (and hence rate); control — mass of chips, volume and concentration of acid, temperature, type of marble.

To investigate concentration instead, use the same mass and size of chips with acid of different concentrations (e.g. 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 mol/dm³).

Part (b): sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid — effect of temperature (or concentration)

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO₂(g) + S(s) + H₂O(l)

The solid sulfur forms as a pale yellow precipitate, making the solution cloudy (opaque).

  1. Draw a black cross on a piece of paper and place a conical flask on top.
  2. Add a fixed volume (e.g. 50 cm³) of sodium thiosulfate solution. Warm it in a water bath to the chosen temperature and record the temperature.
  3. Add a fixed volume (e.g. 5 cm³) of dilute hydrochloric acid, swirl and start the stopwatch.
  4. Look down through the solution from above; stop the clock when the cross can no longer be seen.
  5. Repeat at different temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50 °C). Calculate a relative rate = 1/time.

The SO₂ produced is toxic — do the thiosulfate experiment in a well-ventilated room and asthmatics should take care.

7.4 Collision Theory

For a reaction to happen, reactant particles must collide with each other, and the collision must have at least a minimum amount of energy — the activation energy. Collisions with enough energy to react are called successful (or effective) collisions.

Most collisions are not successful: the particles just bounce off each other. The rate of a reaction therefore depends on:

  • the frequency of collisions (how many collisions there are per second), and
  • the energy of the collisions (what fraction of collisions have energy ≥ activation energy).

Anything that increases the number of successful collisions per second increases the rate of reaction.

Always say "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second" — not just "more collisions". Over the whole reaction the total number of collisions might be the same; it is the frequency that matters for rate.

7.5 Factors Affecting the Rate of Reaction

Temperature

Increasing the temperature increases the rate for two reasons:

  1. Particles move faster, so they collide more frequently.
  2. Particles have more energy, so a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy — more collisions are successful.

The second effect is the more important one, which is why a small temperature rise has such a big effect on rate.

Concentration of a solution

Increasing the concentration means there are more reactant particles in the same volume. Particles are closer together, so collisions are more frequent. The energy of each collision is not changed.

Pressure of a gas

Increasing the pressure of a gas (by squeezing the same number of molecules into a smaller volume, or adding more gas) has the same effect as increasing concentration: more particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions.

Surface area to volume ratio of a solid

Only particles on the surface of a solid can collide with particles in the solution or gas. Breaking a solid into smaller pieces (or a powder) increases the surface area to volume ratio, exposing more particles, so collisions are more frequent.

A cube of side 3 cm is cut into 1 cm cubes. Compare the surface area to volume ratios.
Large cube: SA = 6 × 3² = 54 cm²; V = 3³ = 27 cm³; SA : V = 54 : 27 = 2 : 1.
27 small cubes: SA = 27 × 6 × 1² = 162 cm²; V = 27 cm³; SA : V = 162 : 27 = 6 : 1.
The ratio has tripled, so many more particles are exposed to collisions and the reaction is faster.

Catalysts

A catalyst increases the rate by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy (see 7.7). It does not increase the frequency of collisions; instead, a greater proportion of collisions now have enough energy to react.

A full-mark collision-theory answer usually has a chain of three steps: (1) what happens to the particles ("more particles in the same volume" / "particles have more energy"), (2) the effect on collisions ("more frequent collisions" / "more collisions have energy ≥ activation energy"), (3) "so more successful collisions per second, so the rate increases". Don't say particles "move faster" for a concentration change — they don't.

7.6 Interpreting Rate Graphs

You must be able to interpret graphs of mass, volume or concentration of reactant or product against time.

  • The gradient (steepness) of the curve shows the rate. The steeper the line, the faster the reaction.
  • The curve is steepest at the start, when the concentration of reactants is highest, so collisions are most frequent.
  • The gradient decreases as reactants are used up.
  • The line becomes horizontal when the reaction has stopped because one reactant (the limiting reactant) has been used up.
  • The final amount of product depends only on the amount of limiting reactant, not on the rate. Curve B reaches the same final volume as A, just sooner. Curve C has half the limiting reactant, so only half the final volume.
  • For a graph of mass of flask against time (mass-loss method) or concentration of reactant against time, the curve goes down and levels off — the same rules about steepness apply.

Calculating rates from graphs

Mean rate over a period = change in quantity ÷ time taken (read two points off the curve).

(HT) The rate at a particular instant is found by drawing a tangent to the curve at that time and calculating its gradient:

rate at time t = gradient of tangent = change in y ÷ change in x
(HT) (Illustrative data.) A tangent drawn to a volume–time curve at t = 40 s passes through (10 s, 18 cm³) and (70 s, 60 cm³).
gradient = (60 − 18) ÷ (70 − 10) = 42 ÷ 60 = 0.70 cm³/s. This is the rate at 40 s.
(HT) When drawing a tangent, use a ruler and make the line just touch the curve at the chosen point, with the curve bending away equally on both sides. Make the "triangle" as large as possible to reduce reading errors.
(Illustrative data.) In a mass-loss experiment the mass of the flask falls from 152.40 g to 151.80 g in the first 60 s, and from 151.80 g to 151.62 g in the next 60 s.
Mean rate (0–60 s) = 0.60 ÷ 60 = 0.010 g/s. Mean rate (60–120 s) = 0.18 ÷ 60 = 0.0030 g/s.
The rate falls because the acid is being used up, so its concentration decreases and collisions become less frequent.

7.7 Catalysts and Enzymes

A catalyst is a substance that speeds up the rate of a reaction without altering the products of the reaction, and is itself unchanged chemically and in mass at the end of the reaction.
  • Because it is not used up, only a small amount of catalyst is needed, and it can be reused.
  • A catalyst does not appear in the overall balanced equation (it may be written above the arrow).
  • A catalyst does not change the amount of product made, or the overall energy change of the reaction.
  • Different reactions need different catalysts. Many industrial catalysts are transition metals or their compounds (e.g. iron in the Haber process; vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process; platinum/rhodium in catalytic converters).
  • Catalysts are economically important: reactions can be run at lower temperatures, saving energy and fuel costs, and making less pollution.

How a catalyst works

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. At the same temperature, a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ the (new, lower) activation energy, so there are more successful collisions per second.

Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts — proteins made by living cells that speed up specific reactions.
  • Enzymes in yeast catalyse fermentation, which turns sugars (e.g. glucose) into ethanol and carbon dioxide. This is used in the production of alcoholic drinks such as beer and wine (and also in bread-making, where the CO₂ makes the dough rise).
C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g)   (enzymes in yeast)
  • Enzymes work best at a particular optimum temperature (for yeast fermentation about 30–40 °C). Too cold and the reaction is slow; too hot and the enzyme is denatured (its shape changes) and it stops working.
  • Enzymes also have an optimum pH.
Don't say an enzyme is "killed" at high temperature — enzymes are not alive. Say it is denatured. Fermentation is covered in more detail (making ethanol solution) in Topic 9.

7.8 Heat Energy Changes in Reactions

Changes in heat energy accompany many processes. You need to recall that temperature changes happen when:

  • salts dissolve in water (e.g. ammonium nitrate dissolving — temperature falls; anhydrous copper(II) sulfate or calcium chloride dissolving — temperature rises),
  • neutralisation reactions take place (acid + alkali — temperature rises),
  • displacement reactions take place (e.g. zinc + copper(II) sulfate solution — temperature rises),
  • precipitation reactions take place (two solutions mixed to form an insoluble solid — small temperature change, usually a rise).

These changes can be investigated by measuring temperature changes in an insulated polystyrene cup.

Method (e.g. neutralisation)

  1. Measure 25 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid into a polystyrene cup standing in a beaker.
  2. Record the starting temperature of the acid.
  3. Add 25 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution (at the same starting temperature), put the lid on and stir.
  4. Record the highest temperature reached (or lowest, for an endothermic change).
  5. Temperature change = final temperature − initial temperature. Repeat and calculate a mean.

To compare displacement reactions, add a fixed mass of metal powder to a fixed volume of copper(II) sulfate solution; to investigate dissolving, add a fixed mass of salt to a fixed volume of water.

Main source of error: heat is lost to (or gained from) the surroundings, so the measured temperature change is smaller than it should be. Improvements: use a lid, use a polystyrene (not glass) container, stir to mix evenly, read the highest temperature, or take readings over time and extrapolate the cooling curve back to the time of mixing.

7.9 Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

An exothermic reaction is one in which heat energy is given out to the surroundings — the temperature of the surroundings increases.

An endothermic reaction is one in which heat energy is taken in from the surroundings — the temperature of the surroundings decreases.

The "surroundings" include the water the reaction is dissolved in, the container and the thermometer — so a thermometer dipped in an exothermic reaction mixture shows a rise in temperature.

In an exothermic reaction the chemicals lose energy and the surroundings gain it. Be careful with wording: "the reaction gets hot" is fine, but "the reaction takes in heat so it gets hot" is wrong.

7.10 Bond Breaking, Bond Making and Activation Energy

  • Breaking bonds is endothermic — energy must be supplied to pull atoms apart.
  • Making bonds is exothermic — energy is released when new bonds form.

In every reaction, bonds in the reactants are broken and new bonds in the products are made. Whether the overall reaction is exothermic or endothermic depends on which is bigger:

  • If more energy is released making new bonds than is taken in breaking old bonds, the reaction is exothermic.
  • If more energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released making bonds, the reaction is endothermic.
The activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum amount of energy needed for a reaction to start — the energy colliding particles must have to break bonds and react.

Even exothermic reactions need activation energy: a fuel does not burn until a spark or flame supplies it. Once started, the energy released keeps the reaction going.

7.11 Calculating Energy Changes from Bond Energies (HT)

A bond energy is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond, measured in kJ mol⁻¹ (kJ/mol). The same amount of energy is released when one mole of that bond is formed.

energy change = total energy needed to break bonds − total energy released making bonds ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds made)
  • A negative answer means the reaction is exothermic.
  • A positive answer means the reaction is endothermic.

Method

  1. Write the balanced equation and draw out the displayed formulae so you can see every bond.
  2. Count each type of bond in the reactants (remember the coefficients) and add up the energy to break them.
  3. Do the same for the products — the energy released.
  4. Energy change = (bonds broken) − (bonds made). Include the sign and unit kJ mol⁻¹.

Typical data-book values; exam questions always give you the values to use.

(HT) Calculate the energy change for: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl
Bonds broken: 1 × H–H + 1 × Cl–Cl = 436 + 243 = 679 kJ
Bonds made: 2 × H–Cl = 2 × 432 = 864 kJ
Energy change = 679 − 864 = −185 kJ mol⁻¹ → negative, so exothermic.
(HT) Calculate the energy change for the complete combustion of methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
Displayed: H–C(–H)(–H)–H + 2 O=O → O=C=O + 2 H–O–H
Bonds broken: 4 × C–H = 4 × 412 = 1648; 2 × O=O = 2 × 498 = 996; total = 2644 kJ
Bonds made: 2 × C=O = 2 × 805 = 1610; 4 × O–H = 4 × 464 = 1856; total = 3466 kJ
Energy change = 2644 − 3466 = −822 kJ mol⁻¹ (exothermic — methane is a good fuel).
(HT) Calculate the energy change for the Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
Bonds broken: 1 × N≡N + 3 × H–H = 945 + 3(436) = 945 + 1308 = 2253 kJ
Bonds made: 2 NH₃ contain 6 N–H bonds = 6 × 391 = 2346 kJ
Energy change = 2253 − 2346 = −93 kJ mol⁻¹ (exothermic).
The most common errors are (1) forgetting to multiply by the coefficients in the equation, (2) miscounting bonds — e.g. CO₂ has two C=O bonds and each H₂O has two O–H bonds, and (3) doing "made − broken", which gives the wrong sign. Always write "broken − made".

7.12 Reaction Profiles

A reaction profile (energy level diagram) shows the energy of the reactants and products and how energy changes as the reaction proceeds. You must be able to draw and label reaction profiles for exothermic and endothermic reactions, showing:

  • the reactants and products (as horizontal lines, labelled with names or formulae),
  • the activation energy — an arrow from the reactants up to the peak of the curve,
  • the overall energy change — an arrow from the reactants level to the products level.
The activation energy arrow always starts at the reactants line and ends at the top of the hump — not from the products, and not from the x-axis. The energy-change arrow points down for exothermic and up for endothermic. Label the axes "energy" and "progress of reaction".

Linking it all together

On the reaction profile, going up the hump represents the energy needed to break bonds in the reactants (endothermic); coming down the other side represents energy released as new bonds form (exothermic). The difference in height between reactants and products is the overall energy change you calculated from bond energies. A catalyst lowers the hump but does not move the reactant or product lines.

The energy change for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl is −185 kJ mol⁻¹ and the activation energy is (illustratively) +250 kJ mol⁻¹. Sketch the profile.
Draw reactants (H₂ + Cl₂) higher than products (2HCl) by 185 units; draw the hump 250 units above the reactants; label Eₐ (reactants → peak, upward arrow) and the energy change (reactants → products, downward arrow, −185 kJ mol⁻¹).

Key Points Summary

  • Rate = amount of reactant used (or product formed) ÷ time; units cm³/s, g/s or mol/dm³/s; for "time to a fixed point" experiments, relative rate = 1/t.
  • Choose a method to suit the reaction: gas syringe, mass loss, disappearing cross / colorimeter, pH probe.
  • Core practical: marble chips + HCl (gas volume, surface area or concentration) and sodium thiosulfate + HCl (cloudiness, temperature or concentration). Know the variables and sources of error.
  • Collision theory: particles must collide with energy ≥ activation energy; rate depends on frequency and energy of collisions.
  • Higher concentration, gas pressure or surface area : volume → more frequent collisions. Higher temperature → more frequent and more energetic collisions.
  • On a product–time graph: gradient = rate; steepest at start; flat when the limiting reactant is used up; final amount depends only on the amount of limiting reactant.
  • (HT) Rate at an instant = gradient of the tangent to the curve.
  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction without altering the products and is unchanged in mass and chemically at the end; it provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts; yeast enzymes ferment sugars to make alcoholic drinks.
  • Dissolving salts, neutralisation, displacement and precipitation all involve heat changes — measure with a polystyrene cup, lid and thermometer.
  • Exothermic: heat given out, surroundings get hotter, ΔH negative. Endothermic: heat taken in, surroundings get colder, ΔH positive.
  • Bond breaking is endothermic; bond making is exothermic.
  • (HT) Energy change from bond energies = bonds broken − bonds made (kJ mol⁻¹).
  • Activation energy is the minimum energy needed to start a reaction.
  • Be able to draw and label reaction profiles: reactants, products, Eₐ (reactants → peak) and the overall energy change.
