Topic 3 — Chemical Changes

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 1

Topic 3 covers two big families of reactions. The first half is about acids: what makes a solution acidic or alkaline, the pH scale, how acids react with metals, bases and carbonates, which salts are soluble, and how to make pure samples of salts. The second half is about electrolysis: using electricity to decompose ionic compounds, and predicting what forms at each electrode. Three core practicals sit in this topic: the pH change when calcium oxide or hydroxide is added to hydrochloric acid, preparing copper sulfate crystals, and the electrolysis of copper sulfate solution.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. Topic 3 is shared with GCSE Combined Science; the separate-chemistry-only content of 1CH0 is concentrated in Topics 5 and 9.

3.1 Acids, Alkalis and the pH Scale

Indicators

An indicator is a dye that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acidic or alkaline solution. You must recall the colours of three single indicators:

IndicatorColour in acidColour in alkaliNotes
LitmusRedBluePurple in neutral solution; used as paper or solution
Methyl orangeRedYellowOrange at the end point; good for titrations
PhenolphthaleinColourlessPinkSharp colour change; good for titrations

Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that shows a range of colours, so it can estimate the pH of a solution, not just whether it is acidic or alkaline. A pH meter (or pH probe) gives a more accurate numerical reading.

What makes a solution acidic or alkaline?

Acids are substances that produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), when dissolved in water.
Alkalis are substances that produce hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq), when dissolved in water.
AcidFormulaIons in solutionSalts formed
Hydrochloric acidHClH⁺ + Cl⁻Chlorides
Sulfuric acidH₂SO₄2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻Sulfates
Nitric acidHNO₃H⁺ + NO₃⁻Nitrates
  • The higher the concentration of H⁺ ions, the lower the pH.
  • The higher the concentration of OH⁻ ions, the higher the pH.
  • (HT) pH is a logarithmic scale: each time the H⁺ concentration increases by a factor of 10, the pH decreases by 1. A solution of pH 2 has 10 times the H⁺ concentration of one at pH 3, and 100 times that of one at pH 4.
(HT) Solution A has pH 1 and solution B has pH 4. How many times greater is the H⁺ ion concentration in A than in B?
Difference in pH = 4 − 1 = 3 → factor = 10³ = 1000 times greater.

Concentrated and dilute (HT)

Concentration describes the amount of a substance (solute) in a given volume of solution.
A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid dissolved per unit volume; a dilute acid has a small amount of acid per unit volume.

Strong and weak acids (HT)

A strong acid is completely dissociated (ionised) into ions in aqueous solution — e.g. hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
A weak acid is only partially dissociated in aqueous solution — most of its molecules stay un-ionised — e.g. ethanoic, citric and carbonic acids: CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).

At the same concentration, a strong acid has a higher H⁺ concentration and therefore a lower pH than a weak acid. It also reacts faster with metals and carbonates, because there are more H⁺ ions to collide with.

Strong / weakConcentrated / dilute
DescribesThe proportion of acid molecules that dissociate into ionsThe amount of acid dissolved per unit volume
Can be changed by adding water?No — it is a property of the acidYes — adding water dilutes it
ExampleHydrochloric acid is always strongYou can have dilute HCl or concentrated HCl
Never use "strong" to mean "concentrated". A dilute solution of a strong acid (e.g. 0.01 mol dm⁻³ HCl) and a concentrated solution of a weak acid are both possible. In a 4-6 mark answer, define both pairs of terms explicitly in terms of dissociation and amount per volume.

3.2 Bases, Alkalis and Neutralisation

A base is a substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only — typically a metal oxide or metal hydroxide.
An alkali is a base that is soluble in water (e.g. sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis (copper(II) oxide is an insoluble base).

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base. In terms of ions, the hydrogen ions from the acid react with hydroxide ions from the alkali to make water:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

This ionic equation is the same for every acid–alkali neutralisation, whichever acid and alkali are used. The other ions (e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻) are spectator ions that make up the salt.

Reactions of acids — general equations

ReactionGeneral equationExample
Acid + metalacid + metal → salt + hydrogenZn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)
Acid + metal oxideacid + metal oxide → salt + waterCuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
Acid + metal hydroxideacid + metal hydroxide → salt + waterNaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
Acid + metal carbonateacid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxideCaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
  • Only metals more reactive than hydrogen react with dilute acids (e.g. Mg, Zn, Fe). Copper, silver and gold do not react. Very reactive metals (sodium, potassium) react dangerously and are never added to acids in school.
  • With metals and carbonates you see effervescence (fizzing) as a gas is produced, and the solid disappears.
  • Naming the salt: first part from the metal (or ammonium), second part from the acid — hydrochloric → chloride, sulfuric → sulfate, nitric → nitrate.
Write a balanced equation for magnesium oxide reacting with nitric acid.
Salt: magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO₃)₂ (Mg²⁺ and two NO₃⁻).
MgO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l)

Tests for hydrogen and carbon dioxide

GasTestPositive result
Hydrogen, H₂Hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the test tubeBurns with a squeaky pop (hydrogen reacts with oxygen to make water)

Limewater reaction: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l). Other gas tests you will use alongside electrolysis: oxygen relights a glowing splint; chlorine turns damp blue litmus paper red and then bleaches it white.

Do not mix up the splints: lighted splint + squeaky pop = hydrogen; glowing splint that relights = oxygen. Carbon dioxide puts out a lighted splint, but so do other gases — the only accepted test for CO₂ is limewater.

3.3 Core Practical: pH Change When Calcium Oxide or Calcium Hydroxide is Added to Hydrochloric Acid

Aim: to investigate how the pH of a fixed volume of dilute hydrochloric acid changes as powdered calcium hydroxide (or calcium oxide) is added in small portions.

Ca(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l) CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)

Method

  1. Measure 50 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid (e.g. 0.1 mol dm⁻³) using a measuring cylinder and pour into a beaker.
  2. Measure the starting pH, using a pH meter (calibrated first) or by placing a drop of solution on universal indicator paper with a glass rod and comparing with the colour chart.
  3. Weigh out about 0.3 g of calcium hydroxide powder on a mass balance and add it to the acid. Stir thoroughly with a glass rod.
  4. Record the pH again.
  5. Repeat steps 3–4 until about 10 portions have been added and the pH no longer changes.
  6. Plot a graph of pH (y-axis) against total mass of calcium hydroxide added (x-axis).

Analysis and evaluation

  • At first the pH rises only slowly, because the acid is in excess and most H⁺ ions remain.
  • Close to the neutralisation point the pH rises very steeply — a small amount of base removes the last H⁺ ions.
  • After neutralisation, extra calcium hydroxide makes the solution alkaline. The pH levels off at about 12 because calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble — undissolved white solid remains in the beaker.
  • The mass needed to neutralise the acid can be read from the steepest part of the graph (where pH = 7).
IssueImprovement / explanation
Universal indicator paper only gives whole-number pH and colour matching is subjectiveUse a calibrated pH meter for more precise, objective readings
Powder not fully reacted when pH readStir for the same time (e.g. 30 s) after each addition before measuring
Too few points near neutralisationUse smaller portions (e.g. 0.1 g) near the steep part of the curve
HazardsCalcium hydroxide and calcium oxide are irritant/corrosive to eyes — wear eye protection; calcium oxide reacts exothermically with water

3.4 Solubility of Salts and Precipitation

You must recall the general solubility rules for common ionic compounds in water:

CompoundsSolubleInsoluble (main exceptions)
Common sodium, potassium and ammonium saltsAll soluble—
NitratesAll soluble—
Common chloridesSolubleSilver chloride and lead chloride
Common sulfatesSolubleLead, barium and calcium sulfates
Common carbonates and hydroxidesOnly those of sodium, potassium and ammoniumAll others insoluble (e.g. CaCO₃, CuCO₃, Cu(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃)
A precipitate is an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. A precipitation reaction happens when the positive ion from one solution and the negative ion from another form an insoluble salt.
Will a precipitate form when barium chloride solution is mixed with sodium sulfate solution? If so, name it.
Possible new combinations: barium sulfate and sodium chloride. Sodium salts are all soluble, but barium sulfate is insoluble → a white precipitate of barium sulfate forms.
BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)   ((HT) ionic: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s))
To predict a precipitate, swap partners: write down the two "new" combinations of ions and check each against the solubility rules. If either is insoluble, a precipitate forms. Remember that sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate compounds are always soluble, so they are never the precipitate.

3.5 Making Salts

The method you use to make a salt depends on whether the salt is soluble and whether the base you start with is soluble.

Method 1: soluble salt from an insoluble reactant (excess solid)

When the base (or metal, or carbonate) is insoluble, add it in excess to the acid. Using excess makes sure all the acid reacts, so the final solution contains only salt and water. The unreacted solid is easy to remove by filtration because it is insoluble.

Core Practical: preparing pure, dry hydrated copper sulfate crystals from copper oxide

CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
  1. Measure about 20 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker and warm it gently (e.g. over a water bath or low Bunsen flame) — warming speeds up the reaction. Do not boil.
  2. Add copper(II) oxide powder, a spatula at a time, stirring with a glass rod. Continue until no more dissolves and a little black solid remains — this shows the copper oxide is in excess and all the acid has been used up.
  3. Filter the warm mixture to remove the excess copper oxide. The filtrate is blue copper sulfate solution.
  4. Pour the filtrate into an evaporating basin and heat it (over a water bath) to evaporate some of the water, until crystals start to form on a cold glass rod dipped in it (point of crystallisation).
  5. Leave the basin in a cool place for a day or so so that the solution crystallises slowly, forming blue crystals.
  6. Filter or decant off the remaining liquid, and dry the crystals by patting with filter paper (or in a warm oven, not too hot).
StepReason
Warm the acidIncreases the rate of reaction
Add copper oxide in excessEnsures all the acid reacts — no acid left in the product
FilterRemoves unreacted (insoluble) copper oxide
Heat only until crystallisation point; do not evaporate to drynessGives larger, purer crystals; keeps the water of crystallisation (CuSO₄·5H₂O is blue; overheating gives white anhydrous CuSO₄)
Dry with filter paper / low-temperature ovenRemoves surface water without decomposing the hydrated crystals
Hazards: sulfuric acid is an irritant (corrosive if concentrated) and copper oxide and copper sulfate are harmful — wear eye protection, and avoid spitting by heating gently. If asked why the acid is warmed rather than boiled: boiling would cause the acid to spit and could make it more concentrated.

Method 2: soluble salt from an acid and an alkali — titration

When both reactants are soluble, you cannot add "excess" and filter it off, because the excess would stay dissolved and contaminate the salt. Instead you must add the exact volume of acid needed to neutralise the alkali, found by titration. This method is used to make sodium, potassium and ammonium salts.

  1. Use a pipette (with pipette filler) to measure exactly 25.0 cm³ of alkali (e.g. sodium hydroxide) into a conical flask. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator (phenolphthalein or methyl orange — not universal indicator, whose gradual colour change gives no sharp end point).
  2. Fill a burette with the acid and record the starting volume (read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level).
  3. Add the acid to the alkali while swirling the flask, adding drop by drop near the end point, until the indicator just changes colour. Record the final burette reading; volume added = final − initial (the titre).
  4. Repeat to get concordant results (titres within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and calculate the mean.
  5. Now repeat the titration without the indicator, adding exactly the mean volume of acid to a fresh 25.0 cm³ of alkali. (Alternatively, remove the indicator by adding activated charcoal and filtering.)
  6. Heat the resulting salt solution to the point of crystallisation, leave it to cool and crystallise, then filter and dry the crystals.
Why must the titration be repeated without indicator? The indicator is a dye that would otherwise contaminate the salt crystals. Why can't you add excess alkali and filter? Because the alkali is soluble and cannot be removed by filtering — the salt would be impure.

Method 3: insoluble salts — precipitation

An insoluble salt is made by mixing two solutions, one containing its positive ion and one containing its negative ion. For example, to make silver chloride (or lead iodide, barium sulfate, calcium carbonate):

AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
  1. Dissolve each soluble reactant in distilled (deionised) water — tap water contains ions that could form other precipitates.
  2. Mix the two solutions in a beaker and stir. A precipitate forms immediately.
  3. Filter the mixture; the insoluble salt is the residue on the filter paper.
  4. Wash the residue on the filter paper with distilled water to remove the soluble impurities (the other salt, e.g. NaNO₃, and any excess reactant).
  5. Dry the solid in a warm oven or by leaving it on filter paper.
Choose two solutions to make barium sulfate by precipitation.
Need a soluble barium compound and a soluble sulfate: e.g. barium chloride (or barium nitrate) and sodium sulfate (or dilute sulfuric acid).
BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq). Filter, wash with distilled water, dry.

3.6 Electrolysis

An electrolyte is an ionic compound that is molten or dissolved in water, so that its ions are free to move and it can conduct electricity.
Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte using a direct current of electricity.
  • The negative electrode is the cathode; the positive electrode is the anode.
  • Positive ions (cations) are attracted to the cathode. Negative ions (anions) are attracted to the anode.
  • When ions reach the electrodes they are discharged: cations gain electrons at the cathode and anions lose electrons at the anode, forming elements.
  • Inert electrodes (graphite or platinum) do not react; they just conduct electricity into and out of the electrolyte.
  • Solid ionic compounds cannot be electrolysed — the ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move.

Electrolysis of molten compounds

A molten binary ionic compound contains only two kinds of ion, so the products are simple: the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal forms at the anode.

lead bromide → lead + bromine     PbBr₂(l) → Pb(l) + Br₂(g)

Electrolysis of aqueous solutions

In an aqueous solution, water also provides ions: a very small proportion of water molecules ionise, H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. So there are two cations (the metal ion and H⁺) competing at the cathode and two anions (the non-metal ion and OH⁻) competing at the anode. Only one of each is discharged:

ElectrodeRule
Cathode (−)Hydrogen is produced, unless the metal is less reactive than hydrogen (e.g. copper, silver), in which case the metal is deposited.
Anode (+)Oxygen is produced, unless the solution contains halide ions (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻), in which case the halogen is produced. (Sulfate and nitrate ions are never discharged.)

Two quick checks: pure water conducts very poorly, so a little sulfuric acid (or sodium sulfate) is added to provide ions — the acid itself is not used up. And in NaCl(aq), sodium is never formed at the cathode in solution; hydrogen is, because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen.

Oxidation, reduction and half equations (HT)

Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons (OIL RIG).
In electrolysis, reduction always happens at the cathode (positive ions gain electrons) and oxidation always happens at the anode (negative ions lose electrons).

A half equation shows what happens at one electrode, including electrons (e⁻). Both atoms and charges must balance.

ElectrodeHalf equationProcess
Cathode — leadPb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → PbReduction
Cathode — copperCu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → CuReduction
Cathode — hydrogen2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂Reduction
Anode — bromine2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻Oxidation
Anode — chlorine2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻Oxidation
Anode — oxygen4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻Oxidation
Anode — copper electrode dissolvingCu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻Oxidation
(HT) Write the half equation for the formation of aluminium at the cathode during electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide.
Aluminium ions are Al³⁺, so each needs 3 electrons: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (reduction — electrons gained).
Electrons appear on the left of a cathode half equation (gained) and on the right of an anode half equation (lost). Check charge: in 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ the left is 4− and the right is 4− ✓.

Electrolysis with copper electrodes — purifying copper

If copper sulfate solution is electrolysed using copper electrodes (not inert ones), the anode itself takes part:

  • At the anode, copper atoms lose electrons and go into solution as Cu²⁺ ions ((HT) Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻). The anode gets lighter.
  • At the cathode, Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and copper is deposited ((HT) Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu). The cathode gets heavier.
  • Copper ions are added at the anode at the same rate they are removed at the cathode, so the concentration and blue colour of the solution stay the same.
  • Ideally, the mass lost by the anode equals the mass gained by the cathode.

This is how copper is purified industrially: the anode is a block of impure copper and the cathode is a thin sheet of pure copper. Copper from the anode transfers to the cathode; impurities do not dissolve and fall to the bottom as anode sludge (which can contain valuable metals such as silver and gold). In this case the anode loses more mass than the cathode gains.

3.7 Core Practical: Electrolysis of Copper Sulfate Solution

Aim: to investigate the electrolysis of copper sulfate solution using (a) inert (graphite) electrodes and (b) copper electrodes, and how the mass of copper electrodes changes with current.

Part A — inert (graphite) electrodes

  1. Pour copper sulfate solution into a beaker. Place two graphite rods into it, not touching, held in a lid or electrode holder.
  2. Connect to a low-voltage d.c. supply (e.g. 4 V) and switch on.
  3. Observe: a pink-brown deposit of copper forms on the cathode; bubbles of oxygen form at the anode (test with a glowing splint, which relights).

Part B — copper electrodes

  1. Clean two copper strips with emery paper, wash with propanone (flammable — no flames) or water, dry, and weigh each one. Label anode and cathode.
  2. Set up the circuit with an ammeter and a variable resistor in series with the cell. Adjust the resistor so the current is a set value (e.g. 0.2 A).
  3. Electrolyse for a fixed time (e.g. 20 minutes), keeping the current constant.
  4. Remove the electrodes, rinse gently with distilled water then propanone, allow to dry, and reweigh.
  5. Repeat with fresh electrodes at different currents (e.g. 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 A).
  6. Calculate the change in mass of each electrode; plot change in mass against current.
VariableIn this practical
IndependentCurrent (A)
DependentChange in mass of each electrode (g)
ControlTime, concentration and volume of solution, size of electrodes and distance between them, temperature

Expected results

  • The cathode gains mass and the anode loses mass; the two changes are about equal.
  • The change in mass is directly proportional to the current (a straight line through the origin), because the amount of copper transferred is proportional to the charge passed (charge = current × time).
In 20 minutes at 0.40 A, a copper cathode increases from 12.45 g to 12.61 g, and the anode decreases from 12.50 g to 12.33 g. Calculate the changes and comment.
Cathode: 12.61 − 12.45 = +0.16 g; anode: 12.33 − 12.50 = −0.17 g.
The anode loss is slightly larger than the cathode gain — some copper may have flaked off the anode or been lost from the cathode while rinsing/drying.
Common sources of error to evaluate: copper deposit flaking off the cathode when rinsing (rinse gently, don't wipe); electrodes not completely dry when reweighed (dry thoroughly — water adds mass); current drifting during the run (keep adjusting the variable resistor). Hazards: copper sulfate is harmful and an irritant; propanone is highly flammable.

Key Points Summary

  • Indicators: litmus (red/blue), methyl orange (red/yellow), phenolphthalein (colourless/pink). Universal indicator or a pH meter measures pH.
  • Acids produce H⁺(aq); alkalis produce OH⁻(aq). More H⁺ → lower pH; more OH⁻ → higher pH.
  • (HT) Factor of 10 change in [H⁺] = 1 pH unit. Concentrated/dilute = amount per volume; strong/weak = complete/partial dissociation.
  • Bases neutralise acids to form a salt and water; alkalis are soluble bases. Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
  • Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen; acid + metal oxide/hydroxide → salt + water; acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO₂.
  • Tests: hydrogen — lighted splint, squeaky pop; carbon dioxide — limewater turns milky.
  • Core practical: adding Ca(OH)₂/CaO to HCl — pH rises slowly, then steeply, then levels off at about 12.
  • Solubility: Na, K, NH₄⁺ salts and nitrates soluble; chlorides soluble except Ag and Pb; sulfates soluble except Pb, Ba, Ca; carbonates and hydroxides insoluble except Na, K, NH₄⁺.
  • Soluble salt from insoluble base: excess solid, filter, crystallise (core practical: CuSO₄·5H₂O from CuO). From an alkali: titration, repeat without indicator, crystallise. Insoluble salt: precipitation, filter, wash, dry.
  • Electrolysis: electrolyte = molten or dissolved ionic compound; cations to cathode (−), anions to anode (+).
  • Aqueous solutions: cathode gives H₂ unless the metal is less reactive than hydrogen; anode gives O₂ unless halide ions are present.
  • Know the products for PbBr₂(l), CuCl₂(aq), NaCl(aq), Na₂SO₄(aq), acidified water and CuSO₄(aq).
  • (HT) Reduction (gain of e⁻) at the cathode; oxidation (loss of e⁻) at the anode; write balanced half equations.
  • Copper electrodes: anode dissolves, cathode gains copper, solution colour unchanged — used to purify copper. Core practical: mass change ∝ current.
