Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 1

Topic 5 gathers together the extra content that only appears in the separate science GCSE. It has four strands: transition metals, alloys and corrosion; quantitative analysis (concentrations, titrations, percentage yield, atom economy and molar gas volume); dynamic equilibria applied to industry (the Haber process conditions and fertilisers); and chemical cells and fuel cells. It builds directly on Topic 1 (moles and formula masses) and Topic 4 (reactivity, the Haber process and equilibrium), so revise those first.

The whole of Topic 5 is chemistry only — every point carries a "C" suffix in the specification (5.1C–5.27C) and is not examined in Combined Science. Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. The topic contains one core practical: an accurate acid–alkali titration (CP5, spec 5.9C).

5.1 Transition Metals

The transition metals are the block of metals in the centre of the periodic table, between Group 2 and Group 3. Most metals are transition metals. Examples include iron (Fe), copper (Cu), nickel (Ni), chromium (Cr), manganese (Mn), cobalt (Co), zinc (Zn), silver (Ag), gold (Au) and platinum (Pt).

Typical properties of transition metals

Transition metals have the typical properties of metals (shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable, ductile) and in addition:

  • High melting points (except mercury) — e.g. iron melts at about 1540 °C.
  • High densities — e.g. iron about 7.9 g cm⁻³, copper about 8.9 g cm⁻³.
  • They form coloured compounds.
  • The metals and their compounds show catalytic activity — e.g. iron in the Haber process.

5.2 Corrosion and Rusting

Corrosion is the gradual destruction of a metal caused by its oxidation, usually by reaction with oxygen (and often water) in the environment. Rusting is the corrosion of iron (and steel): it needs both oxygen and water.
iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)

In rusting, iron atoms lose electrons to form iron ions — the iron is oxidised (it gains oxygen and loses electrons). Rust is flaky and crumbles away, exposing fresh iron underneath, so rusting continues until the iron is destroyed. Salt water speeds up rusting.

Preventing rusting

The specification asks you to explain three approaches: excluding oxygen, excluding water, and sacrificial protection.

MethodHow it worksExamples
Barrier methods — painting, oiling/greasing, coating with plasticForm a layer that excludes oxygen and water from the iron surfacePaint on cars and bridges; grease on bike chains and machinery; plastic-coated garden wire
Electroplating / tin-platingA thin layer of a less reactive metal acts as a barrierTin-plated steel food cans; chromium-plated taps
Storing with a drying agentRemoves water vapour from the airSilica gel packets with tools
Sacrificial protectionA more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) is attached to or coated on the iron. It is oxidised in preference to iron, so the iron does not rust even if exposed.Magnesium or zinc blocks bolted to ships' hulls and underground pipes
GalvanisingCoating iron with zinc. Acts as a barrier and provides sacrificial protection if scratched.Galvanised buckets, fences, car bodies, lamp posts
Explain why a scratched galvanised (zinc-coated) bucket does not rust, but a scratched tin-plated can does.

Galvanised: zinc is more reactive than iron, so where the iron is exposed the zinc is oxidised instead (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻); the zinc is sacrificed and the iron stays protected.
Tin-plated: tin is less reactive than iron, so it only works as a barrier. Once scratched, oxygen and water reach the iron and it rusts — in fact faster than unprotected iron, because the iron now loses electrons in preference to the tin.
For sacrificial protection you must say the protecting metal is more reactive than iron and is oxidised instead of iron (or "loses electrons more readily"). "The zinc protects the iron" alone scores nothing. Remember the sacrificial metal must be replaced when it has corroded away.

5.3 Electroplating

Electroplating uses electrolysis to coat a metal object with a thin layer of another metal. It is used to improve appearance (e.g. silver or gold plating) and/or resistance to corrosion (e.g. chromium or tin plating).

The set-up:

  • The object to be plated is the cathode (negative electrode).
  • The anode (positive electrode) is made of the plating metal.
  • The electrolyte is a solution containing ions of the plating metal.

At the cathode, metal ions gain electrons and are deposited on the object. At the anode, metal atoms lose electrons and go into solution, replacing the ions used up — so the concentration of the electrolyte stays the same.

Plating metalPurposeExample
Silver / goldAttractive appearance at low cost; resists tarnishingCutlery, jewellery
ChromiumShiny and hard; resists corrosionTaps, bicycle handlebars

5.4 Alloys

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually other metals, sometimes carbon). Converting a pure metal into an alloy often increases its strength (and hardness).

Why alloys are stronger — the model

In a pure metal all the atoms are the same size and are arranged in regular layers. When a force is applied, the layers can slide over each other easily, so pure metals are soft and malleable. In an alloy, atoms of different sizes distort the regular layers, so it is much more difficult for the layers to slide — the alloy is stronger and harder.

The marking points are: pure metal → regular layers of identical atoms → layers slide; alloy → different-sized atoms → disrupt / distort the layers → harder for layers to slide → stronger. Do not say the bonds in an alloy are "stronger".

Alloy steels

Pure iron is too soft for most uses, and the iron straight from the blast furnace (cast iron, about 4% carbon) is brittle. Iron is therefore made into steels by controlling the carbon content and alloying it with other metals such as chromium, nickel, manganese, tungsten or vanadium. Each alloy steel has properties suited to a particular use.

5.5 Concentration of Solutions

Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution. It can be measured in g dm⁻³ (mass per volume) or mol dm⁻³ (moles per volume). 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.
concentration (g dm⁻³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)

(HT) Calculating concentrations in mol dm⁻³, and converting between g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³ (spec 5.8C), is Higher tier only:

concentration (mol dm⁻³) = amount of solute (mol) ÷ volume of solution (dm³) g dm⁻³ = mol dm⁻³ × Mr     ↔     mol dm⁻³ = g dm⁻³ ÷ Mr
(HT) 5.85 g of sodium chloride (Mr = 58.5) is dissolved in water to make 250 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration in (a) g dm⁻³ and (b) mol dm⁻³.

Volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³
(a) concentration = 5.85 ÷ 0.250 = 23.4 g dm⁻³
(b) moles = 5.85 ÷ 58.5 = 0.100 mol; concentration = 0.100 ÷ 0.250 = 0.400 mol dm⁻³
Check: 0.400 × 58.5 = 23.4 g dm⁻³ ✓
(HT) A solution of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, has a concentration of 4.9 g dm⁻³. Convert this to mol dm⁻³. (Ar: H = 1, S = 32, O = 16)

Mr = (2 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16) = 98
concentration = 4.9 ÷ 98 = 0.050 mol dm⁻³

5.6 Core Practical: Acid–Alkali Titration (CP5)

A titration is used to find the exact volume of one solution that reacts with a measured volume of another. If the concentration of one is known, the other can be calculated.

Method

  1. Rinse the pipette with the alkali and the burette with the acid (removes water/other substances that would dilute them).
  2. Use a pipette and pipette filler to transfer 25.0 cm³ of alkali (e.g. sodium hydroxide) into a conical flask. Read the bottom of the meniscus on the mark at eye level.
  3. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator (e.g. methyl orange or phenolphthalein) and place the flask on a white tile.
  4. Fill the burette with acid using a funnel (then remove the funnel), make sure the jet below the tap is filled, and record the initial reading to the nearest 0.05 cm³.
  5. Add acid while swirling the flask. Do a quick rough (range-finder) titration first.
  6. Repeat accurately: near the expected end-point add acid drop by drop until the indicator just changes colour permanently. Record the final reading.
  7. Calculate the titre (final − initial). Repeat until you have concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and calculate the mean of the concordant titres only.
IndicatorColour in alkaliColour in acidUse?
PhenolphthaleinPinkColourlessYes — sharp change
Methyl orangeYellowRed (orange at end-point)Yes — sharp change
Universal indicator / litmusRange of coloursRange of coloursNo — gradual colour change gives no sharp end-point

Recording and processing results

RoughRun 1Run 2Run 3
Final reading / cm³23.1022.4544.6022.60
Initial reading / cm³0.000.0022.450.20
Titre / cm³23.1022.4522.1522.40

Run 2 (22.15 cm³) is not within 0.10 cm³ of the others, so it is not concordant (probably the end-point was misjudged). Concordant titres: 22.45 and 22.40 cm³. Mean = (22.45 + 22.40) ÷ 2 = 22.425 = 22.43 cm³. The rough titre is never included in the mean.

Precision answers: a pipette measures a fixed volume very accurately (better than a measuring cylinder); a burette delivers variable volumes accurately; the white tile makes the colour change easier to see; swirling mixes the reactants; adding dropwise avoids overshooting the end-point. To make a pure salt from the titration, repeat with the same volumes but no indicator, then evaporate and crystallise.

5.7 Titration Calculations (HT)

Use the results of a titration to find an unknown concentration or an unknown volume. Follow four steps every time:

  1. Write the balanced equation and note the mole ratio.
  2. Calculate the moles of the substance whose volume and concentration you know: moles = concentration × volume (dm³).
  3. Use the ratio to find the moles of the other substance.
  4. Calculate the unknown: concentration = moles ÷ volume (dm³), or volume = moles ÷ concentration.
(HT) 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 21.40 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide in mol dm⁻³ and in g dm⁻³. (Mr NaOH = 40)

NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O   (ratio 1 : 1)
moles HCl = 0.100 × (21.40 ÷ 1000) = 0.002140 mol
moles NaOH = 0.002140 mol
concentration NaOH = 0.002140 ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.0856 mol dm⁻³
in g dm⁻³: 0.0856 × 40 = 3.42 g dm⁻³
(HT) 25.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide is neutralised by 18.75 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid. Calculate the concentration of the acid.

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O   (ratio 1 : 2)
moles NaOH = 0.150 × 0.0250 = 0.00375 mol
moles H₂SO₄ = 0.00375 ÷ 2 = 0.001875 mol
concentration H₂SO₄ = 0.001875 ÷ 0.01875 = 0.100 mol dm⁻³
(HT) What volume of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is needed to neutralise 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ potassium hydroxide?

KOH + HCl → KCl + H₂O   (1 : 1)
moles KOH = 0.150 × 0.0200 = 0.00300 mol = moles HCl
volume HCl = 0.00300 ÷ 0.200 = 0.0150 dm³ = 15.0 cm³
The two classic errors are forgetting to divide cm³ by 1000, and ignoring the mole ratio for sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄ reacts with 2 NaOH). Give answers to 3 significant figures, matching the data.

5.8 Percentage Yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product that could be made from the reactants, calculated from the balanced equation. The actual yield is the mass of product actually obtained.
percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100

Why the actual yield is less than the theoretical yield

  • Incomplete reactions — some reactant does not react (e.g. a reversible reaction reaches equilibrium, or the reaction is stopped too soon).
  • Practical losses during the experiment — product left behind in apparatus, lost when filtering, transferring liquids or during evaporation and crystallisation.
  • Competing, unwanted reactions (side reactions) — some reactants form different products.
50.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. 22.4 g of calcium oxide is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield. (Mr: CaCO₃ = 100, CaO = 56)

From the equation, 100 g of CaCO₃ gives 56 g of CaO, so 50.0 g gives a theoretical yield of 56 × (50.0 ÷ 100) = 28.0 g
percentage yield = (22.4 ÷ 28.0) × 100 = 80.0%
A student expects to make 12.0 g of magnesium sulfate. The percentage yield is 65%. What mass did the student actually obtain?

actual yield = 65 ÷ 100 × 12.0 = 7.8 g

5.9 Atom Economy

Atom economy is a measure of the proportion of the reactants that end up as the desired (useful) product. A high atom economy means less waste.
atom economy = (relative formula mass of the desired product ÷ sum of relative formula masses of all reactants) × 100%

Use the balancing numbers from the equation. If there is only one product (an addition reaction), the atom economy is 100%.

Calculate the atom economy for making calcium oxide by heating limestone: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (Mr: CaCO₃ = 100, CaO = 56)

atom economy = 56 ÷ 100 × 100 = 56% (the other 44% leaves as CO₂ waste)
Iron is produced by: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Calculate the atom economy for iron. (Ar: Fe = 56, C = 12, O = 16)

Mr Fe₂O₃ = 160; 3 × Mr CO = 3 × 28 = 84 → total reactants = 244
desired product = 2 × 56 = 112
atom economy = 112 ÷ 244 × 100 = 45.9%

Choosing a reaction pathway (HT)

Industry chooses a route to a product by weighing up several factors, not just one:

FactorWhat to look for
Atom economyHigh — less waste, more efficient use of raw materials, more sustainable
YieldHigh percentage yield — more product from the same reactants
RateFast enough to make product in an acceptable time
Equilibrium positionFor reversible reactions, conditions that give an acceptable proportion of product
Usefulness of by-productsIf the by-product can be sold or used, a low atom economy matters less
OtherCost and availability (renewable or finite) of raw materials, energy needed, safety
(HT) Compare the two routes to ethanol shown above.

Hydration of ethene: atom economy 100%, fast, continuous process and gives pure ethanol — but needs high temperature (~300 °C) and pressure (~60–70 atm) with a phosphoric acid catalyst, and ethene comes from crude oil, a finite resource.
Fermentation: atom economy only 51% and slow (batch process, takes days), producing a dilute solution that must be distilled — but uses renewable sugar from crops at low temperature (~30 °C), and the CO₂ by-product can be captured and sold.
Conclusion: where crude oil is cheap and plentiful, hydration is often chosen for its efficiency; where crops are plentiful and oil is expensive, fermentation may be preferred.

5.10 Molar Volume of Gases (HT)

One mole of any gas occupies the same volume at the same temperature and pressure. At room temperature and pressure (RTP) — 20 °C and 1 atmosphere — the molar volume of a gas is 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³).
volume of gas (dm³) = amount (mol) × 24    ↔    amount (mol) = volume (dm³) ÷ 24 volume of gas (cm³) = amount (mol) × 24 000
(HT) Calculate the volume of hydrogen produced at RTP when 0.48 g of magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. (Ar Mg = 24)

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂   (Mg : H₂ = 1 : 1)
moles Mg = 0.48 ÷ 24 = 0.020 mol → 0.020 mol H₂
volume H₂ = 0.020 × 24 = 0.48 dm³ (480 cm³)
(HT) What mass of calcium carbonate must react with excess acid to produce 600 cm³ of carbon dioxide at RTP? (Mr CaCO₃ = 100)

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂   (1 : 1)
moles CO₂ = 600 ÷ 24 000 = 0.0250 mol → 0.0250 mol CaCO₃
mass = 0.0250 × 100 = 2.50 g

Avogadro's law — reacting volumes of gases

Avogadro's law: equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules. So for reactions between gases, the ratio of volumes is the same as the mole ratio in the balanced equation — you do not need to convert to moles at all.

(HT) Propane burns completely: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l). What volume of oxygen is needed to burn 20 cm³ of propane, and what volume of carbon dioxide is produced? (All volumes at RTP.)

Volume ratio propane : oxygen : carbon dioxide = 1 : 5 : 3
oxygen = 5 × 20 = 100 cm³
carbon dioxide = 3 × 20 = 60 cm³
(The water is a liquid at RTP, so it has a negligible volume and is not included.)
(HT) 50 cm³ of carbon monoxide is burned in 40 cm³ of oxygen: 2CO(g) + O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g). What is the total volume of gas at the end (RTP)?

50 cm³ CO needs 25 cm³ O₂ → oxygen is in excess by 40 − 25 = 15 cm³.
CO₂ formed = 50 cm³.
Total gas = 50 + 15 = 65 cm³
The 24 dm³ value is given on the exam paper when needed, but you must know when to use it: only for gases, and only at RTP. Watch the units — if the volume is in cm³ use 24 000.

5.11 Dynamic Equilibria in Industry — the Haber Process

Recall from Topic 4 that the Haber process is a reversible reaction between nitrogen and hydrogen forming ammonia:

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    forward reaction exothermic

(HT) Industrial chemists must choose conditions that give an acceptable yield in an acceptable time at an acceptable cost. The choice depends on the availability and cost of raw materials and energy, and on the temperature, pressure and catalyst.

ConditionEffect on yieldEffect on rateCost / practical issuesChoice
TemperatureLower T gives a higher yield (forward reaction is exothermic)Lower T makes the reaction too slowHeating costs energy450 °C — compromise between yield and rate
PressureHigher P gives a higher yield (4 gas molecules → 2)Higher P increases rate (particles closer together)Very high pressure needs expensive, thick-walled plant and pumps; more energy to compress gases; greater safety risk200 atm — compromise between yield and cost/safety
CatalystNo effect on yieldIncreases rate — equilibrium reached fasterCheap (iron); lasts a long time; allows a lower temperatureIron

Rate of attainment of equilibrium (HT)

Changing the conditions affects how quickly equilibrium is reached, as well as (for temperature, pressure and concentration) where the equilibrium position lies:

  • Higher temperature — particles have more energy and collide more often, so equilibrium is reached faster (even though, for the Haber process, the yield at equilibrium is lower).
  • Higher pressure (gases) — particles are closer together, collisions are more frequent, so equilibrium is reached faster.
  • Higher concentration of reactants — more frequent collisions, so equilibrium is reached faster.
  • Catalyst — speeds up the forward and backward reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster but the position of equilibrium (yield) is unchanged.

Raw materials and energy (HT)

  • Nitrogen comes from the air — abundant and free (but separating it uses energy).
  • Hydrogen comes from natural gas (methane) — cheap but a finite fossil fuel.
  • The reaction is exothermic: the heat released is used to pre-heat incoming gases, saving energy.
  • Unreacted N₂ and H₂ are recycled, so over many passes almost all the raw material is converted, even though only about 15% reacts per pass.
(HT) For "explain why 450 °C is used", give both halves: "a lower temperature would give a higher yield because the forward reaction is exothermic, but the rate would be too slow; 450 °C gives an acceptable yield in an acceptable time." The same compromise idea applies to pressure (yield vs cost/safety).

5.12 Fertilisers

Fertilisers provide plants with essential elements to promote growth. The three main elements are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K). NPK fertilisers contain compounds of all three.

Fertilisers must be soluble in water so that plants can absorb the ions through their roots. Many nitrogen fertilisers are ammonium salts or nitrates made from ammonia.

Making ammonium nitrate

Ammonia (an alkali) reacts with nitric acid in a neutralisation reaction to form ammonium nitrate, which is particularly rich in nitrogen (it contains nitrogen in both ions):

ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate NH₃(aq) + HNO₃(aq) → NH₄NO₃(aq)
Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃. (Ar: N = 14, H = 1, O = 16)

Mr = 14 + (4 × 1) + 14 + (3 × 16) = 80
% N = (2 × 14) ÷ 80 × 100 = 35%

Ammonium sulfate — laboratory vs industry

Laboratory preparationIndustrial production
Raw materialsBought ammonia solution and dilute sulfuric acidMust be made first in several stages: ammonia by the Haber process (N₂ from air, H₂ from natural gas); sulfuric acid by the Contact process (from sulfur, air and water)
ScaleSmall scale — a few gramsVery large scale — thousands of tonnes

When comparing lab and industrial preparation, the specification's key words are scale (small vs much larger) and stages (in industry the raw materials ammonia and sulfuric acid have to be manufactured first). Use the words "batch" and "continuous" for extra credit.

5.13 Chemical Cells

A chemical cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy. A simple cell is made by placing two different metals in an electrolyte. A chemical cell produces a voltage until one of the reactants is used up.

The more reactive metal loses electrons more readily, so electrons flow through the external circuit from the more reactive metal to the less reactive metal. The bigger the difference in reactivity between the two metals, the larger the voltage. A battery is two or more cells connected in series.

Metals usedApproximate voltage
Magnesium / copperLarge (about 2 V)
Zinc / copperMedium (about 1 V)
Iron / copperSmall (about 0.5 V)
Copper / copperZero — the metals must be different

In non-rechargeable cells (e.g. alkaline batteries) the chemical reactions stop when one of the reactants has been used up and the cell is "flat". Rechargeable cells can be recharged because the reactions are reversed when an external current is supplied.

5.14 Hydrogen–Oxygen Fuel Cells

In a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell, hydrogen and oxygen are used to produce a voltage, and water is the only product. Unlike a chemical cell, the reactants (fuel and oxygen) are supplied continuously, so the fuel cell keeps producing electricity as long as they are supplied.
hydrogen + oxygen → water     2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

Evaluating fuel cells

StrengthsWeaknesses
The only product is water — no CO₂ or other pollutants at the point of useHydrogen is a gas: it takes up a large volume, so it must be stored under high pressure or as a liquid at very low temperature; tanks are heavy and bulky
More efficient than burning fuel in an engine — fewer energy-transfer stagesHydrogen is highly flammable/explosive, so storage and transport carry risks
Produce a continuous supply of electricity as long as fuel is supplied — no long recharging time (quick refuelling)Hydrogen is usually made from natural gas (releases CO₂) or by electrolysis of water, which needs electricity that may come from fossil fuels
Few moving parts, quiet, little maintenanceFuel cells are expensive (use catalysts such as platinum); few hydrogen refuelling stations
Lighter than rechargeable batteries for the same energy stored in long-range use; no toxic metal waste when disposed ofFuel cells eventually wear out and contain materials that need careful disposal
Evaluate the use of hydrogen fuel cells, rather than rechargeable lithium batteries, to power buses.

For fuel cells: buses can be refuelled in minutes rather than charged for hours, so they can run all day; the only exhaust is water, so there is no local air pollution; a large bus would need a very heavy battery pack.
Against: hydrogen must be stored in large high-pressure tanks and is flammable; hydrogen production often releases CO₂; refuelling infrastructure is limited and fuel cells are expensive.
Judgement: for buses running long routes from a single depot (where one hydrogen supply can be installed), fuel cells are a good choice; for short urban routes, batteries may be cheaper.
"Water is the only product" is only true at the point of use. In evaluation questions, gain the higher marks by mentioning where the hydrogen comes from — if it is made using fossil fuels, CO₂ is still produced overall.

Key Points Summary

  • 5.1C — Most metals are transition metals: high melting point, high density, coloured compounds, catalytic activity (iron in the Haber process).
  • 5.2C, 5.3C — Corrosion is oxidation; rusting needs oxygen and water. Prevent it by excluding oxygen/water (paint, oil, grease, plastic, plating) or by sacrificial protection with a more reactive metal (zinc, magnesium; galvanising).
  • 5.4C — Electroplating: object = cathode, plating metal = anode, electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal; improves appearance and corrosion resistance.
  • 5.5C, 5.6C — Alloys are stronger because different-sized atoms distort the layers so they cannot slide; iron is alloyed (e.g. with chromium/nickel → stainless steel) to make alloy steels.
  • 5.7C — Relate uses to properties: aluminium, copper, gold, magnalium (Al + Mg), brass (Cu + Zn).
  • 5.8C (HT) — concentration (mol dm⁻³) = mol ÷ dm³; convert g dm⁻³ ↔ mol dm⁻³ using Mr.
  • 5.9C (CP5) — Accurate titration: pipette, burette, indicator, white tile, dropwise near end-point, concordant titres within 0.10 cm³.
