Topic 1 — Key Concepts in Chemistry

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 1 and Paper 2 (examined on both papers)

Topic 1 is the toolkit for the whole course. It covers how we write chemical formulae and equations, what atoms are made of, how the periodic table is organised, how atoms bond together, how bonding explains the properties of substances, and how to do the core chemical calculations (relative formula mass, empirical formulae, concentration and the mole). Because it is examined on both papers, marks from this topic turn up in nearly every question — time spent here pays off everywhere.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. Topic 1 is shared with GCSE Combined Science; the separate-chemistry-only content of 1CH0 is concentrated in Topics 5 and 9.

Naming ionic compounds: the metal (or ammonium) is named first. A name ending in -ide means the compound contains just the two elements named (sodium chloride = Na and Cl only; the exception is hydroxide, OH⁻). A name ending in -ate means the negative ion also contains oxygen (copper sulfate contains SO₄²⁻; potassium nitrate contains NO₃⁻).

Word and symbol equations

A word equation names the reactants and products. A balanced symbol equation uses formulae and has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, because atoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. You balance an equation by changing the large numbers in front of formulae, never the subscripts inside them.

magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

Ionic equations (HT)

An ionic equation shows only the particles that actually change in a reaction. Ions that are present but do not take part are called spectator ions and are left out.

  1. Write the balanced symbol equation with state symbols.
  2. Split every aqueous ionic substance into its ions (solids, liquids, gases and covalent molecules stay as they are).
  3. Cross out ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
  4. Check the atoms and the total charge balance.
Write the ionic equation for the precipitation of silver chloride:
AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
Ag⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) + Na⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)
Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectator ions, so: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

Other ionic equations you will meet: neutralisation, H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l); and a displacement such as Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).

1.2 Atomic Structure

How the model of the atom developed

Our model of the atom has changed as new experimental evidence appeared. Each new model kept what still worked and changed what the evidence disproved.

Scientist / dateModelKey evidence or idea
Dalton (early 1800s)Atoms are tiny solid spheres that cannot be divided; atoms of one element are identical; different elements have different atomsExplained fixed proportions in which elements combine
Thomson (1897)"Plum pudding": a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in itDiscovery of the electron — atoms contain smaller particles, so they are divisible
Rutherford (1909–11)Nuclear model: tiny, dense, positive nucleus containing most of the mass, surrounded by mostly empty space where electrons areAlpha-particle scattering (Geiger & Marsden): most alpha particles passed straight through gold foil; a few were deflected; a very small number bounced back
Bohr (1913)Electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels (shells) at specific distancesExplained why atoms emit and absorb light only at certain energies; calculations agreed with observations
Later work (Chadwick, 1932)Nucleus contains protons and neutronsDiscovery of the neutron, explaining why atomic masses were larger than the proton count suggested
For the alpha-scattering experiment, link each observation to a conclusion: most passed straight through → the atom is mostly empty space; some were deflected → the nucleus is positively charged; very few bounced back → the nucleus is very small and contains most of the mass.

Structure of the atom

An atom has a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons arranged in shells (energy levels).

ParticleRelative massRelative chargeLocation
Proton1+1Nucleus
Neutron10Nucleus
Electron1/1836 (negligible)−1Shells around the nucleus
  • An atom is neutral overall because it contains equal numbers of protons and electrons; their charges cancel.
  • The nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom: its radius is about 1/100 000 of the atom's radius.
  • Almost all of the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons are far heavier than electrons.
  • Atoms are about 10⁻¹⁰ m (0.1 nm) across, and simple molecules are of the same order of magnitude (about 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁹ m). The nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁴ m across.

Atomic number and mass number

Atomic number (Z) = the number of protons in the nucleus. All atoms of the same element have the same number of protons, and this is what identifies the element.
Mass number (A) = the total number of protons + neutrons in the nucleus.
Number of neutrons = A − Z.

Atoms are written in the notation ᴬZX, with the mass number on top and the atomic number below, e.g. ¹²₆C, ³⁵₁₇Cl, ⁵⁶₂₆Fe.

How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in an atom of ³⁹₁₉K?
Protons = Z = 19; electrons = 19 (neutral atom); neutrons = 39 − 19 = 20.

1.3 Isotopes and Relative Atomic Mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers.

Isotopes of an element have the same electronic configuration, so they have the same chemical properties. Their physical properties that depend on mass (such as density) differ slightly.

Why relative atomic masses are not whole numbers

Most elements exist naturally as a mixture of isotopes. The relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) is the weighted mean mass of the atoms of an element, taking into account the mass and the abundance of each isotope, compared with 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Because it is an average, it is often not a whole number (e.g. Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5).

Calculating relative atomic mass (HT)

Aᵣ = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100
(HT) Chlorine is 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl. Calculate the relative atomic mass of chlorine.
Aᵣ = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5
(HT) Copper consists of 69.2% ⁶³Cu and 30.8% ⁶⁵Cu. Calculate Aᵣ(Cu) to 1 decimal place.
Aᵣ = (63 × 69.2 + 65 × 30.8) ÷ 100 = (4359.6 + 2002.0) ÷ 100 = 63.6
Sanity-check your answer: Aᵣ must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses and be closer to the more abundant isotope. If abundances are given as ratios (e.g. 3 : 1), divide by the total of the ratio (4) instead of 100.

1.4 The Periodic Table

Mendeleev's periodic table

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements in order of increasing relative atomic mass, but he did not follow that order blindly:

  • He placed elements with similar properties in the same vertical group, even if this meant swapping the order of some pairs.
  • He left gaps for elements he believed had not yet been discovered.
  • He used the trends in his table to predict the properties of these missing elements (e.g. "eka-silicon", later found as germanium, with properties very close to his predictions). These successful predictions convinced other scientists that his table was useful.

Mendeleev swapped some pairs, for example tellurium (Aᵣ 127.6) and iodine (Aᵣ 126.9): ordering by mass would have put iodine in the wrong group, so he put iodine with chlorine and bromine, whose properties it matches. Later, the discovery of protons and isotopes explained why this was correct: the elements are really in order of atomic number, and an element's Aᵣ can be "out of order" because of the relative abundance of its heavier isotopes (tellurium has a high proportion of heavy isotopes).

The modern periodic table

  • Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.
  • The horizontal rows are periods; the vertical columns are groups.
  • Elements in the same group have the same number of outer-shell electrons and so similar chemical properties.
  • Metals are found to the left and in the centre; non-metals are found to the right (top right). Most elements are metals.

Electrons occupy shells (energy levels) around the nucleus. The lowest-energy shell (closest to the nucleus) fills first. For the first 20 elements:

  • 1st shell holds up to 2 electrons
  • 2nd shell holds up to 8 electrons
  • 3rd shell: for elements 1–20, holds 8 before electrons go into the 4th shell

Linking configuration to position in the table

  • Number of occupied shells = period number. Chlorine (2.8.7) has 3 shells → Period 3.

1.6 Ionic Bonding

Forming ions

An ion is an atom or group of atoms with an overall electric charge, formed when atoms lose or gain electrons.
Cations are positive ions (electrons lost). Anions are negative ions (electrons gained).
  • Metal atoms lose their outer-shell electrons to form positive ions. Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, Group 3 (aluminium) → 3+.
  • Non-metal atoms gain electrons to fill their outer shell, forming negative ions. Group 6 → 2−, Group 7 → 1−.
  • The ions formed have the electronic configuration of a noble gas (a full outer shell). Na⁺ is 2.8, like neon; Cl⁻ is 2.8.8, like argon.

Protons, neutrons and electrons in ions

Forming an ion changes only the number of electrons; the nucleus is unchanged.

How ionic bonds form

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms. The oppositely charged ions formed are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction — this is an ionic bond.

Formulae of ionic compounds

An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.

Work out the formula of aluminium sulfate.
Ions: Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻. Lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6 → 2 × Al³⁺ (+6) and 3 × SO₄²⁻ (−6).
Formula: Al₂(SO₄)₃ (brackets are needed around a group ion when there is more than one of it).

Structure of ionic compounds

Ionic compounds form giant ionic lattices: a regular three-dimensional arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions, held together by strong electrostatic forces acting in all directions.

1.7 Covalent Bonding

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. Covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms. The bond is strong because both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair.

By sharing electrons, each atom usually achieves a full outer shell. A double bond (two shared pairs) is found in O₂ and CO₂; a triple bond (three shared pairs) is found in N₂.

When drawing dot-and-cross diagrams, put shared pairs in the overlap of the circles and count: every atom (except H, which needs 2) should end with 8 outer electrons. Check the total number of electrons equals the sum of the atoms' outer electrons.

1.8 Structure, Bonding and Properties

Substances can be classified by their structure and bonding into ionic, simple molecular (covalent), giant covalent and metallic. Polymers are a further important group of covalent substances. The type of structure explains the physical properties.

Ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points — a lot of energy is needed to overcome the many strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice.
  • Do not conduct electricity when solid — ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move.
  • Conduct when molten or dissolved in water — the ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Many are soluble in water.

Simple molecular substances

Examples: H₂, O₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CO₂, CH₄. Atoms within each molecule are held by strong covalent bonds, but the forces between molecules (intermolecular forces) are weak.

  • Low melting and boiling points — only the weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome; the covalent bonds do not break when the substance melts or boils. Many are gases or liquids at room temperature.
  • Intermolecular forces get stronger as molecules get larger, so melting and boiling points generally increase with molecule size.
  • Do not conduct electricity — the molecules have no overall charge and there are no free electrons or ions.
The most common lost mark in this topic: saying covalent bonds break when a simple molecular substance boils. They do not — only the intermolecular forces are overcome. Always name which forces are involved.

Giant covalent substances

Very large numbers of atoms joined by covalent bonds in a continuous network, e.g. diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide (silica). They have very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken, and they are insoluble in water.

Polymers

Polymers such as poly(ethene) are very large molecules made of many repeating units joined by strong covalent bonds into long chains. Because the molecules are so large, the intermolecular forces between chains are relatively strong, so polymers are solids at room temperature. Most do not conduct electricity.

Metallic bonding

Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a "sea" of delocalised electrons (the outer-shell electrons, which are free to move through the whole structure).
  • High melting points (mostly) — strong attraction between the positive ions and delocalised electrons.
  • Good conductors of electricity and heat — delocalised electrons carry charge and energy through the structure.
  • Malleable (can be hammered into shape) — the layers of ions can slide over each other while the delocalised electrons keep holding them together.
  • Typical metal properties: shiny, high density, solid at room temperature (except mercury), form positive ions and basic oxides. Typical non-metal properties: dull, brittle, low melting points (except giant covalent ones), poor conductors (except graphite), form negative ions and acidic oxides.

1.9 Calculations: Formula Mass, Empirical Formulae and Concentration

Relative formula mass

Relative formula mass (Mᵣ) is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula.
Calculate Mᵣ of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂. (Ca = 40, O = 16, H = 1)
Mᵣ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74
Calculate Mᵣ of hydrated copper sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O. (Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1)
CuSO₄ = 63.5 + 32 + 64 = 159.5; 5H₂O = 5 × 18 = 90 → Mᵣ = 249.5

Percentage by mass of an element in a compound:

% by mass = (Aᵣ × number of atoms of that element) ÷ Mᵣ × 100

Empirical and molecular formulae

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The molecular formula is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.

Examples: ethane C₂H₆ has empirical formula CH₃; glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ has empirical formula CH₂O. For ionic and giant covalent substances we always use the empirical formula (NaCl, SiO₂).

To find an empirical formula from masses (or percentages):

  1. Write down the mass (or %) of each element.
  2. Divide each by its Aᵣ.
  3. Divide all answers by the smallest one.
  4. If necessary, multiply to get whole numbers (e.g. 1.5 → ×2).
A compound contains 2.4 g of carbon and 0.6 g of hydrogen. Its Mᵣ is 30. Find the empirical and molecular formulae.
C: 2.4 ÷ 12 = 0.2   H: 0.6 ÷ 1 = 0.6 → ratio 0.2 : 0.6 = 1 : 3 → empirical formula CH₃.
Mᵣ of CH₃ = 15; 30 ÷ 15 = 2 → molecular formula C₂H₆.

Practical: finding the empirical formula of magnesium oxide

  1. Weigh a clean, dry crucible and lid. Add a coiled strip of magnesium ribbon (cleaned with sandpaper to remove the oxide layer) and weigh again.
  2. Heat strongly on a pipeclay triangle. Lift the lid slightly from time to time with tongs to let oxygen in, while preventing white magnesium oxide smoke escaping.
  3. When the magnesium no longer glows, remove the lid and heat for a few more minutes. Allow to cool and reweigh.
  4. Repeat heating, cooling and weighing until the mass is constant (heating to constant mass) to ensure all the magnesium has reacted.
Mass of Mg = 0.24 g; mass of magnesium oxide = 0.40 g. Find the empirical formula. (Mg = 24, O = 16)
Mass of O = 0.40 − 0.24 = 0.16 g.
Mg: 0.24 ÷ 24 = 0.010   O: 0.16 ÷ 16 = 0.010 → ratio 1 : 1 → MgO.
Errors in this practical: if MgO smoke escapes, the final mass is too low; if some Mg does not react, the oxygen mass is too low. Both make the ratio of O to Mg smaller than it should be.

Conservation of mass

The law of conservation of mass: no atoms are lost or made during a chemical reaction, so the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants.
  • In a closed system (nothing can enter or leave, e.g. a precipitation reaction in a sealed flask), the mass measured on a balance stays the same.
  • In a non-enclosed system the mass on the balance may appear to change:
    • Increase if a reactant comes from the air — e.g. when magnesium burns, oxygen from the air is added, so the solid gains mass.
    • Decrease if a gaseous product escapes — e.g. thermal decomposition of a metal carbonate (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂), or a carbonate reacting with an acid, loses CO₂ to the air.
12.5 g of calcium carbonate is heated until it fully decomposes, leaving 7.0 g of calcium oxide. What mass of carbon dioxide was produced?
By conservation of mass: 12.5 − 7.0 = 5.5 g of CO₂ escaped.

Reacting masses from a balanced equation

The balanced equation tells you the ratio in which substances react. Multiply each Mᵣ by its balancing number to get the reacting masses, then scale to the mass you are given. (Higher-tier students can also do this with moles — see 1.10.)

What mass of magnesium oxide forms when 6.0 g of magnesium burns completely? 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Mg = 24, O = 16)
From the equation: 2 × 24 = 48 g of Mg gives 2 × 40 = 80 g of MgO.
So 6.0 g of Mg gives 80 × (6.0 ÷ 48) = 10 g of MgO.

Concentration in g dm⁻³

concentration (g dm⁻³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)

1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so divide a volume in cm³ by 1000 to convert to dm³.

5.0 g of sodium hydroxide is dissolved in water to make 250 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration.
Volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³. Concentration = 5.0 ÷ 0.250 = 20 g dm⁻³.
What mass of solute is in 40 cm³ of a 15 g dm⁻³ solution?
Mass = concentration × volume = 15 × 0.040 = 0.60 g.

1.10 The Mole (HT)

One mole of a substance contains the same number of particles (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons) as there are atoms in 12 g of carbon-12. This number is the Avogadro constant: 6.02 × 10²³ per mole.
The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its Aᵣ or Mᵣ (e.g. 1 mol of H₂O has a mass of 18 g).
number of moles = mass (g) ÷ Mᵣ   ↔   n = m ÷ Mᵣ number of particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³
How many molecules are in 8.8 g of carbon dioxide? (C = 12, O = 16)
Mᵣ(CO₂) = 44 → moles = 8.8 ÷ 44 = 0.20 mol.
Molecules = 0.20 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.2 × 10²³ (2 s.f.). Number of atoms = 3 × that = 3.6 × 10²³.

Reacting masses using moles

  1. Calculate the moles of the substance whose mass you know (n = m ÷ Mᵣ).
  2. Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation to find moles of the substance you want.
  3. Convert back to mass (m = n × Mᵣ).
What mass of iron is produced from 32 g of iron(III) oxide? Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (Fe = 56, O = 16)
Mᵣ(Fe₂O₃) = 160 → moles = 32 ÷ 160 = 0.20 mol.
Ratio Fe₂O₃ : Fe = 1 : 2 → moles Fe = 0.40 mol.
Mass Fe = 0.40 × 56 = 22.4 g.

Deducing an equation from reacting masses

If you know the masses of all reactants and products, convert each to moles and then find the simplest whole-number ratio. These numbers are the balancing numbers in the equation.

4.8 g of magnesium reacts with 3.2 g of oxygen to form 8.0 g of magnesium oxide. Deduce the balanced equation. (Mg = 24, O = 16)
Mg: 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.20 mol; O₂: 3.2 ÷ 32 = 0.10 mol; MgO: 8.0 ÷ 40 = 0.20 mol.
Ratio 0.20 : 0.10 : 0.20 = 2 : 1 : 2 → 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.

Limiting reactants

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely used up; it limits the amount of product that can form. The other reactant is in excess — some is left over at the end.

The mass of product formed is directly proportional to the amount of the limiting reactant: double the limiting reactant and you double the product (as long as the other reactant stays in excess). Chemists often use an excess of one (cheaper) reactant to make sure all of a more expensive reactant reacts.

6.0 g of magnesium is added to a solution containing 0.40 mol of HCl. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Which reactant is limiting, and what mass of hydrogen forms? (Mg = 24, H = 1)
Moles Mg = 6.0 ÷ 24 = 0.25 mol. To react fully it would need 2 × 0.25 = 0.50 mol HCl, but only 0.40 mol is available → HCl is limiting, Mg is in excess.
Moles H₂ = 0.40 ÷ 2 = 0.20 mol → mass = 0.20 × 2 = 0.40 g.
To find the limiting reactant, divide the moles of each reactant by its balancing number in the equation; the smallest result is the limiting reactant. Always base the product calculation on the limiting reactant.

  • Recall formulae of common elements, compounds and ions; -ide = two elements, -ate = contains oxygen.

  • (HT) Ionic equations leave out spectator ions — e.g. H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
  • Hazard symbols: flammable, oxidising, toxic, corrosive, harmful; match each hazard to a precaution.
  • Atomic model: Dalton → Thomson (electron) → Rutherford (nucleus, alpha scattering) → Bohr (shells) → neutron (Chadwick).
  • Proton: mass 1, charge +1. Neutron: mass 1, charge 0. Electron: mass 1/1836, charge −1. Atoms ~10⁻¹⁰ m; nucleus ~1/100 000 of atomic radius but contains almost all the mass.
  • Atomic number = protons; mass number = protons + neutrons. Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons.
  • Aᵣ = weighted mean of isotope masses, so often not a whole number; (HT) calculate it from isotope masses and abundances.
  • Mendeleev ordered by atomic mass, left gaps, predicted properties and swapped pairs such as Te/I; the modern table is ordered by atomic number.
  • Electronic configuration (first 20 elements): shells = period; outer electrons = group.
  • Ionic bonding: electron transfer from metal to non-metal; giant lattice; high mp; conducts when molten or aqueous.
  • Covalent bonding: shared pairs between non-metals; simple molecules have weak intermolecular forces and low mp; giant covalent structures have very high mp.
  • Diamond (4 bonds, hard, non-conductor), graphite (3 bonds, layers, conducts), graphene (single layer), fullerenes (C₆₀, nanotubes).
  • Metallic bonding: positive ions + delocalised electrons → conductors, malleable.
  • Every model of bonding has limitations — know at least one for each.
  • Mᵣ = sum of Aᵣ; empirical formula = simplest whole-number ratio (MgO practical: heat to constant mass).
  • Mass is conserved; apparent changes in open systems come from gases entering or leaving.
  • Reacting masses: scale the masses given by (Mᵣ × balancing number) in the equation.
  • Concentration (g dm⁻³) = mass ÷ volume in dm³.
  • (HT) Moles = mass ÷ Mᵣ; Avogadro constant 6.02 × 10²³; use mole ratios for reacting masses, deducing equations and limiting reactants.
