Topic 9 — Separate Chemistry 2

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 2 — chemistry-only content

This topic brings together four areas that appear only in GCSE Chemistry: qualitative analysis (identifying ions with chemical tests and instruments), organic chemistry beyond crude oil (alkanes, alkenes, alcohols and carboxylic acids), polymers (addition, condensation and natural polymers), and the bulk and surface properties of materials, including nanoparticles.

The whole of Topic 9 is chemistry-only — it is not in Combined Science (in the specification every point is labelled with a "C"). It contains two core practicals: identifying the ions in unknown salts, and comparing the temperature rise produced by burning different alcohols.

9.1 Tests for Cations — Flame Tests

The test for any ion must be unique: it must give a result that no other ion gives, otherwise a positive result could not tell you which ion is present. For example, calcium and aluminium ions both give a white precipitate with a few drops of sodium hydroxide, so you must go on to add excess (or use a flame test) to tell them apart; adding dilute acid first in the anion tests removes carbonate ions that would otherwise also give a precipitate.

Many metal ions produce a characteristic flame colour when their compounds are heated strongly. The heat excites electrons, which then release energy as light of particular colours.

Metal ionFlame colour
Lithium, Li⁺red (crimson)
Sodium, Na⁺yellow (orange-yellow)
Potassium, K⁺lilac
Calcium, Ca²⁺orange-red (brick red)
Copper, Cu²⁺blue-green

Method

  1. Clean a nichrome (or platinum) wire loop by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and holding it in a blue Bunsen flame until it gives no colour.
  2. Dip the loop in the acid again, then into the solid sample so some sticks to it.
  3. Hold the loop at the edge of a blue (roaring) flame and observe the colour.
  4. Clean the wire thoroughly between tests so samples don't contaminate each other.
Limitations of flame tests: if a sample contains a mixture of metal ions, one colour can mask another (sodium's intense yellow hides most others). Some ions give no flame colour, and colours are judged by eye (subjective). This is one reason instrumental methods such as flame photometry are useful (9.4).

Why add acid first?

  • In the halide and sulfate tests, the acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would otherwise also form a precipitate with silver or barium ions and give a false positive.
  • For the halide test you must use nitric acid, not hydrochloric acid — HCl contains chloride ions, which would give a white precipitate whatever the sample.
  • For the sulfate test you use hydrochloric acid, not sulfuric acid — H₂SO₄ contains sulfate ions.
A really common exam question: "Why is dilute nitric acid added before silver nitrate?" Answer: to react with/remove any carbonate ions, which would also give a precipitate. "Why not hydrochloric acid?" Because it adds chloride ions, giving a white precipitate even if the sample has no chloride.

9.4 Core Practical — Identifying the Ions in Unknown Salts

The core practical uses the tests above to identify the cation and anion in unknown salts.

Safety and good practice

  • Wear eye protection; sodium hydroxide is corrosive and silver nitrate stains skin. Barium chloride is toxic. Concentrated HCl used to clean wires is corrosive.
  • Use small amounts (a few drops) of reagents and add NaOH dropwise so you see the first precipitate before testing with excess.
  • Use a fresh, clean test tube for every test to avoid contamination.
An unknown white solid gives an orange-red flame. A solution of it gives a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH that does not dissolve in excess. With dilute HCl and BaCl₂ solution it gives a white precipitate. Identify the salt.
Orange-red flame + white precipitate insoluble in excess → Ca²⁺. White precipitate with BaCl₂ → SO₄²⁻. The salt is calcium sulfate, CaSO₄.
A green solution gives a green precipitate with NaOH(aq) that slowly turns brown at the surface. With dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate it gives a white precipitate. Identify the salt and (HT) write the ionic equation for the precipitate with NaOH.
Green precipitate → Fe²⁺; white ppt with AgNO₃ → Cl⁻. Salt = iron(II) chloride, FeCl₂.
Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)

9.5 Instrumental Methods and Flame Photometry

Instrumental methods of analysis use machines to detect and measure substances. Compared with simple chemical tests, they can improve:

  • sensitivity — they can detect very small amounts of a substance,
  • accuracy — results are reliable and not dependent on judging colours by eye,
  • speed — tests are quick and can be automated, running many samples.

Disadvantages: the equipment is expensive, needs trained operators, and results usually have to be compared with reference data.

Flame photometry

In a flame photometer, a solution is sprayed into a flame. The metal ions emit light; the instrument measures the wavelengths of light given out and the intensity of that light. Each metal ion produces a characteristic emission spectrum (a pattern of lines of specific wavelengths).

You must be able to evaluate data from a flame photometer to:

  1. identify metal ions by comparing the data (e.g. emission line wavelengths) with reference data. This works even in mixtures, because each ion's lines appear separately — unlike a flame test where colours mask each other;
  2. determine the concentration of ions in dilute solution using a calibration curve.

Using a calibration curve

  1. Make up a series of standard solutions of the metal ion with known concentrations.
  2. Measure the emission intensity (reading) for each standard with the flame photometer.
  3. Plot reading (y) against concentration (x) and draw a line of best fit — the calibration curve.
  4. Measure the reading for the unknown solution, then read across to the line and down to find its concentration.
(Illustrative data.) Using the calibration curve above, a sample of mineral water gives a reading of 42. Read across from 42 to the line, then down: concentration of Na⁺ = 5.0 mg per dm³. If the sample had been diluted 10 times before measuring, the original concentration would be 5.0 × 10 = 50 mg per dm³.
A calibration curve only gives reliable values within the range of the standards measured — don't extrapolate far beyond it. If the reading is too high, dilute the sample by a known factor and multiply back.

9.6 Alkanes and Alkenes

Alkanes — saturated hydrocarbons

Alkanes are hydrocarbons containing only single C–C bonds. They are saturated: each carbon atom forms four single bonds, so no more atoms can be added to the molecule. General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
NameMolecular formulaStructural formula
methaneCH₄CH₄
ethaneC₂H₆CH₃CH₃
propaneC₃H₈CH₃CH₂CH₃
butaneC₄H₁₀CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃

Alkenes — unsaturated hydrocarbons

Alkenes are hydrocarbons containing a carbon–carbon double bond, C=C. They are unsaturated: the double bond can open up so that more atoms can be added. The C=C double bond is the functional group of the alkenes. General formula CₙH₂ₙ.
NameMolecular formulaStructural formula
etheneC₂H₄CH₂=CH₂
propeneC₃H₆CH₂=CHCH₃
butene (but-1-ene)C₄H₈CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃

There is no "methene" — an alkene needs at least two carbon atoms to have a C=C bond. Butene has isomers (the double bond can be in different positions); you only need to draw one correct structure.

Addition reaction with bromine — the test for alkenes

Alkenes undergo addition reactions: the C=C double bond opens and atoms add across it, forming a saturated product. With bromine:

C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂    (ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane) CH₂=CH₂ + Br–Br → CH₂Br–CH₂Br
Test for an alkene (C=C): shake the compound with bromine water (orange). An alkene decolourises it — orange → colourless. An alkane does not react, so the bromine water stays orange.
Say "colourless", never "clear". Clear means see-through (orange bromine water is clear too!). Bromine water decolourising shows a C=C bond is present — the molecule is unsaturated.

Combustion of alkanes and alkenes

The complete combustion of alkanes and alkenes involves the oxidation of the hydrocarbons to produce carbon dioxide and water:

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O    (propane) C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O    (ethene)

Alkenes tend to burn with a smokier flame than alkanes in air, because they have a higher proportion of carbon, so incomplete combustion is more likely. (Incomplete combustion is covered in Topic 8.)

9.7 Alcohols

Alcohols are a homologous series of compounds containing the –OH functional group (hydroxyl group). The general formula is CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH.
AlcoholMolecular formulaStructural formula
methanolCH₃OHCH₃OH
ethanolC₂H₅OHCH₃CH₂OH
propanol (propan-1-ol)C₃H₇OHCH₃CH₂CH₂OH
butanol (butan-1-ol)C₄H₉OHCH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH

Only the straight-chain alcohols with –OH on the end carbon (propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol) are required. Displayed formulae: draw the alkane chain and replace one end H with –O–H, e.g. ethanol: H–C(H)(H)–C(H)(H)–O–H.

Alcohols can be dehydrated (water removed) to form alkenes, e.g. ethanol → ethene + water: C₂H₅OH → C₂H₄ + H₂O.

When drawing the displayed formula of an alcohol, show the O–H bond (draw –O–H, not just –OH, if the question says "displayed"). The –OH in an alcohol is covalently bonded — alcohols are not alkalis and do not contain hydroxide ions.

Making ethanol by fermentation

A solution of ethanol is made by the fermentation of carbohydrates (sugars) using yeast:

C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g)
  1. Dissolve a sugar (e.g. glucose or sucrose) in water and add yeast.
  2. Keep the mixture warm — about 30–40 °C (the optimum temperature for the yeast enzymes; above this the enzymes are denatured, below it the reaction is too slow).
  3. Exclude air (anaerobic conditions) — e.g. use an air lock. With oxygen present, the yeast respires aerobically and microbes can oxidise the ethanol to ethanoic acid.
  4. The reaction is complete when bubbles of CO₂ stop. Fermentation stops when the ethanol concentration reaches about 15% because the ethanol kills the yeast.
  5. Remove the yeast by filtration.

Concentrating ethanol by fractional distillation

The dilute ethanol solution is concentrated by fractional distillation. Ethanol (b.p. 78 °C) has a lower boiling point than water (100 °C), so it evaporates first, rises up the fractionating column and is condensed and collected. The temperature at the top of the column stays at about 78 °C while ethanol is distilling. This is how spirits (e.g. whisky, vodka) are made, and how ethanol is concentrated for use as a fuel.

Oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid

Ethanol can be oxidised to ethanoic acid, either by microbes (bacteria) in air, or by chemical oxidising agents (e.g. acidified potassium dichromate, which turns from orange to green). This is why an opened bottle of wine left exposed to air turns sour — vinegar is a dilute solution of ethanoic acid.

CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O    ([O] = oxygen from the oxidising agent)

9.8 Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids are a homologous series containing the –COOH functional group (carboxyl group). General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH.
Carboxylic acidFormulaCarbon atomsNote
methanoic acidHCOOH1in ant and nettle stings
ethanoic acidCH₃COOH2vinegar
propanoic acidC₂H₅COOH (CH₃CH₂COOH)3food preservative
butanoic acidC₃H₇COOH (CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH)4smell of rancid butter
Count the carbons carefully — the carbon in –COOH is included in the name. Butanoic acid has four carbons: C₃H₇COOH, not C₄H₉COOH. The displayed –COOH group is C double-bonded to one O and single-bonded to an O–H.

Reactions of carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids dissolve in water to form acidic solutions (pH below 7). They are weak acids — only partially ionised in water — so they have a higher pH than a strong acid of the same concentration and react more slowly. They show typical acid reactions:

  • With metal carbonates → salt + water + carbon dioxide (fizzing):
    2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂ (the salt is sodium ethanoate)
  • With reactive metals → salt + hydrogen: 2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂
  • With alkalis → salt + water: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
Homologous seriesFunctional groupEnding of nameExample
alkanesnone-aneethane, C₂H₆
alkenesC=C-eneethene, C₂H₄
alcohols–OH-olethanol, C₂H₅OH

Members of the same homologous series have similar chemical reactions because their molecules contain the same functional group. So you can predict products for other members: e.g. propanol can be oxidised to propanoic acid, and propanoic acid reacts with sodium carbonate to give sodium propanoate, water and carbon dioxide.

9.9 Core Practical — Combustion of Alcohols

Investigate the temperature rise produced in a known mass of water by the combustion of the alcohols ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol.

Method

  1. Measure a known volume of water (e.g. 100 cm³ ≈ 100 g) into a copper calorimeter can; clamp it a fixed distance above the burner. Record the starting temperature.
  2. Weigh the spirit burner (with lid) containing the alcohol.
  3. Light the wick and heat the water, stirring with the thermometer, until the temperature has risen by a set amount (e.g. 20 °C).
  4. Extinguish the flame with the lid and immediately reweigh the burner. Mass of alcohol burned = initial mass − final mass.
  5. Repeat for each alcohol, keeping the mass of water, distance from flame, wick length and temperature rise the same.
  6. Calculate temperature rise per gram of alcohol burned (or mass needed for the fixed temperature rise) to compare.
AlcoholMass burned / gTemperature rise / °CTemperature rise per gram / °C g⁻¹
ethanol1.1020.018.2
propanol0.9520.021.1
butanol0.8820.022.7
pentanol0.8420.023.8

Illustrative data only. Trend: as the chain gets longer, more energy is released per gram (and much more per mole), because each molecule has more C–H and C–C bonds to break and more CO₂ and H₂O bonds formed.

(Illustrative data.) Burning 0.92 g of propanol raises the temperature of 100 g of water from 19.5 °C to 37.0 °C. Find the temperature rise per gram of propanol.
Temperature rise = 37.0 − 19.5 = 17.5 °C. Rise per gram = 17.5 ÷ 0.92 = 19 °C g⁻¹ (2 s.f.).
The measured values are always lower than the true energy released because of heat loss to the surroundings (and to heating the can), and incomplete combustion (soot on the can). Improvements: draught shields, lid on can, keep flame distance fixed and small, stir the water, use a fresh wick. Safety: alcohols are highly flammable — keep stoppered bottles away from flames.

9.10 Addition Polymers

A polymer is a substance of high average relative molecular mass made up of small repeating units. The small molecules that join together are called monomers.

Addition polymerisation

Alkenes (and other monomers with a C=C double bond) join together in addition polymerisation. The double bond in each monomer opens up and the monomers join in a long chain. The polymer is the only product, and it is saturated (the C=C bonds are gone).

n CH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–    ethene → poly(ethene)

Other addition polymers

MonomerMonomer structurePolymerRepeat unit

propeneCH₂=CHCH₃poly(propene)–(CH₂–CH(CH₃))–
chloroetheneCH₂=CHClpoly(chloroethene), PVC–(CH₂–CHCl)–

Deducing structures

  • Monomer → polymer: draw the two C atoms of the C=C as a single bond; keep all the other groups attached in the same positions; add bonds out to each side through square brackets; put n after the bracket.
  • Polymer → monomer: take the two-carbon repeat unit, remove the extension bonds and brackets, and put back a C=C double bond between the two carbons.
The repeat unit of a polymer is –(CH₂–CH(C₆H₅))–. Draw the monomer.
Replace the C–C single bond in the chain by a double bond and remove the extension bonds: CH₂=CH(C₆H₅) — this is phenylethene (styrene); the polymer is poly(phenylethene), "polystyrene".
The main chain of an addition polymer is always carbon atoms only. Side groups (CH₃, Cl, F) hang off it. A very common error is putting the CH₃ of propene into the main chain — the repeat unit of poly(propene) has only two carbons in the backbone.

Problems with polymers

  • Availability of starting materials: most monomers come from crude oil, a finite resource; as it runs out, polymers will become more expensive.
  • Persistence in landfill: most addition polymers are non-biodegradable — microorganisms cannot break them down — so they persist in landfill sites for hundreds of years and pollute oceans (harming wildlife; microplastics).
  • Gases produced during disposal by combustion: burning plastics releases energy but produces CO₂ (greenhouse gas) and can give toxic gases — e.g. PVC releases hydrogen chloride; incomplete combustion gives CO and soot.

9.11 Condensation Polymers (HT)

In condensation polymerisation, monomers with two functional groups join together and a small molecule (usually water) is lost each time a link forms.

Polyesters

A polyester is formed when a monomer with two carboxylic acid groups (a dicarboxylic acid) reacts with a monomer with two alcohol groups (a diol). Each reaction between –COOH and –OH forms an ester link and releases one molecule of water.

n HOOC–□–COOH + n HO–○–OH → –(OC–□–COO–○–O)ₙ– + 2n H₂O
Addition polymerisationCondensation polymerisation
Monomersone type, each with a C=C double bondusually two different monomers, each with two functional groups
Productspolymer onlypolymer and a small molecule (e.g. water)
Examplepoly(ethene), PVC, PTFEpolyesters (e.g. PET for bottles and fibres)

Biodegradable polymers

Some polyesters (e.g. poly(lactic acid), PLA, made from plant starch) are biodegradable — they can be broken down by microorganisms, so they cause fewer landfill problems.

9.14 Nanoparticles and Surface Properties

Nanoscience refers to structures that are 1–100 nm in size, of the order of a few hundred atoms. Nanoparticles are particles in this size range (1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m).

For comparison, atoms are about 0.1–0.5 nm across, so a nanoparticle contains a few hundred atoms; fine dust particles (PM₂.₅) are 100–2500 nm.

Why nanoparticles behave differently

Nanoparticles show different properties from the same material in bulk because of their very high surface area to volume ratio. A much larger fraction of their atoms are on the surface, so:

  • they can be much more reactive and make very effective catalysts (less material needed for the same surface area);
  • their optical, electrical and other properties can be different (e.g. nanoparticles of gold appear red or purple, not gold-coloured; nanoparticulate titanium dioxide is transparent rather than white).

As the side length of a cube decreases by a factor of 10, the surface area to volume ratio increases by a factor of 10.

Calculate the surface area to volume ratio of a cube-shaped nanoparticle of side 20 nm.
SA = 6 × 20² = 2400 nm²; V = 20³ = 8000 nm³; SA : V = 2400 ÷ 8000 = 0.3 nm⁻¹ (or 3 : 10).
A particle of side 2 nm would have SA : V = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 nm⁻¹ — ten times larger.

Applications of nanoparticles

  • Sun creams — nanoparticles of titanium dioxide or zinc oxide absorb/block UV, give better skin coverage and are transparent (no white residue).
  • Medicine — targeted drug delivery (nanoparticles can carry drugs into cells); silver nanoparticles are antibacterial, used in wound dressings and to coat surgical instruments.
  • Catalysts — high surface area makes them very efficient (e.g. in catalytic converters).
  • Electronics — tiny circuits and components; carbon nanotubes conduct electricity.
  • Cosmetics and self-cleaning glass.

Risks of nanoparticles

  • Their properties are not fully understood, and long-term effects have not yet been studied — they are a relatively new technology.
  • Because they are so small, they may be breathed in or absorbed through the skin and enter cells and the bloodstream, possibly causing damage.
  • Nanoparticles that are washed down drains may harm aquatic organisms and the environment (e.g. silver nanoparticles may kill helpful bacteria in sewage works).
  • Some people argue products containing nanoparticles should be tested more thoroughly and clearly labelled.
For a "discuss the use of nanoparticles" question, give a benefit linked to high surface area to volume ratio, then a risk (not fully understood, may enter the body/cells, environmental release), and come to a balanced conclusion. Be careful with units: 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m, not 10⁻⁶ m.

Key Points Summary

  • Flame tests: Li⁺ red, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Cu²⁺ blue-green. Clean nichrome wire with conc. HCl; mixtures can mask colours.
  • NaOH(aq): Al³⁺ white ppt (dissolves in excess), Ca²⁺ white ppt (stays), Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ orange-brown. NH₄⁺: warm with NaOH → NH₃ turns damp red litmus blue.
  • Anions: halides — dilute HNO₃ then AgNO₃: Cl⁻ white, Br⁻ cream, I⁻ yellow. Sulfate — dilute HCl then BaCl₂: white ppt. Carbonate — dilute acid: CO₂ turns limewater milky.
  • Core practical: identify cation and anion in unknown salts by combining these tests; (HT) write ionic equations for precipitates.
  • Instrumental methods improve sensitivity, accuracy and speed. A flame photometer identifies ions by comparing with reference spectra (works for mixtures) and finds concentrations from a calibration curve.
  • Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: methane, ethane, propane, butane) are saturated; alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ: ethene, propene, butene) are unsaturated with a C=C functional group.
  • Alkenes decolourise bromine water (orange → colourless) by addition; complete combustion oxidises hydrocarbons to CO₂ + H₂O.
  • Alcohols have –OH (methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol). Ethanol is made by fermenting sugar with yeast (30–40 °C, no air), then concentrated by fractional distillation.
  • Ethanol can be oxidised (by microbes or chemicals) to ethanoic acid. Carboxylic acids have –COOH (methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic, butanoic); they are weak acids and react with carbonates to give salt + water + CO₂.
  • Core practical: burn ethanol, propanol, butanol, pentanol to heat a known mass of water; compare temperature rise per gram; longer chain → more energy released. Heat loss and incomplete combustion make values low.
  • A polymer has high average relative molecular mass made of small repeating units. Addition polymers: poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC, PTFE — know how to go from monomer to repeat unit and back, and link uses to properties.
  • Polymer problems: crude-oil starting materials, non-biodegradable in landfill, toxic gases when burned; recycling helps but needs collection and sorting.
  • (HT) Polyesters are condensation polymers of a dicarboxylic acid + diol, with water lost at each ester link.
  • DNA (nucleotides), proteins (amino acids), starch and cellulose (sugars) are natural polymers.
  • Compare ceramics (hard, brittle, insulating), polymers (light, flexible, insulating), composites (matrix + reinforcement, e.g. strong and light) and metals (strong, malleable, conductive) to choose materials for a use.
  • Nanoparticles are 1–100 nm; very high surface area to volume ratio gives different properties. Uses: sun creams, catalysts, medicine, antibacterial silver; risks: not fully understood, may enter cells, environmental effects.
