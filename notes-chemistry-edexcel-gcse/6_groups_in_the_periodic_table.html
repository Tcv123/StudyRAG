Topic 6 — Groups in the Periodic Table

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 2

This topic looks closely at three groups of the periodic table: Group 1 (the alkali metals), Group 7 (the halogens) and Group 0 (the noble gases). Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell, so they react in similar ways — and as you go down a group there are clear trends in physical properties and reactivity. The key skill examiners test is using a pattern to predict the properties of an element you have not studied (e.g. rubidium, fluorine, astatine, krypton) and explaining trends in terms of electronic configurations.

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only. Topic 6 is common to GCSE Chemistry and Combined Science — there is no chemistry-only content and no core practical in this topic.

6.1 Classifying Groups 1, 7 and 0

The group number (for Groups 1–7) equals the number of electrons in the outer shell. Elements are classified as alkali metals (Group 1), halogens (Group 7) or noble gases (Group 0) because of their position in the periodic table, which reflects their outer-shell electron arrangement.
GroupNameOuter-shell electronsTypical behaviour
1Alkali metals1Lose 1 electron to form 1+ ions (e.g. Na⁺); very reactive metals
7Halogens7Gain 1 electron to form 1− ions (halide ions, e.g. Cl⁻); reactive non-metals; exist as diatomic molecules (Cl₂)
0Noble gasesFull outer shell (2 for He, 8 for the others)Very unreactive (inert); exist as single atoms (monatomic)
Helium has only 2 outer electrons, yet it is in Group 0 — because its outer shell (the first shell) is full. If asked why helium is placed in Group 0 rather than Group 2, say it has a full outer shell and is unreactive like the other noble gases.

6.2 Group 1 — The Alkali Metals

Physical properties

The alkali metals are unusual metals:

  • They are soft — they can be cut with a knife (softness increases down the group).
  • They have relatively low melting points (decreasing down the group).
  • They have low densities — lithium, sodium and potassium float on water.
  • They are shiny when freshly cut but quickly tarnish as they react with oxygen in the air, so they are stored under oil.

Melting point decreases steadily down the group. Density generally increases (potassium is an exception).

Reactions with water

All alkali metals react with cold water to produce a metal hydroxide and hydrogen. The hydroxide dissolves to form an alkaline solution (pH above 7) — which is why they are called alkali metals.

alkali metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g) 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) 2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g)
MetalObservations with cold water
LithiumFloats; fizzes steadily; moves slowly around the surface; gradually disappears. Does not melt.
SodiumFloats; fizzes rapidly; melts into a silvery ball; moves quickly around the surface; disappears quickly. The hydrogen may ignite with an orange flame if the sodium is held still.
PotassiumFloats; melts; fizzes very violently; the hydrogen ignites spontaneously with a lilac flame; may spit and crackle; disappears very quickly.
"Describe" questions want observations — things you would see or hear (fizzes, floats, melts, moves, lilac flame, gets smaller). "Hydrogen is produced" is a conclusion, not an observation; "bubbles/fizzing" is the observation. Never say "a gas is produced" when you mean "effervescence".

The pattern in reactivity — and predictions

Reactivity with water increases down Group 1: lithium < sodium < potassium. Following the pattern:

  • Rubidium will react even more violently than potassium — it may explode; it will melt instantly and the hydrogen will ignite.
  • Caesium reacts explosively with water.
  • All will still form a metal hydroxide (RbOH, CsOH) and hydrogen.
Predict the equation and the observations for the reaction of rubidium with water.

Equation (same pattern as Li, Na, K): 2Rb(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2RbOH(aq) + H₂(g)
Observations: rubidium is below potassium so it is more reactive; it would react more violently than potassium — immediate melting, very rapid fizzing, the hydrogen ignites and the reaction may be explosive. The solution formed is strongly alkaline.

Explaining the trend in terms of electronic configuration

When an alkali metal reacts, its atoms lose their single outer electron to form a 1+ ion:

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Going down the group:

  1. The atoms have more electron shells, so the atoms get larger.
  2. The outer electron is further from the nucleus (and more shielded by inner shells).
  3. So the attraction between the positive nucleus and the outer electron is weaker.
  4. The outer electron is therefore lost more easily, so the metal is more reactive.
This is a classic 3–4 mark explanation. Work in a chain: more shells → outer electron further from nucleus → weaker attraction → electron lost more easily → more reactive. Do not say "the nucleus has less charge" — the nuclear charge actually increases down the group.

6.3 Group 7 — The Halogens

The halogens are non-metals that exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂) held together by a single covalent bond. They are toxic and have strong smells.

The pattern in physical properties

Going down Group 7:

  • The colour gets darker.
  • Melting and boiling points increase, so the state changes from gas → liquid → solid. This is because the molecules get larger, so the intermolecular forces between them become stronger and more energy is needed to overcome them.
Use the pattern to predict the state and colour of fluorine and astatine at room temperature.

Fluorine is above chlorine: lower melting and boiling points than chlorine, so it is a gas; paler colour, so pale yellow.
Astatine is below iodine: higher melting point than iodine (114 °C), so a solid; darker than iodine, so black.

The test for chlorine

Test for chlorine gas: hold damp blue litmus paper in the gas. Chlorine turns the litmus paper red and then bleaches it white.

The paper must be damp because chlorine reacts with water to form acids, one of which is a bleach. Chlorine is toxic, so this test should be carried out in a fume cupboard.

Write "damp litmus paper is bleached" — both words matter. Saying it "turns white" without "bleached" may not score, and dry litmus paper does not work.

Reactions with metals — forming metal halides

Halogens react with metals to form metal halides, which are ionic salts containing halide ions (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻):

metal + halogen → metal halide 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)    (sodium burns with a bright orange flame; white solid forms) 2Fe(s) + 3Cl₂(g) → 2FeCl₃(s)    (hot iron wool glows; brown solid forms) Mg(s) + Br₂(g) → MgBr₂(s)

The reactivity decreases down the group: chlorine reacts most vigorously with hot iron wool, bromine less vigorously and iodine only slowly. Following the pattern, fluorine reacts explosively with most metals even when cold, and astatine would react very slowly.

Halogen with hot iron woolObservationProduct
ChlorineIron wool glows brightlyIron(III) chloride (orange-brown solid)
Bromine vapourIron wool glows, less brightlyIron(III) bromide (red-brown solid)
Iodine vapourIron wool glows only faintly; slowIron(II) iodide (grey solid)

Reactions with hydrogen — hydrogen halides

Halogens react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides, which are colourless gases:

H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)    (hydrogen chloride) H₂(g) + Br₂(g) → 2HBr(g)    (hydrogen bromide)

Hydrogen halides dissolve in water to form acidic solutions: hydrogen chloride forms hydrochloric acid, HCl(aq), which turns blue litmus red. Again reactivity decreases down the group: hydrogen and fluorine explode even in the dark and cold; hydrogen and chlorine explode in sunlight; hydrogen and iodine react only slowly and reversibly when heated. Astatine would form hydrogen astatide, HAt, which would also dissolve to give an acidic solution.

Predict the product when hydrogen reacts with iodine, and what forms when the product dissolves in water.

Pattern: H₂ + X₂ → 2HX. So H₂(g) + I₂(g) → 2HI(g) — hydrogen iodide (reaction slower than for chlorine or bromine).
Hydrogen iodide dissolves in water to form an acidic solution (hydroiodic acid), pH below 7.

6.4 Halogen Displacement Reactions

A more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halogen from an aqueous solution of its halide (salt). The order of reactivity is chlorine > bromine > iodine.
chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)

Halide solutions (KCl, KBr, KI) are all colourless, so a colour change shows that a reaction has happened and the free halogen has been released.

Halogen water added ↓ / halide solution →Potassium chloride, KClPotassium bromide, KBrPotassium iodide, KI
Chlorine water (very pale green)—✓ Turns orange / yellow-orange (Br₂ formed)✓ Turns brown (I₂ formed)
Bromine water (orange)✗ No reaction (stays orange)—✓ Turns brown (I₂ formed)
Iodine solution (brown)✗ No reaction✗ No reaction—

Carrying out halogen displacement reactions

Although not a core practical, Edexcel often asks you to describe or interpret this experiment:

  1. Place about 2 cm³ of each halide solution (potassium chloride, potassium bromide, potassium iodide) in separate test tubes, or in the wells of a spotting tile.
  2. Add a few drops of chlorine water to each and look for a colour change against a white background.
  3. Repeat with fresh halide solutions using bromine water, then iodine solution.
  4. Record “reaction / no reaction” and the colour in a results grid like the one above.

Safety: chlorine and bromine are toxic and corrosive — use dilute solutions, work in a well-ventilated room or fume cupboard and wear eye protection. Variables: keep the volume and concentration of each solution the same so the colour changes can be compared fairly.

Bromine water and iodine solution are both orange-brown at some concentrations, so a colour change from orange to brown can be hard to judge. Examiners accept “orange/yellow” for bromine and “brown” for iodine in water. If you are given a different colour description in the question, use the question’s wording.

Predicting the reactions of astatine

Astatine is at the bottom of Group 7, so it is the least reactive of these halogens. It would be displaced by all of chlorine, bromine and iodine from astatide solutions, and astatine would not displace any of them:

I₂(aq) + 2NaAt(aq) → 2NaI(aq) + At₂ At₂ + 2NaI(aq) → no reaction

Displacement as redox (HT)

Halogen displacement reactions are redox reactions. The more reactive halogen gains electrons (is reduced) to form halide ions; the halide ions of the less reactive halogen lose electrons (are oxidised) to form the halogen. The metal ions (K⁺, Na⁺) are spectator ions.

Ionic equation: Cl₂(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq) Reduction: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻   |   Oxidation: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻
(HT) Bromine water is added to sodium iodide solution. Write the ionic equation and half equations, and state which species is oxidised and which is reduced.

Full equation: Br₂(aq) + 2NaI(aq) → 2NaBr(aq) + I₂(aq)
Na⁺ ions are spectators. Ionic equation: Br₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)
Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻   (gain of electrons → bromine is reduced)
2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻   (loss of electrons → iodide ions are oxidised)
Be precise: it is the iodide ions that are oxidised, not "iodine". And bromine molecules are reduced to bromide ions. Remember OIL RIG, and check that electrons appear on opposite sides of the two half equations.

Explaining the reactivity trend in terms of electronic configuration

Halogen atoms have 7 outer electrons. When they react they gain one electron to complete their outer shell and form a 1− ion (e.g. Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻; for the molecule, Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻).

Going down the group:

  1. The atoms have more electron shells and are larger.
  2. The outer shell is further from the nucleus (and more shielded by inner electrons).
  3. So there is a weaker attraction between the nucleus and an incoming electron.
  4. It is harder to gain an electron, so the halogen is less reactive.
The halogen explanation is the mirror image of the Group 1 explanation. Group 1: reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is lost more easily. Group 7: reactivity decreases down the group because an electron is gained less easily. Make sure you say "gain" for halogens — writing "lose" is a common, costly slip.

On reactingLose 1 electron → 1+ ionGain 1 electron → 1− ion

6.5 Group 0 — The Noble Gases

Why the noble gases are inert

The noble gases are chemically inert (very unreactive) because their atoms have a full outer shell of electrons (2 for helium, 8 for the others). Their electronic configuration is already stable, so they have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons. As a result they exist as single atoms (monatomic gases) rather than molecules.

Patterns in physical properties

Going down Group 0:

  • Boiling point increases (the atoms get larger and the forces between atoms get stronger).
  • Density increases (the atoms get heavier).
  • All are colourless gases at room temperature.

The boiling points of argon and xenon are −186 °C and −108 °C. Predict the boiling point of krypton.

Krypton lies between argon and xenon, so its boiling point should be roughly halfway: (−186 + −108) ÷ 2 = about −147 °C (actual value −153 °C). Any answer between −186 and −108 °C that follows the trend is accepted.
Radon has a density of about 9 g dm⁻³ and air about 1.2 g dm⁻³. Explain why radon gas can build up in the basements of houses.

Radon is much denser than air (following the trend that density increases down Group 0), so it sinks and collects in low-lying spaces such as basements.

6.6 Using Group Trends to Make Predictions

Many Topic 6 questions give you data for some elements in a group and ask you to predict a property of another. Use these rules:

  • Identify the direction of the trend (increasing or decreasing down the group) from the data given.
  • Place the unknown element in the correct position and make sure your prediction continues the trend — for an element in the middle, give a value between its neighbours.
  • For chemical reactions, predict the same type of product as the other members (hydroxide + hydrogen for Group 1; metal halide or hydrogen halide for Group 7) and a faster or slower reaction according to the reactivity trend.
  • Give a reason that refers to the position in the group (“because it is lower down Group 1, so…”).

7Melting/boiling point, colourIncrease; darkerHigher melting point, more likely solid, darker colour
0Boiling point, densityIncreaseHigher boiling point and higher density
0ReactivityAll very unreactiveStill inert (full outer shell)
Francium is the element below caesium in Group 1. Predict (a) its electronic arrangement in terms of outer electrons, (b) how it would react with water, (c) the formula of its chloride.

(a) Like every Group 1 element it has one electron in its outer shell (with more shells than caesium).
(b) It would react extremely violently / explosively with water, forming francium hydroxide, FrOH (an alkaline solution), and hydrogen — because its outer electron is furthest from the nucleus and is lost most easily.
(c) It forms a 1+ ion, Fr⁺, and chloride is Cl⁻, so the chloride is FrCl.
Sodium reacts with chlorine to form sodium chloride. Explain, in terms of electrons, what happens to each atom.

Sodium (2.8.1) loses its one outer electron to form Na⁺ (2.8). Chlorine (2.8.7) gains that electron to form Cl⁻ (2.8.8). Both ions now have a full outer shell — the same stable electronic configuration as a noble gas (neon and argon respectively). The oppositely charged ions attract to form ionic sodium chloride: 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.

Key Points Summary

  • 6.1 — Classify alkali metals (Group 1), halogens (Group 7) and noble gases (Group 0) by their position and outer-shell electrons.
  • 6.2 — Alkali metals are soft with relatively low melting points.
  • 6.3 — Li, Na and K react with water: float, fizz, form metal hydroxide (alkaline) + hydrogen; K ignites with a lilac flame. 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂.
  • 6.4 — Reactivity increases down Group 1; predict that Rb and Cs react even more violently.
  • 6.5 — Explain: more shells → outer electron further from nucleus → weaker attraction → lost more easily.
  • 6.6, 6.7 — Cl₂ pale yellow-green gas; Br₂ red-brown liquid; I₂ grey-black solid. Down Group 7: darker colour, higher melting/boiling points; predict F and At.
  • 6.8 — Test for chlorine: damp blue litmus paper turns red then is bleached.
  • 6.9, 6.10 — Halogens + metals → metal halides; halogens + hydrogen → hydrogen halides, which dissolve in water to form acids; reactivity decreases down the group.
  • 6.11 — A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from halide solutions (Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂); predict that astatine is displaced by all of them.
  • 6.12 (HT) — Halogen displacement is redox: the halogen gains electrons (reduced), the halide ion loses electrons (oxidised); write ionic and half equations.
  • 6.13 — Explain: more shells → outer shell further from nucleus → weaker attraction for an extra electron → harder to gain → less reactive.
  • 6.14 — Noble gases are inert because they have full outer shells (stable electronic configuration).
  • 6.15 — Uses depend on inertness (argon in bulbs and welding), low density and non-flammability (helium balloons).
  • 6.16 — Boiling point and density increase down Group 0; use the trend to predict values for other noble gases.
