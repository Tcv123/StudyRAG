Topic 8 — Fuels and Earth Science

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 2

Most of the energy used for transport, heating and electricity still comes from burning fossil fuels made from crude oil and natural gas. This topic covers how crude oil is separated and processed into useful fuels, what happens when those fuels burn (and the pollutants formed), and then zooms out to the Earth's atmosphere: how it formed, how it changed, and how human activity is now changing the climate.

Topic 8 is common to GCSE Chemistry and Combined Science — there is no chemistry-only content in this topic, and no Higher-tier-only content. There is no core practical in Topic 8, but you should know the laboratory cracking experiment and the test for oxygen. The naming, formulae and reactions of individual alkanes and alkenes are covered in Topic 9 (chemistry-only).

8.1 Hydrocarbons and Crude Oil

Hydrocarbons are compounds that contain carbon and hydrogen atoms only.

Methane (CH₄), octane (C₈H₁₈) and ethene (C₂H₄) are hydrocarbons. Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) is not — it contains oxygen.

Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, containing molecules in which carbon atoms are joined in chains or rings.
  • Crude oil is an important source of useful substances: fuels (petrol, diesel, kerosene, etc.) and feedstock for the petrochemical industry (raw materials for making plastics, solvents, detergents, medicines, dyes and lubricants).
  • Crude oil is a finite resource: it was formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient marine organisms (mainly plankton) buried in mud, under heat and pressure, and is being used up far faster than it can form. It is non-renewable.
  • Because it is a mixture, the hydrocarbons in it are not chemically bonded to each other, so they can be separated by a physical process — fractional distillation.
You do not need to know the names, formulae or structures of specific ring molecules in crude oil — just that crude oil contains both chain and ring hydrocarbons.

Homologous series

A homologous series is a series of compounds which:
  • have the same general formula,
  • differ by CH₂ in molecular formulae from neighbouring compounds,
  • show a gradual variation in physical properties, as exemplified by their boiling points,
  • have similar chemical properties (because they have the same functional group).

The alkanes are the main homologous series in crude oil, general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

8.2 Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil

Crude oil is separated into simpler, more useful mixtures called fractions by fractional distillation. Each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with a similar number of carbon atoms, so they have similar boiling points.

How it works

  1. Crude oil is heated in a furnace until most of it evaporates to a vapour.
  2. The vapour enters near the bottom of a tall fractionating column that is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top (there is a temperature gradient).
  3. The vapours rise up the column and cool.
  4. Each hydrocarbon condenses when it reaches the level where the temperature is just below its boiling point; the liquid is collected on trays and piped off as a fraction.
  5. Hydrocarbons with high boiling points (large molecules) condense low down; hydrocarbons with low boiling points (small molecules) rise further and condense near the top. The smallest molecules stay as gases and leave at the top.
  6. The very largest molecules don't evaporate at all and are collected at the bottom as bitumen.
Use the words evaporate (or vaporise) and condense correctly. The column separates by boiling point, which depends on molecule size. You do not need to know the names of specific fractionating columns.

8.3 How the Properties of Fractions Differ

Fractions differ in their properties because their molecules contain different numbers of carbon and hydrogen atoms. The bigger the molecules:

PropertySmall molecules (e.g. gases, petrol)Large molecules (e.g. fuel oil, bitumen)Explanation
Boiling pointlowhighlarger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces between them, so more energy is needed to separate them
Ease of ignitioneasy to ignite (very flammable)hard to ignitesmaller molecules are more volatile — they evaporate easily to form a flammable vapour
Viscosity (how thick / how hard to pour)low — runnyhigh — thick, flows slowlylonger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces and can tangle, so they flow past each other less easily
It is the forces between molecules (intermolecular forces) that are overcome when hydrocarbons boil — not the covalent bonds inside the molecules. Saying "covalent bonds break when it boils" is a classic lost mark.

8.4 Combustion of Hydrocarbon Fuels

Complete combustion

Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon fuel happens when there is a plentiful supply of oxygen. The products are carbon dioxide and water, and energy is given out (exothermic).
hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

The carbon and hydrogen in the fuel are oxidised. Complete combustion gives a clean blue flame and releases the maximum amount of energy from the fuel.

Balance the equation for the complete combustion of octane, C₈H₁₈.
C₈H₁₈ + O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O (balance C then H). Oxygen atoms on the right = 16 + 9 = 25, so 12½ O₂.
Double everything to remove the half: 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O.

Incomplete combustion

If the supply of oxygen is limited, there is not enough oxygen to oxidise all the carbon to CO₂. Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide (CO) and/or carbon (soot), as well as water. Less energy is released.

2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O    (carbon monoxide formed) CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O    (carbon / soot formed)

Incomplete combustion often gives a smoky, yellow flame (glowing soot particles).

Why carbon monoxide is toxic

  • Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless gas, so it cannot be detected by our senses.
  • When breathed in, CO combines with haemoglobin in red blood cells (more strongly than oxygen does).
  • This reduces the capacity of the blood to carry oxygen around the body, leading to drowsiness, headaches, unconsciousness and eventually death.

Problems caused by incomplete combustion in appliances

  • Faulty or poorly ventilated gas fires, boilers and cookers can produce carbon monoxide — this is why homes should have CO detectors and appliances should be serviced regularly.
  • Soot (carbon particles) blackens appliances and buildings, can block flues and chimneys (so waste gases, including CO, cannot escape) and causes breathing problems if inhaled.
  • Less energy is released from the fuel, so incomplete combustion wastes fuel and money.
For "why does incomplete combustion produce CO?" the answer is: there is insufficient oxygen to oxidise all the carbon in the fuel fully to carbon dioxide. Don't confuse CO (carbon monoxide, toxic) with CO₂ (carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas).

8.5 Other Pollutants from Fuels

Sulfur dioxide and acid rain

Some hydrocarbon fuels (especially coal, diesel and fuel oil) contain sulfur compounds as impurities. When the fuel is burned, the sulfur is oxidised to sulfur dioxide:

S + O₂ → SO₂

Sulfur dioxide dissolves in rainwater to form an acidic solution — acid rain. (In the air it can be further oxidised, forming sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄.)

Problems caused by acid rain:

  • Damages buildings and statues made of limestone or marble (calcium carbonate reacts with acid) and corrodes metal structures.
  • Acidifies lakes and rivers, harming or killing fish and other aquatic life.
  • Damages trees and plants (leaves are damaged and nutrients are leached from soil).
  • SO₂ itself irritates the lungs and can trigger asthma.

Reducing SO₂: remove sulfur from fuels before they are sold (low-sulfur petrol and diesel), and "scrub" waste gases from power stations (e.g. with calcium oxide/carbonate).

Oxides of nitrogen

When fuels are burned in engines, the temperature is very high. At these high temperatures, nitrogen and oxygen from the air (not from the fuel!) combine to form oxides of nitrogen (NOₓ), such as nitrogen monoxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂):

N₂ + O₂ → 2NO     2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂
  • Nitrogen is normally very unreactive (strong N≡N triple bond), which is why the reaction only happens at the high temperatures inside an engine.
  • Oxides of nitrogen cause acid rain, photochemical smog, and breathing problems (they irritate the lungs and worsen asthma).
  • Catalytic converters on car exhausts convert NOₓ back to N₂ (and CO to CO₂).
The nitrogen in NOₓ comes from the air, not from the fuel. Hydrocarbon fuels don't contain nitrogen. Say: "the high temperature in the engine provides enough energy for nitrogen and oxygen from the air to react".

8.6 Hydrogen as an Alternative Fuel

Hydrogen can be used as a fuel for cars, either burned in a combustion engine or used in a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell to generate electricity for an electric motor (fuel cells are covered in Topic 5).

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

8.7 Fossil Fuels Are Non-Renewable

  • Petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are non-renewable fossil fuels obtained from crude oil.
  • Methane is a non-renewable fossil fuel found in natural gas.
  • Coal is also a fossil fuel. Fossil fuels formed over millions of years and are being used up much faster than they are replaced — they will eventually run out.
  • As supplies decrease, they become harder and more expensive to extract. Crude oil is also needed as a feedstock for plastics and other chemicals, which is another reason not to burn it all.

8.8 Cracking

Cracking involves the breaking down of larger, saturated hydrocarbon molecules (alkanes) into smaller, more useful ones, some of which are unsaturated (alkenes).

Cracking is a thermal decomposition reaction. It is carried out by passing the vapour of a large alkane over a hot catalyst (e.g. aluminium oxide or a zeolite) at a high temperature (several hundred °C).

C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄    (decane → octane + ethene) C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ + C₂H₄ C₁₄H₃₀ → C₆H₁₄ + 4C₂H₄
Complete the cracking equation: C₁₆H₃₄ → C₁₀H₂₂ + ______
Carbon: 16 − 10 = 6; hydrogen: 34 − 22 = 12. The missing product is C₆H₁₂ (an alkene, since it fits CₙH₂ₙ). Alternatively it could be written as 3C₂H₄.
Cracking equations must balance: the total C and H atoms on each side are equal. At least one product must be an alkene (because there are not enough H atoms for all the products to be alkanes). Alkanes fit CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, alkenes fit CₙH₂ₙ.

Why cracking is necessary

  • Fractional distillation produces more of the large-molecule fractions (e.g. fuel oil, diesel) than there is demand for, and not enough of the small-molecule fractions such as petrol, which are in high demand.
  • Cracking converts the surplus long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter-chain hydrocarbons that match demand — making the most of a finite resource.
  • Cracking also produces alkenes (e.g. ethene, propene), which are reactive and are used as feedstock to make polymers (plastics) and other chemicals, such as ethanol.

Cracking in the laboratory

  • The catalyst is heated strongly, then the paraffin is warmed so its vapour passes over the hot catalyst and cracks.
  • The gas collected (mainly ethene and other small alkenes) decolourises bromine water (orange → colourless), showing it is unsaturated.
  • Safety: remove the delivery tube from the water before stopping heating, to prevent suck-back of cold water into the hot tube (which could crack it). Discard the first tube of gas (it is mainly air).

8.9 The Earth's Early Atmosphere

Scientists' ideas about the early atmosphere are based on evidence such as the gases released by volcanoes today, the composition of the atmospheres of other planets (e.g. Mars and Venus, mainly CO₂), the chemistry of ancient rocks and fossils, and air trapped in ice cores — but the evidence is limited because it was billions of years ago, so the ideas are a theory that may change.

  • The gases produced by volcanic activity formed the Earth's early atmosphere.
  • The early atmosphere is thought to have contained:
    • little or no oxygen,
    • a large amount of carbon dioxide,
    • water vapour,
    • small amounts of other gases (e.g. nitrogen, ammonia, methane).
  • You must be able to interpret evidence relating to this — e.g. rocks containing iron(II) compounds (not iron(III) oxides) formed early in Earth's history suggest there was little oxygen; later "red beds" containing oxidised iron show oxygen had appeared.

How the atmosphere changed

  1. Formation of the oceans: as the Earth cooled, the water vapour condensed to form liquid water, which collected to form the oceans.
  2. CO₂ dissolved in the oceans: the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere decreased as it dissolved in the oceans as they formed. Some of this dissolved carbon dioxide was later locked up in carbonate rocks (e.g. limestone, formed from shells of marine organisms) and in fossil fuels.
  3. Photosynthesis added oxygen: the growth of primitive plants (algae, then plants) used carbon dioxide and released oxygen by photosynthesis. Consequently the amount of oxygen in the atmosphere gradually increased and CO₂ decreased further.
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂    (photosynthesis)

Composition of today's atmosphere

Dry air today is approximately 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon (and small amounts of other noble gases) and about 0.04% carbon dioxide, plus a variable amount of water vapour.

Test for oxygen

Test for oxygen: insert a glowing splint into the gas — oxygen relights the glowing splint.
Don't mix up the gas tests: oxygen relights a glowing splint; hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky/cloudy; chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper.

8.10 The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect keeps the Earth warm enough for life. Without any greenhouse gases the average surface temperature would be about −18 °C rather than about +15 °C.

  1. The Sun emits radiation, mostly short-wavelength (visible light and ultraviolet), which passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, warming it.
  2. The warm Earth radiates energy back out as longer-wavelength infrared radiation.
  3. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere — carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour — absorb some of this infrared radiation and then re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the Earth.
  4. This reduces the amount of heat lost to space, keeping the lower atmosphere and surface warmer.
Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation — they do not "reflect" heat, and the greenhouse effect is not caused by the hole in the ozone layer. Name the three greenhouse gases in the specification: carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour.

8.11 Human Activity and Climate Change

Human activities increase the amounts of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere:

Greenhouse gasHuman sources
Carbon dioxideburning fossil fuels (power stations, vehicles, heating); deforestation (fewer trees to absorb CO₂, and burning the wood releases CO₂); cement production
Methanecattle and other livestock (digestion), rice paddy fields, landfill sites (decomposing waste), leaks from gas extraction

An increase in greenhouse gases enhances the greenhouse effect, causing global warming — an increase in the Earth's average surface temperature — which leads to climate change.

Evaluating the evidence

You must be able to evaluate the evidence for human activity causing climate change, considering:

  • the correlation between the change in atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration, the consumption of fossil fuels and temperature change — since the Industrial Revolution all three have risen together;
  • the uncertainties caused by the location where measurements are taken (e.g. readings near cities or volcanoes are not representative; there were few measuring stations in the past, especially over oceans) and the historical accuracy of measurements (old thermometers were less accurate; pre-1950s CO₂ values come from indirect methods such as air bubbles in ice cores, and older temperatures from tree rings and other proxies).

A correlation (two things rising together) does not on its own prove that one causes the other. The case for human activity is strong because there is also a mechanism (CO₂ absorbs infrared), the vast majority of climate scientists agree after peer review, and computer climate models only match the observed warming when human emissions are included. In an evaluation, mention both the correlation and the uncertainties.

Potential effects of increased CO₂ and methane

  • Rising sea levels — melting ice caps/glaciers and thermal expansion of seawater → flooding of low-lying land and coastal erosion.
  • Changes in rainfall patterns and more frequent and severe extreme weather (storms, floods, droughts, heatwaves).
  • Changes to the distribution of species (wildlife habitats shift; some species may become extinct) and to where food crops can be grown — affecting food security.
  • Ocean acidification — more CO₂ dissolves in seawater, lowering its pH, harming coral reefs and shellfish.

Key Points Summary

  • Hydrocarbons contain carbon and hydrogen only. Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons (chains and rings); it is a finite source of fuels and petrochemical feedstock.
  • A homologous series has the same general formula, neighbours differ by CH₂, physical properties (e.g. boiling point) change gradually, and chemical properties are similar.
  • Fractional distillation: crude oil is vaporised; vapours rise up a column that is cooler at the top; fractions condense at different heights according to boiling point.
  • Fractions, top to bottom: gases (heating/cooking), petrol (cars), kerosene (aircraft), diesel oil (some cars, trains), fuel oil (ships, some power stations), bitumen (roads, roofs).
  • Larger molecules → higher boiling point, harder to ignite, more viscous (stronger intermolecular forces).
  • Complete combustion → CO₂ + H₂O + energy. Limited oxygen → incomplete combustion → CO and/or C (soot).
  • CO is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin and reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood. Faulty appliances produce CO and soot.
  • Sulfur impurities → SO₂ → acid rain (damages buildings, lakes, trees). Engine temperatures make N₂ and O₂ from air form NOₓ.
  • Hydrogen fuel: only water produced, more energy per kg; but storage, safety, infrastructure and how the hydrogen is made are problems.
  • Petrol, kerosene and diesel (from crude oil) and methane (natural gas) are non-renewable fossil fuels.
  • Cracking breaks large alkanes into smaller, more useful alkanes and alkenes, using heat and a catalyst; needed because supply of large fractions exceeds demand and alkenes are needed for polymers.
  • Early atmosphere (from volcanoes): little or no O₂, lots of CO₂, water vapour, small amounts of other gases. Water vapour condensed to form oceans; CO₂ dissolved in oceans; photosynthesis by primitive plants used CO₂ and released O₂.
  • Oxygen relights a glowing splint.
  • Greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, H₂O) absorb and re-emit infrared radiation from the Earth, keeping it warm.
  • Evaluate evidence for human-caused climate change: correlation of CO₂, fossil fuel use and temperature; uncertainties from measurement location and historical accuracy.
  • Effects: rising sea levels, extreme weather, changes to habitats and food production. Mitigation: renewables, CCS, efficiency, reforestation — consider scale, risk and environmental implications.
