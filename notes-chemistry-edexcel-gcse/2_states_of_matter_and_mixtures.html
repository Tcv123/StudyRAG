Topic 2 — States of Matter and Mixtures

Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Chemistry (1CH0) — Paper 1

This topic explains how the particle model accounts for solids, liquids and gases and the changes between them, and then uses those ideas to separate mixtures. Every separation technique works because the substances in a mixture differ in a physical property — particle size, solubility, boiling point, or how strongly they are attracted to a stationary phase. If you can name the property, you can choose the technique. The topic finishes with the treatment of water for drinking and for chemical analysis, and includes one core practical (inks: distillation and chromatography).

Content marked (HT) is Higher Tier only — this topic has no Higher-only points. Topic 2 is shared with GCSE Combined Science; there is no chemistry-only content in it.

2.1 The Three States of Matter

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions). The particle model represents each particle as a small solid sphere. The state of a substance depends on how the particles are arranged, how they move and how much energy they have — and that depends on the strength of the forces between them compared with the energy of the particles.

When describing a state, cover three things: arrangement (regular or random), closeness (touching or far apart) and movement (vibrate, slide, move quickly). A typical 3-mark question gives one mark for each.

Limitations of the simple particle model

  • Particles are shown as solid, inelastic spheres, but real particles are atoms, molecules or ions with internal structure and are not hard balls.
  • The model does not show the forces between particles or how strong they are.
  • Diagrams are 2D and not to scale — the spaces between gas particles are much larger than can be drawn.
  • It does not show that particles in a liquid and gas move at a range of speeds.

2.2 Changes of State

Changes of state are physical changes, not chemical changes: no new substances are formed and the change can be reversed. The particles themselves (and the mass) stay the same; only their arrangement, movement and energy change.

Explaining changes of state

ChangeWhat happens to the particles
Melting (solid → liquid)Heating gives the particles more kinetic energy; they vibrate more until they have enough energy to overcome some of the forces holding them in fixed positions. They can then move past each other.
Boiling (liquid → gas)Particles gain enough energy to overcome almost all the forces between them and escape, moving far apart. Boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point.
Evaporation (liquid → gas)Happens only at the surface of a liquid and at temperatures below the boiling point: the most energetic surface particles escape.
Condensing (gas → liquid)Cooling reduces the energy of the particles; they slow down and attractive forces pull them close together.
Freezing (liquid → solid)Particles lose energy and the forces between them hold them in fixed positions in a regular arrangement.
Sublimation (solid → gas)Particles at the surface gain enough energy to escape straight into the gas state (e.g. solid carbon dioxide, "dry ice"; iodine on warming).
The melting point is the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid (the same temperature as the freezing point for a pure substance).
The boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid boils, turning into a gas throughout the liquid.
The stronger the forces between particles, the more energy is needed to overcome them, and the higher the melting and boiling points.

Heating and cooling curves

When a pure solid is heated steadily, its temperature rises until it reaches the melting point. The temperature then stays constant while it melts, even though energy is still being supplied, because the energy is being used to overcome the forces between particles rather than increase their kinetic energy. The same happens at the boiling point.

A cooling curve is the reverse: the temperature stays constant at the boiling point while the gas condenses and at the melting point while the liquid freezes, because energy is released as forces form between particles.

Predicting the state of a substance

You can predict the physical state of a substance at a given temperature from its melting and boiling points:

  • Temperature below the melting point → solid
  • Temperature between the melting and boiling points → liquid
  • Temperature above the boiling point → gas

2.3 Pure Substances and Mixtures

In chemistry, a pure substance is a single element or a single compound, not mixed with any other substance.
A mixture contains two or more substances (elements and/or compounds) that are not chemically joined. The substances in a mixture keep their own properties and can be separated by physical methods.

In everyday language "pure" means natural or with nothing added — "pure orange juice" or "pure mineral water". Chemically, both are mixtures (orange juice contains water, sugars, acids; mineral water contains dissolved salts).

ElementCompoundMixture
Only one type of atomTwo or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportionsTwo or more substances not chemically combined; proportions can vary
Cannot be broken down chemicallyCan only be separated by chemical reactionsCan be separated by physical methods
e.g. O₂, Fe, Ce.g. H₂O, NaCl, CO₂e.g. air, sea water, ink, crude oil

Using melting point data to test purity

  • A pure substance melts (and boils) at a specific, sharp temperature. Pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at standard pressure.
  • An impure substance (a mixture) melts over a range of temperatures, and usually at a lower temperature than the pure substance. Impurities also usually raise the boiling point and make it boil over a range.
A sample of aspirin melts between 128 °C and 133 °C. Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. What can you conclude?
The sample melts below the true melting point and over a range (5 °C), so it is impure.

2.4 Separating Mixtures

Choose a method by asking: what physical property is different between the substances?

Mixture to separateProperty usedTechnique

Soluble solid from its solution (e.g. salt from salt water)Solvent evaporates, solute does notCrystallisation
Solvent from a solution (e.g. water from sea water)Very different boiling pointsSimple distillation
Two or more miscible liquids (e.g. ethanol and water; crude oil)Boiling points that are fairly closeFractional distillation
Several dissolved substances (e.g. dyes in ink)Different attraction to paper vs solventPaper chromatography

Filtration

The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel. The filter paper has tiny holes: liquid and dissolved substances (small particles) pass through as the filtrate, while the insoluble solid (larger particles) stays behind as the residue.

Crystallisation

  1. Pour the solution into an evaporating basin.
  2. Heat gently (ideally over a water bath or with a low flame) to evaporate some of the solvent and make the solution more concentrated.
  3. Stop heating when the solution is saturated — test by dipping in a cold glass rod: crystals forming on the rod show the point of crystallisation has been reached.
  4. Leave the solution to cool slowly; crystals form as the solubility falls.
  5. Filter off the crystals, wash with a little cold distilled water, and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven.
Crystallisation is preferred to evaporating to dryness because (1) larger, purer crystals form, (2) soluble impurities stay in solution, and (3) hydrated salts (such as copper sulfate crystals) keep their water of crystallisation instead of decomposing to a powder.

Simple distillation

Simple distillation separates a solvent from a solution (e.g. pure water from salty water or ink), or a liquid from substances with a much higher boiling point.

  1. Heat the solution in a flask. The solvent (lower boiling point) boils and turns into vapour; the dissolved solid stays behind.
  2. The vapour passes into a Liebig condenser, cooled by cold water flowing in at the bottom and out at the top (so the jacket stays full and cooling is most efficient).
  3. The vapour condenses back to a liquid and drips into the collecting vessel as the distillate.
  4. The thermometer bulb is level with the side-arm so it measures the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask — this should stay at the boiling point of the pure solvent (100 °C for water).
Safety points: add anti-bumping granules to give smooth boiling; never heat a closed system (the apparatus must be open at the collecting end); do not let the flask boil dry. If the solvent is flammable, heat with an electric heater or water bath instead of a Bunsen burner.

Fractional distillation

Fractional distillation separates a mixture of miscible liquids with different boiling points, such as ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C), or the fractions of crude oil. A fractionating column filled with glass beads (or a ridged column) is placed between the flask and the condenser.

  • The mixture is heated. Both liquids evaporate, but the one with the lower boiling point evaporates more.
  • The column has a temperature gradient: hottest at the bottom, coolest at the top. Vapours condense on the glass beads and re-evaporate many times; at each stage the vapour becomes richer in the more volatile liquid.
  • The liquid with the higher boiling point condenses and runs back down into the flask. The liquid with the lower boiling point reaches the top and passes into the condenser.
  • The thermometer reading stays at the boiling point of the first liquid (78 °C for ethanol) while it distils. When the temperature rises, change the collecting vessel to collect the next fraction.

Paper chromatography

Paper chromatography separates a mixture of soluble substances (such as the dyes in an ink or food colouring).

Chromatography involves two phases:
The stationary phase does not move — in paper chromatography it is the paper (strictly, the water held in the paper's fibres).
The mobile phase moves through the stationary phase — it is the solvent (e.g. water or ethanol).
Each substance is distributed between the two phases. A substance that is more soluble in the solvent (more attracted to the mobile phase) spends more time in it and travels further up the paper. A substance more strongly attracted to the paper travels a shorter distance.

Rf values

Rf = distance travelled by the substance (spot) ÷ distance travelled by the solvent (solvent front)
  • Both distances are measured from the baseline; measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Rf is always less than 1 and has no units.
  • For a given solvent, paper and temperature, each substance has a characteristic Rf value, so Rf values can be compared with those of known substances (or a database) to identify unknowns.
On a chromatogram the solvent front is 8.0 cm from the baseline. A red spot is 5.2 cm from the baseline. Calculate its Rf.
Rf = 5.2 ÷ 8.0 = 0.65

Interpreting chromatograms

  • A pure substance produces one spot (in every solvent tried). A mixture produces two or more spots — although a single spot in one solvent does not prove purity, as two substances might have the same Rf in that solvent.
  • An unknown substance can be identified if its spot is at the same height (same Rf) as a known reference substance run on the same paper.
  • A spot that stays on the baseline is insoluble in that solvent — try a different solvent.
Why is the baseline drawn in pencil, not ink? Graphite is insoluble and will not move with the solvent; ink would dissolve and separate, confusing the results. Why must the baseline be above the solvent level? Otherwise the samples dissolve straight into the solvent in the beaker instead of travelling up the paper.

2.5 Core Practical: Investigating Inks Using Distillation and Chromatography

This core practical separates an ink into its solvent and its dyes. You need to know the method, the reasons for each step, and how to analyse the results.

Part 1 — Simple distillation of ink

  1. Place about 25 cm³ of ink in a round-bottomed (or conical) flask with a few anti-bumping granules. Set up the condenser and collecting tube as in the distillation diagram.
  2. Heat gently. Record the temperature on the thermometer when liquid starts to drip from the condenser — this is the boiling point of the solvent.
  3. Collect a few cm³ of colourless distillate, then stop heating before the flask boils dry.
  4. The distillate is the solvent (often water, boiling at about 100 °C). The dyes, which have much higher boiling points, are left in the flask.

Part 2 — Paper chromatography of ink

  1. Draw a pencil baseline about 1–2 cm from the bottom of a strip of chromatography paper.
  2. Use a capillary tube to put a small, concentrated spot of the ink on the baseline (and spots of reference dyes alongside, if available). Let each spot dry and re-spot to concentrate it.
  3. Hang the paper in a beaker containing solvent to a depth below the baseline. Cover the beaker with a lid (so the atmosphere is saturated with solvent vapour and the solvent does not evaporate from the paper).
  4. When the solvent has risen to near the top, remove the paper and immediately mark the solvent front in pencil. Allow to dry.
  5. Measure the distance from the baseline to the centre of each spot and to the solvent front; calculate the Rf value of each dye.

2.6 Water Treatment

Making water safe to drink (potable water)

Potable water is water that is safe to drink. It is not pure in the chemical sense: it contains low levels of dissolved salts, but harmful microbes and solid particles have been removed. In the UK, most drinking water comes from fresh water sources (rivers, lakes, reservoirs and groundwater); waste water can also be treated and reused. Ground water and waste water are made potable in three main stages:

StageWhat it removes and how
SedimentationWater is left to stand in large tanks; large insoluble particles (soil, grit) sink to the bottom under gravity. Chemicals may be added to make small particles clump together (flocculation) so they settle faster.
FiltrationWater passes through filter beds of sand and gravel, which trap the smaller insoluble particles that did not settle.
ChlorinationChlorine is added to kill harmful microorganisms (bacteria) that could cause disease. This is a chemical, not physical, step.

Water from the sea

Where fresh water is scarce, drinking water can be made from sea water by distillation (desalination). The water is boiled and the steam condensed, leaving the salts behind. This needs a very large amount of energy to boil the water, so it is expensive and used mainly in countries with little fresh water and plentiful cheap energy.

Water for chemical analysis

Tap water still contains dissolved salts (ions such as Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻) and traces of chlorine. Water used in chemical analysis must not contain dissolved salts, because these ions could react with the substances being tested or give false positive results (for example, chloride ions in tap water would give a white precipitate with silver nitrate in a test for chloride). For this reason deionised or distilled water is used in analysis, made by distilling tap water.

"Explain why distilled water rather than tap water is used to make solutions for analysis." Two marks: tap water contains dissolved ions/salts (1) which would react with the reagents or contaminate the sample, giving inaccurate results (1).

2.7 Choosing a Separation Method — Worked Problems

How would you obtain pure, dry sand and pure salt crystals from a mixture of sand and salt?
1. Add water and stir (warm gently) — salt dissolves, sand does not.
2. Filter — sand is the residue; wash it with distilled water and dry it in a warm oven.
3. Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until crystals start to form on a cold glass rod, then leave it to cool (crystallisation). Filter off and dry the salt crystals.
Propanone boils at 56 °C and water at 100 °C. They mix completely. Which method separates them, and which liquid is collected first?
They are miscible liquids with different boiling points → fractional distillation. Propanone, with the lower boiling point, is collected first (thermometer reads 56 °C).
A green food colouring gives a single spot in water but two spots (yellow and blue) when ethanol is the solvent. What does this show?
The colouring is a mixture of at least two dyes. In water they have the same Rf so they did not separate; ethanol separates them because the dyes differ in their solubility in ethanol.

Key Points Summary

  • Solids: regular, touching, vibrate. Liquids: random, touching, move past each other. Gases: random, far apart, fast random movement.
  • Melting, boiling and sublimation need energy to overcome forces between particles; freezing and condensing release energy.
  • Changes of state are physical changes: no new substance forms and mass is conserved.
  • Temperature stays constant during a change of state of a pure substance (flat sections on heating/cooling curves).
  • Below mp = solid; between mp and bp = liquid; above bp = gas.
  • Chemically "pure" = a single element or compound; everyday "pure" means nothing added.
  • Pure substances melt at a sharp, fixed temperature; mixtures melt over a range, usually lower.
  • Filtration: insoluble solid from liquid. Crystallisation: soluble solid from solution. Simple distillation: solvent from solution. Fractional distillation: miscible liquids with different boiling points. Paper chromatography: soluble substances such as dyes.
  • Chromatography: stationary phase = paper; mobile phase = solvent. Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent; pure substance = one spot.
  • Core practical: distil ink to collect its solvent (record boiling point); use chromatography (pencil baseline above solvent, lid on) to separate and identify the dyes.
  • Drinking water: sedimentation → filtration → chlorination. Sea water can be distilled, but it uses a lot of energy.
  • Water for chemical analysis must be distilled/deionised because dissolved ions in tap water would interfere with tests.
