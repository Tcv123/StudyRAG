1.7 Sustaining Ecosystems: Polar Environments

1. Where Are Cold Environments Found?

Cold environments — areas where temperatures are very low for most of the year, so ice, snow and frozen ground dominate. Geographers divide them into polar environments (Antarctica, the Arctic Ocean ice and Greenland ice sheet) and tundra environments (the treeless lands of northern Canada, Alaska, northern Russia and Scandinavia that fringe the Arctic). Some high mountain areas (alpine) are also cold environments.

2. Physical Characteristics of a Cold Environment

Climate

  • High latitude means the sun's rays strike the ground at a low angle and pass through more atmosphere, so they are spread over a larger area and heating is weak.
  • Snow and ice have a high albedo — they reflect up to 80–90% of incoming sunlight, keeping temperatures low.
  • Cold, sinking air creates high pressure, so precipitation is low. Strong katabatic winds blow off the ice sheets.
  • Extreme seasonal variation in daylight — the "midnight sun" in summer and 24-hour darkness in winter.

Permafrost and soils

Permafrost — ground that remains frozen (at or below 0°C) for at least two consecutive years. In the tundra it can be hundreds of metres deep. Above it is the active layer, which thaws in summer.
  • Because meltwater cannot drain through the frozen ground below, the active layer becomes waterlogged in summer, creating bogs, marshes and shallow lakes.
  • Soils are thin, acidic and infertile. Low temperatures mean decomposition is extremely slow, so dead plant material builds up as peat — a huge store of carbon.

3. Interdependence in Cold Environments

  • Climate → plants: the short growing season (6–10 weeks) and low temperatures limit plants to small, slow-growing species.
  • Permafrost → plants: roots cannot penetrate the frozen ground, so trees cannot grow; waterlogging favours mosses and sedges.
  • Plants → animals: lichens and mosses are the main food for caribou (reindeer) and lemmings; lemmings are prey for Arctic foxes and snowy owls.
  • Plants → permafrost: the vegetation and peat layer insulate the ground, protecting the permafrost from summer heat. If vegetation is removed (by vehicles or buildings), the permafrost thaws, the ground subsides and becomes boggy.
  • Animals → people: Indigenous peoples such as the Inuit and Iñupiat have traditionally depended on caribou, seals, whales and fish for food, clothing and tools.

5. Biodiversity

  • Cold environments have low biodiversity — only around 1,700 plant species live in the Arctic tundra, compared with tens of thousands in the Amazon.
  • Food webs are simple, with few species at each level. A change to one species (e.g. a crash in lemming numbers) can have major knock-on effects — the ecosystem is fragile.
  • Issues: climate change (the Arctic is warming nearly four times faster than the global average), loss of sea ice for polar bears, oil spills, and the spread of species from further south.

6. Case Study: Alaska — Opportunities and Challenges

Alaska, USA

Alaska is the largest US state (about 1.7 million km²), lying mainly between 55°N and 71°N. Its north is Arctic tundra underlain by permafrost; the population is only about 740,000, mostly in the south around Anchorage. About 15% of residents are Alaska Native peoples.

Development opportunities

  • Oil and gas: the Prudhoe Bay oilfield on the North Slope, discovered in 1968, was the largest in North America. Oil revenue funds most of the state's budget and the Alaska Permanent Fund, which pays every resident an annual dividend (typically $1,000–$2,000).
  • Mineral extraction: gold, zinc and lead — the Red Dog Mine is one of the world's largest zinc mines.
  • Fishing: Alaska lands around 60% of all US seafood (salmon, pollock, crab), employing tens of thousands of people.
  • Tourism: over 2 million visitors a year come for wilderness, glaciers, whale-watching and the Northern Lights — cruise ships along the coast and national parks such as Denali.

Development challenges

  • Extreme temperatures: winter temperatures below −40°C make outdoor work dangerous, freeze machinery and require heated buildings, adding cost.
  • Inaccessibility: vast distances, mountains and few roads. Many communities can only be reached by air or by sea; ice roads are used in winter.
  • Buildings and infrastructure: heat from buildings and pipes can thaw permafrost, making the ground subside and buildings crack. Buildings are raised on stilts or piles, and the Trans-Alaska Pipeline (1,287 km, from Prudhoe Bay to the ice-free port of Valdez) is raised on supports for about half its length, with heat pipes to keep the ground frozen, and built in a zigzag to cope with ground movement and earthquakes.
  • Environmental risk: the Exxon Valdez oil spill (1989) released around 41 million litres of oil into Prince William Sound, killing hundreds of thousands of seabirds and thousands of sea otters.

7. The Value of Cold Environments as Wilderness Areas

Wilderness — a natural area largely undisturbed by human activity, with few or no permanent inhabitants, roads or buildings.
  • Scientific research: ice cores from Antarctica and Greenland hold a record of past climates going back 800,000 years; cold environments are an early warning of climate change.
  • Biodiversity: unique species found nowhere else (polar bears, emperor penguins, musk oxen).
  • Global climate regulation: high-albedo ice reflects sunlight; permafrost stores around 1,500 billion tonnes of carbon — roughly twice as much as the atmosphere.
  • Indigenous cultures and traditional ways of life.
  • Unspoilt landscapes valued for their beauty, recreation and wellbeing.

Why are cold environments fragile?

  • Plants grow extremely slowly, so damaged vegetation can take decades to recover — vehicle tracks on tundra can remain visible for 50 years or more.
  • Low temperatures mean oil and waste break down very slowly.
  • Simple food webs mean the loss of one species affects many others.
  • Thawing permafrost releases methane and CO₂, causing further warming (a positive feedback).

9. Antarctica — Characteristics and Threats

  • Location and size: a continent of about 14 million km² centred on the South Pole, almost entirely covered by an ice sheet averaging over 2 km thick — around 70% of the world's fresh water.
  • Climate: the coldest, driest and windiest continent; the interior is a polar desert with under 50 mm of precipitation a year. The lowest temperature recorded on Earth (−89.2°C) was at Vostok station.
  • Wildlife: almost all life is found on the coasts and in the Southern Ocean — penguins, seals, whales, seabirds and krill, which is the base of the marine food web.
  • People: no permanent population; around 1,000–5,000 scientists and support staff at research stations depending on the season.

How people use Antarctica

  • Scientific research — ice cores, climate monitoring, ozone layer studies (the ozone hole was discovered by the British Antarctic Survey in 1985).
  • Tourism — over 100,000 visitors a season, mostly on cruise ships to the Antarctic Peninsula.
  • Fishing — krill and toothfish in the Southern Ocean.

Threats

  • Climate change: the Antarctic Peninsula is one of the fastest-warming places on Earth; ice shelves such as Larsen B (2002) have collapsed; sea ice extent hit record lows in 2023.
  • Tourism: risk of oil spills from ships, disturbance to wildlife, trampling and introduction of non-native species.
  • Overfishing: of krill and toothfish, affecting whales, seals and penguins.
  • Possible future resource exploitation — minerals and oil, if the mining ban were ever lifted.
