2.2 Dynamic Development

1. Defining Development

Development refers to improvements in the standard of living and quality of life for people within a country. It is measured across two broad dimensions:

  • Economic development — growth in wealth, industrialisation, trade, and GDP per capita.
  • Human development — improvements in quality of life: health, education, wellbeing, and political freedom.

Development is best understood as a continuum — a sliding scale from the least to most developed — rather than a binary division of "developed" and "developing" worlds. Countries sit at different points on this continuum and move along it over time.

Classification of Countries

ACs (High Income Countries) — GNI per capita above ~$13,900 (World Bank threshold, 2025). Examples: USA, UK, Germany, Japan, Australia.
LIDCs (Low Income Countries) — GNI per capita below ~$1,135 (2025). Examples: Niger, South Sudan, DR Congo, Afghanistan.
EDCs (Newly Emerging Economies) — Countries rapidly industrialising and growing, often moving from LIDC status. Examples: China, India, Brazil, Nigeria, Mexico, Indonesia, Turkey, South Africa.
Exam Tip: Avoid using the terms "first world / third world" or "developed / developing" in exam answers — they imply a binary and are outdated. Use AC, LIDC, EDC, or "more/less economically developed country" (MEDC/LEDC). Examiners reward precise terminology.

5. Patterns of Development

Wealth and development are unevenly distributed globally. A broad pattern is visible in the North-South divide, summarised by the Brandt Line.

Key patterns of global wealth distribution:

  • ACs are concentrated in North America, Western Europe, Japan, and Australia/NZ.
  • LIDCs are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South/Southeast Asia.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest concentration of the world's LIDCs.
  • EDCs — the BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) and MINT (Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey) — are rapidly changing the global economic landscape.
  • The Brandt Line is a simplification: Australia and New Zealand are geographically south but economically "north".
Exam Tip: The Brandt Line is a useful concept but always note its limitations — it oversimplifies a complex spectrum. Australia and NZ are exceptions. Many EDCs are in the "south" but are growing rapidly.

7. Theories of Development

Rostow's modernisation theory (1960)

W. W. Rostow argued that all countries pass through five stages of economic growth:

  1. Traditional society — mostly subsistence farming, little technology.
  2. Pre-conditions for take-off — investment in infrastructure, education and commercial farming; some industry.
  3. Take-off — rapid growth of manufacturing industries; investment from abroad.
  4. Drive to maturity — economy diversifies; technology spreads; living standards rise.
  5. High mass consumption — wealthy society with a large service sector and high consumer spending.

Criticisms: based on the experience of Europe and the USA; ignores colonialism and the unequal world trading system; assumes development is only economic growth.

Frank's dependency theory (1960s)

  • André Gunder Frank argued that the world is divided into a wealthy core (developed countries) and a poor periphery (developing countries).
  • The core keeps the periphery poor by buying its raw materials cheaply and selling manufactured goods back at high prices, and through debt and aid with conditions. Development in the core depends on underdevelopment in the periphery.
  • Criticisms: it cannot explain how countries such as South Korea, Singapore and China have grown rapidly within the global economy.
Exam Tip: Compare the theories: Rostow is optimistic (all countries can develop by following the same path); Frank is pessimistic (the global system prevents development). India and China support parts of both.

8. Causes of Uneven Development

Uneven development has multiple, interrelated causes — both physical and human.

Physical Causes

  • Landlocked countries — no access to sea trade routes, making trade expensive (Mali, Chad, Zambia). 16 of the world's 32 landlocked developing countries are in Africa.
  • Tropical climate — high temperatures and humidity increase prevalence of diseases (malaria, dengue) which reduce workforce productivity. Seasonal droughts reduce agricultural output.
  • Natural hazards — frequent earthquakes, floods, cyclones destroy infrastructure and divert development funds to disaster relief (Haiti, Bangladesh).
  • Lack of natural resources — limited mineral or energy wealth restricts income generation. However, the "resource curse" shows that over-reliance on a single resource (e.g. oil in Nigeria) can actually worsen inequality if wealth is poorly distributed.

Human Causes

  • Colonialism — European powers extracted resources, cash crops, and labour from colonies for centuries. Infrastructure built to serve colonial export needs, not local development. Borders drawn arbitrarily, ignoring ethnic groups, causing post-independence conflict.
  • Trade inequality — "Terms of trade" work against LIDCs. LIDCs export primary commodities (cocoa, coffee, copper) at volatile, low prices; import manufactured goods at higher prices. "Commodity trap" — prices set by global markets, not producers.
  • Debt — Many LIDCs borrowed heavily in the 1970s-80s for development; interest repayments now exceed incoming aid. World Bank and IMF Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) required cuts to health/education spending — worsening development.
  • Political instability — corruption diverts funds; civil war destroys infrastructure and displaces populations. South Sudan — world's youngest nation — suffered civil war from 2013 to 2018 and continuing instability since, preventing development. Conflict deters foreign investment.
  • Poor governance — weak institutions, lack of rule of law, and corruption reduce effectiveness of development spending.
Exam Tip: "Explain why development is uneven" questions require you to state, explain, and link multiple causes. Don't just list them — explain the mechanism (e.g. "landlocked countries face higher transport costs, which makes imports more expensive and reduces trade, slowing economic growth").

10. Globalisation and Development

Globalisation — the increasing interconnectedness of the world through trade, investment, migration, and communications.

Transnational Corporations (TNCs)

TNCs (Transnational Corporations) — companies that operate in multiple countries. Examples: Nike, Apple, Shell, Unilever, Samsung. They locate headquarters in ACs but manufacturing in LIDCs and EDCs to exploit lower labour costs, tax advantages, and weaker regulations.
Positive impacts on LIDCs and EDCsNegative impacts on LIDCs and EDCs
Creates employment (often thousands of jobs)Low wages — often close to minimum; poor working conditions
Technology transfer to local workers and firmsProfits repatriated back to AC headquarters — "leakage"
Brings Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)Environmental damage — weaker regulations exploited
Develops infrastructure (roads, ports)Worker exploitation — long hours, unsafe conditions (Rana Plaza, Bangladesh 2013)
Increases exports and government tax revenueUndermines local industries through competition

The Global Shift

Since the 1970s–80s, manufacturing has shifted from ACs (USA, UK, Germany) to EDCs and LIDCs (China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Mexico). This "global shift" has brought rapid industrialisation to Asia in particular, reducing absolute poverty for hundreds of millions of people. However, it has also led to deindustrialisation and job losses in traditional manufacturing regions of ACs (e.g. Rust Belt, USA; steel towns in Wales/NE England).

Exam Tip: For TNC evaluation questions, structure your answer around: economic impacts (jobs, FDI, tax), social impacts (working conditions, urbanisation), and environmental impacts. Use a named TNC with specific details — Nike in Vietnam, or Apple's Foxconn factories in China — to access top marks.

11. Trade and Development

International trade is a major driver of development — but access to trade and its terms are unequal.

Fairtrade

Fairtrade — a trading partnership that ensures producers in LIDCs receive a guaranteed minimum price for their goods, plus a "Fairtrade Premium" (extra money for community projects). Managed by the Fairtrade Foundation. Nearly 2 million farmers and workers in around 70 countries. Products include cocoa, coffee, bananas, cotton, gold.
  • Benefits: stable income, social premium for schools/wells/healthcare, empowers smallholder farmers, encourages environmental standards.
  • Criticisms: only a small fraction of the Fairtrade price reaches farmers; consumers pay more but benefit may be limited; not all large-scale producers can access Fairtrade certification.

Trade Blocs and the WTO

  • Trade blocs (e.g. EU single market, NAFTA/USMCA) reduce tariffs between member countries — benefitting members but excluding LIDCs.
  • WTO (World Trade Organisation) — sets global trade rules. Accused of favouring AC agendas; agricultural subsidies in the EU/USA undercut LIDC farmers.

Commodity Dependence

  • Ghana — heavily dependent on cocoa and gold exports. When cocoa prices fall, government revenue falls sharply.
  • Nigeria — oil accounts for ~90% of export revenue and ~70% of government income. The "Dutch Disease": oil wealth causes the currency to rise, making other exports uncompetitive and damaging manufacturing.

13. Other Strategies to Reduce Inequality

Microfinance / Microcredit

Microfinance — small loans (micro-loans) given to individuals (often women) in LIDCs to start or grow small businesses, without requiring collateral. Developed by the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh (founded 1983 by Muhammad Yunus). Over 9 million borrowers, 97% women. Average loan ~$200. Success rate high: ~98% repayment.

Benefits: empowers women, breaks poverty cycle, stimulates local economy, does not create national debt.
Criticisms: high interest rates in some schemes, small scale — cannot address systemic inequality.

Debt Relief

The HIPC (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries) initiative — IMF/World Bank programme that cancelled around $76 billion of debt for over 35 qualifying countries. Freed up government funds for health and education spending (Mozambique, Uganda, Bolivia). Conditions applied — some argue these conditions still favour creditor countries.

Intermediate/Appropriate Technology

Appropriate technology — technology that is suited to the local environment, skills, culture, and economic circumstances of the community. Often simple, low-cost, repairable locally. Championed by NGO Practical Action (formerly ITDG).
  • Wind pumps for irrigation (Kenya) — no fuel needed, maintained locally.
  • Solar cookers (India) — reduce firewood use, improve health (less indoor smoke).
  • Biosand filters (Cambodia) — clean water at low cost.
  • Treadle pumps (Bangladesh) — foot-powered irrigation for small farmers.

Tourism

Tourism brings foreign exchange to LIDCs and EDCs. Kenya — safari and beach tourism earns billions of dollars a year, creating jobs in rural areas. Maldives — ~28% of GDP from tourism. However: leakage (profits leaving to TNC-owned hotels in ACs), environmental damage (reef degradation), seasonality of income, vulnerability to global events (COVID-19 devastated Maldives tourism).

Export Processing Zones (EPZs)

EPZs are designated areas where companies pay reduced taxes and have relaxed regulations to attract foreign investment and manufacturing. China used EPZs (Shenzhen Special Economic Zone from 1980) as a key driver of rapid industrialisation and export growth.

15. Case Study: Ethiopia — A Low-Income Developing Country

Ethiopia — location and context
  • A landlocked country in the Horn of Africa, bordered by Eritrea, Djibouti, Somalia, Kenya, South Sudan and Sudan. Its capital is Addis Ababa, headquarters of the African Union.
  • Population about 130 million — the second largest in Africa — and growing rapidly.
  • Ethiopia was never colonised (apart from a brief Italian occupation, 1936–41), but suffered severe famines in 1984–85 and civil conflict, including the Tigray war (2020–22).
  • The highlands are cooler and wetter; lowlands in the east and south are hot and prone to drought.

Development indicators

Progress and obstacles

  • Progress: for over a decade (2004–2019) Ethiopia had one of the world's fastest-growing economies (around 10% a year); poverty fell, life expectancy rose by over 10 years, and child mortality fell by more than half since 2000.
  • Obstacles: dependence on rain-fed farming makes it vulnerable to drought (2015–16 drought left over 10 million needing food aid); landlocked position raises trade costs; conflict; high debt; rapid population growth.

The role of TNCs

  • The government has built industrial parks such as Hawassa Industrial Park to attract clothing and textile TNCs (suppliers to brands such as PVH — owner of Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger).
  • Chinese firms such as Huajian (shoes) have opened factories, attracted by low labour costs.
  • Benefits: tens of thousands of jobs, many for young women; export earnings; skills and infrastructure.
  • Problems: very low wages (among the lowest in the global garment industry), high staff turnover, profits leaving the country, and vulnerability to trade decisions (the USA suspended Ethiopia's duty-free access in 2022).

Aid and top-down vs bottom-up projects

  • Ethiopia is one of the largest recipients of international aid in Africa — from the USA, the World Bank, the UK and others.
  • Top-down — Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) on the Blue Nile: Africa's largest hydroelectric project (over 5,000 MW), largely funded by Ethiopia itself through bonds. It could double the country's electricity supply and allow power exports, but has caused tension with Egypt and Sudan over Nile water.
  • Bottom-up — Productive Safety Net Programme (PSNP): pays around 8 million vulnerable people in cash or food in return for work on community projects such as terracing, tree planting and water harvesting, reducing hunger and land degradation.
  • Bottom-up — WaterAid and local NGOs: hand pumps, springs and sanitation in rural villages.
Exam Tip: OCR asks how an LIDC is linked to the wider world — through trade, aid, TNCs and international relations. Ethiopia's links with China (investment, the Addis Ababa–Djibouti railway) are a strong example.
