C2.1 Purity and Separating Mixtures

What Does "Pure" Mean?

In chemistry, a pure substance is a single element or a single compound, not mixed with any other substance. Elements and compounds are pure; mixtures are impure.

In everyday language "pure" means natural, clean or with nothing added (e.g. "pure orange juice", "pure mountain spring water"). Scientifically these are mixtures: orange juice contains water, sugars, acids and many other compounds; spring water contains dissolved minerals and gases. A solution is never pure — it is a mixture of solute and solvent.

Everyday use of "pure"Scientific meaning
Nothing added; natural; unprocessed; "100% pure honey"Contains only one substance (one element or one compound)
"Pure water" from a springNot pure — contains dissolved salts and gases. Only distilled water is (close to) pure H₂O.

Using Melting Point Data to Test Purity

Every pure substance has a fixed, sharp melting point (and boiling point) at a given pressure. Impurities change this:

  • A pure substance melts at a single, exact temperature that matches the data-book value.
  • An impure substance melts over a range of temperatures, and usually below the pure melting point (impurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point).
Data book: pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. Sample A melts sharply at 136 °C; sample B melts between 128 °C and 133 °C. Which is pure?
Sample A is pure — it melts at a sharp, fixed temperature equal to the data value. Sample B is impure: it melts over a 5 °C range and below 136 °C.

Measuring melting point: a little powdered solid is packed into a thin glass capillary tube and heated slowly in an electric melting-point apparatus (or attached to a thermometer in an oil bath). The temperature is read when the solid first starts to melt and when it has all melted.

Relative Atomic, Molecular and Formula Mass

Relative atomic mass (Ar) — the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element, taking into account its naturally occurring isotopes, compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12. (Given on the Periodic Table.)

Relative molecular mass (Mr) — the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule (covalent substances, e.g. H₂O, CO₂).

Relative formula mass (Mr) — the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula of a compound (used for ionic compounds and giant structures, e.g. NaCl, CaCO₃). Both have no units.
Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Calculate its Ar.
Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5.
Calculate the relative formula mass of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂. (Ar: Ca = 40, O = 16, H = 1)
The bracket doubles everything inside it: Ca + 2 × (O + H) = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74.

In a balanced equation the total Mr of the reactants equals the total Mr of the products (conservation of mass). Multiply each Mr by the big number in front of the formula:

Show that mass is conserved in 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. (Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16)
Reactants: 2 × 24 + (2 × 16) = 48 + 32 = 80. Products: 2 × (24 + 16) = 2 × 40 = 80. 80 = 80, so mass is conserved.

To find an empirical formula from a model: count the atoms of each element, write the ratio, then divide by the highest common factor. Going the other way, if you know the empirical formula and the Mr, the molecular formula is a whole-number multiple: e.g. empirical formula CH₂ with Mr = 56 → (CH₂ = 14) → 56 ÷ 14 = 4 → C₄H₈. For giant structures (ionic lattices, diamond, SiO₂) only the empirical formula can be given, because there are no separate molecules.

Formulations

A formulation is a mixture designed as a useful product. Each component is added in a carefully measured quantity for a particular purpose.

In an alloy (e.g. steel = iron + carbon; brass = copper + zinc), atoms of different sizes distort the regular layers of the metal, so the layers cannot slide over each other as easily — the alloy is harder and stronger than the pure metal. Other examples: paints, medicines (tablets with the active drug plus fillers, binders and coatings), fertilisers, cleaning products, cosmetics, fuels and foods. Many useful materials are formulations rather than pure substances, because the mixture has better properties than any single component.

Separation Techniques

Mixtures can be separated by physical methods because the substances in them are not chemically bonded. The method depends on the property that differs between the components.

Filtration and Crystallisation

  • Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid (e.g. sand from salty water). The liquid passes through the tiny holes in the filter paper (the filtrate); the solid particles are too large and stay behind (the residue).
  • Crystallisation obtains a soluble solid from a solution (e.g. copper sulfate crystals). Heat the solution in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the solvent until the solution is saturated (crystals start to form on a glass rod dipped in and removed). Leave it to cool slowly; crystals form as the solubility falls. Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven.
  • Evaporation to dryness is quicker but gives small crystals and can decompose some salts (e.g. hydrated salts lose their water of crystallisation) — crystallisation gives larger, purer crystals.

Simple Distillation

Simple distillation separates a liquid (solvent) from a solution, e.g. pure water from salty water or ink. The solution is heated; the solvent boils and its vapour passes into the condenser, where it is cooled and condenses back to a liquid (the distillate). The dissolved solid stays in the flask.

Two classic diagram marks: the thermometer bulb is level with the side arm (it measures the temperature of the vapour going into the condenser, not of the liquid), and cold water enters the condenser at the bottom (lower end) and leaves at the top, so the jacket stays completely full of cold water. The collecting flask is not sealed, and the condenser outlet does not dip into the distillate.

Fractional Distillation

Fractional distillation separates a mixture of miscible liquids with different boiling points (e.g. ethanol, b.p. 78 °C, and water, b.p. 100 °C; or crude oil into fractions).

  1. The mixture is heated; vapours of both liquids rise up the fractionating column (packed with glass beads, which give a large surface area).
  2. The column is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top. Vapour of the higher-boiling liquid (water) condenses on the beads and drips back into the flask.
  3. The liquid with the lowest boiling point reaches the top first, passes into the condenser and is collected. The thermometer stays at its boiling point (78 °C) while it distils.
  4. When the temperature rises, change the collecting flask — the next liquid is now distilling.
PAG C4 — Distillation: distil a mixture (e.g. orange juice, cherry cola, inks or a hydrocarbon mixture) using simple or fractional distillation. Use a Bunsen burner, water bath or electric heater (flammable liquids such as ethanol must not be heated with a naked flame), record the temperature at which each fraction distils, and test the distillate (e.g. boiling point or density) to check its purity. Anti-bumping granules give smooth boiling.

Chromatography

Chromatography separates mixtures of soluble substances (e.g. dyes in ink, food colourings, amino acids) and is used to identify substances and test their purity.

All chromatography has two phases:
• a stationary phase that does not move — the paper (paper chromatography) or the thin layer of silica / alumina on a glass, metal or plastic plate (TLC).
• a mobile phase that moves through it — the solvent (water, ethanol or another solvent) in paper chromatography and TLC, or an unreactive carrier gas in gas chromatography.
Separation depends on how each substance is distributed between the two phases. A substance that is more attracted to (spends more time in) the mobile phase travels further; one that is more strongly attracted to the stationary phase travels a shorter distance.

Choice of solvent: water (aqueous) is used for water-soluble substances such as food colourings and some inks. Substances that do not dissolve in water (e.g. permanent-marker ink, chlorophyll, many organic compounds) need a non-aqueous solvent, such as ethanol or propanone — these are flammable, so no naked flames.

Locating agents: colourless substances (e.g. amino acids, sugars) do not show up as spots. After running, the chromatogram is sprayed with a locating agent that reacts to form coloured spots (e.g. ninhydrin turns amino acids purple), or a TLC plate containing a fluorescent dye is viewed under ultraviolet light, or placed in a tank of iodine vapour.

Interpreting Chromatograms and Rf Values

Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent
(both measured from the start line; Rf has no units and is always less than 1)
  • A pure substance gives one spot (in every solvent tried). A mixture gives two or more spots.
  • Two spots that have travelled the same distance (same Rf) in the same solvent are probably the same substance. Compare with known reference substances run on the same paper.
  • Rf values depend on the solvent, the stationary phase and the temperature, so compare values obtained under the same conditions.
  • A spot that stays on the start line is insoluble in that solvent.
In a chromatogram the solvent front is 8.0 cm from the start line. A spot has an Rf of 0.65. How far did it travel?
Rearrange: distance moved by substance = Rf × distance moved by solvent = 0.65 × 8.0 = 5.2 cm.

Gas Chromatography (GC)

In gas chromatography the mobile phase is an unreactive carrier gas (e.g. nitrogen or helium) and the stationary phase is a liquid or solid coating inside a long, thin, coiled column in an oven. The vaporised sample is carried through; each substance takes a different time to come out — its retention time. A detector records a peak for each substance. The number of peaks shows how many substances are present, the retention time helps identify them (compared with standards), and the peak size shows relative amounts. GC is very sensitive and works with tiny samples — useful for testing purity of fuels, drugs, food flavourings and for detecting drugs in blood.

Choosing Chromatography to Test Purity

Which method? Paper chromatography suits coloured, soluble substances (pure = a single spot in several different solvents); TLC suits small samples and colourless organic compounds (with a locating agent or UV); GC suits volatile substances and tiny amounts (pure = a single peak at the retention time of the pure substance).

PAG C3 — Separation techniques (chromatography): use paper or thin layer chromatography to identify the mixture of dyes in an unknown ink. Draw a pencil start line ~1.5 cm from the bottom, spot the unknown and reference dyes, stand the paper in a shallow layer of solvent (below the line) in a covered beaker, then mark the solvent front, measure distances and calculate Rf values.

Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

Electrons occupy shells (energy levels). For the first 20 elements: the first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second up to 8, and the third up to 8 (then the fourth starts). The innermost shells fill first.

  • Elements are arranged in order of atomic number (number of protons = number of electrons in the atom).
  • The period (row) number = the number of occupied shells. Sodium (2,8,1) has 3 shells → Period 3.
  • The group number = the number of electrons in the outer shell (for Groups 1–7). Chlorine (2,8,7) → Group 7. Group 0 atoms have full outer shells (helium has 2).
  • Metals are on the left: their atoms have few outer electrons (1, 2 or 3) that are lost to form positive ions. Non-metals are on the right: their atoms have many outer electrons (4–7), and they gain or share electrons.

Electron arrangements, Z = 1–20: H 1 · He 2 · Li 2,1 · Be 2,2 · B 2,3 · C 2,4 · N 2,5 · O 2,6 · F 2,7 · Ne 2,8 · Na 2,8,1 · Mg 2,8,2 · Al 2,8,3 · Si 2,8,4 · P 2,8,5 · S 2,8,6 · Cl 2,8,7 · Ar 2,8,8 · K 2,8,8,1 · Ca 2,8,8,2. (Hydrogen, 1, is a non-metal usually placed on its own.)

Reactions and Electron Arrangement

The chemical reactions of an element depend on its outer-shell electrons, which depend on its atomic number. Atoms react to gain a stable, full outer shell (like the noble gases):

  • Group 0 atoms already have full outer shells, so they are unreactive (inert) and exist as single atoms.
  • Group 1 atoms have 1 outer electron, easily lost → 1+ ions. All Group 1 elements react in similar ways (e.g. with water to form hydroxides + hydrogen) because they have the same number of outer electrons.
  • Group 2 atoms lose 2 electrons → 2+ ions. Group 3 (Al) → 3+.
  • Group 6 atoms gain 2 electrons → 2− ions; Group 7 atoms gain 1 electron → 1− ions (or share electrons with other non-metals).
  • Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer electrons.

All chemical bonds are electrostatic forces of attraction between opposite charges — a bond is not a physical "stick". For covalent bonds, say the attraction is between the shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of both atoms.

Ionic Bonding

When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms. Both form ions with full outer shells (the electron arrangement of a noble gas). The oppositely charged ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction — the ionic bond.

When drawing dot and cross diagrams for binary ionic compounds (two elements):

  1. Work out the ion charges from the groups (Group 1: +, Group 2: 2+, Group 6: 2−, Group 7: −).
  2. Balance the charges to get the formula (MgCl₂ needs two Cl⁻ for one Mg²⁺).
  3. Draw each ion with its new electron arrangement; use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other's. The metal ion usually has an empty outer shell, so draw its full inner shell (or show it as empty).
  4. Put each ion in square brackets with its charge at the top right.

Covalent Bonding

When non-metal atoms bond, they share pairs of electrons so each atom gets a full outer shell. A shared pair is a single covalent bond; two shared pairs make a double bond; three make a triple bond. Covalent bonds are strong. Pairs of outer electrons not involved in bonding are called lone pairs.

Count before you finish a dot and cross diagram: every H must have 2 electrons, every other atom 8 in its outer shell, and the total number of dots must equal the outer electrons of that element (O = 6, N = 5, C = 4, Cl = 7). Shared pairs go in the overlap of the circles.

Metallic Bonding

In a metal, the atoms are packed in a regular giant lattice. Each atom's outer-shell electrons become delocalised — free to move through the whole structure. The metallic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the sea of delocalised electrons.

Mendeleev and the Modern Periodic Table

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the 60 or so known elements in order of increasing atomic weight (relative atomic mass), and put elements with similar properties in the same vertical group. His key ideas:

  • He left gaps for elements not yet discovered, and predicted their properties from the elements around them. When "eka-silicon" (germanium) was discovered in 1886, its properties closely matched his predictions — strong evidence for his table.
  • He sometimes swapped the order of pairs of elements so that they fell in the group with similar properties, even though this broke the atomic-weight order: e.g. tellurium (Ar 127.6) was placed before iodine (Ar 126.9) so that iodine sits with the other halogens.

In the early 20th century, the discovery of protons and the measurement of the atomic number of each element (Moseley, 1913) showed that the true order is by atomic number, not atomic weight. Ordering by atomic number:

  • puts every element in the group with similar properties without any swaps (Te/I, Ar/K, Co/Ni all fall into place);
  • explains the anomalies: atomic weight depends on isotopes (numbers of neutrons), which Mendeleev could not know about;
  • links position to electron arrangement: the group = outer electrons, so elements in a group react similarly;
  • confirms there are no further missing elements between known ones. The noble gases (Group 0), discovered later, slotted in as a complete new group.

C2.3 Properties of Materials

Carbon: Chains, Rings and Families of Compounds

A carbon atom has 4 outer electrons (2,4), so it forms four covalent bonds. Carbon atoms can bond strongly to each other as well as to other elements (H, O, N, Cl…). This means carbon can form:

  • long chains (straight or branched) of almost any length;
  • rings of carbon atoms;
  • single, double and triple bonds;
  • families (homologous series) of similar compounds, such as the alkanes and alkenes, with the same general formula and similar chemical properties.

This explains the vast array of natural and synthetic organic compounds — millions of them, including fuels, plastics, medicines, proteins, fats, sugars and DNA.

Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element. Carbon's allotropes have very different properties because their atoms are bonded and arranged differently.

Graphite conducts because it has delocalised electrons that can move through the structure — not "free ions". It is soft because the forces between layers are weak, not because covalent bonds are weak. Its melting point is high because melting means breaking the strong covalent bonds within the layers.

Bonds, Intermolecular Forces and Changes of State

To melt or boil a substance, energy must be transferred to overcome the forces holding the particles together. The stronger the forces that must be overcome, the more energy is needed and the higher the melting / boiling point.

Simple molecular substances (H₂O, CO₂, Cl₂) have low melting and boiling points: only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules are overcome — the strong covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken. Polymers have stronger forces between their long chains, so they are solids. Ionic compounds (NaCl, m.p. 801 °C), metals (iron, 1538 °C) and giant covalent structures (diamond, > 3500 °C) have high melting points because many strong ionic, metallic or covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy.

For simple molecules, intermolecular forces increase with the size of the molecule, so larger molecules have higher boiling points: CH₄ (−162 °C) < C₈H₁₈ (126 °C); F₂ and Cl₂ are gases, Br₂ is a liquid and I₂ is a solid at room temperature.

The #1 bonding mistake: "water boils at 100 °C because the covalent bonds break". Boiling water gives steam — still H₂O molecules. Only the intermolecular forces between the molecules are overcome. Breaking the O–H bonds would make new substances (H and O).

Bulk Properties and Structure

The properties of a bulk material come from the way huge numbers of particles are bonded and arranged — individual atoms do not have bulk properties. A single copper atom is not "shiny", "ductile" or "a conductor", and a single carbon atom is not "hard" — these are properties of the whole structure (diamond and graphite are both made only of carbon atoms but are completely different).

Nanoparticles

Nanoscience is the study of structures 1–100 nm in size (1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m), containing a few hundred to a few thousand atoms. Compare this with atoms and small molecules (~0.1–0.5 nm, i.e. 10⁻¹⁰ m) and with fine dust / "coarse particles" (PM10, 2500–10 000 nm).

A silver nanoparticle has a diameter of 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ m; a silver atom has a diameter of 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Roughly how many atoms wide is the nanoparticle?
1.6 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 0.55 × 10² ≈ 55 atoms across (an order of magnitude estimate: ~10²).

Surface Area to Volume Ratio

As particles get smaller, their surface area to volume ratio increases. For a cube of side L: surface area = 6L², volume = L³, so SA : V = 6/L. Dividing the side length by 10 multiplies the SA : V ratio by 10.

Calculate the surface area to volume ratio of a cube of side 2 nm and of side 20 nm.
2 nm cube: SA = 6 × 2² = 24 nm²; V = 2³ = 8 nm³; ratio = 24 / 8 = 3 : 1 (or 3 nm⁻¹).
20 nm cube: SA = 6 × 20² = 2400 nm²; V = 20³ = 8000 nm³; ratio = 2400 / 8000 = 0.3 : 1.
The side length is 10 times smaller, so the SA : V ratio is 10 times bigger.

How this affects properties: a much larger fraction of the atoms in a nanoparticle are on the surface, where they can react. So nanoparticles:

  • are much more reactive and make very effective catalysts — smaller quantities are needed than of the bulk material;
  • dissolve and react faster (e.g. powders vs lumps; crushed vs whole tablets);
  • can have different properties from the same material in bulk (e.g. nanoparticles of gold are red / purple and catalytic; nanoparticles of titanium dioxide are transparent but still absorb UV light).
PAG C8 link — dissolving tablets: compare how long whole, halved, quartered and powdered effervescent (or indigestion) tablets take to dissolve / stop fizzing in water. Smaller pieces have a larger total surface area, so more particles are exposed and the reaction is faster. Keep the volume and temperature of water the same; time with a stopwatch, or measure mass loss of CO₂ on a balance. (Rates are developed fully in Topic C5.)

Titanium dioxide / zinc oxideAbsorb / block UV light but are transparent (no white film on skin)Sun creams; self-cleaning glass
Silver nanoparticlesAntibacterial; large surface areaWound dressings, socks, food-storage containers, deodorants
Carbon nanotubesVery strong and light; conduct electricityReinforcing sports equipment; electronic circuits; possible drug delivery
Fullerenes (C₆₀ cages)Hollow cage; can carry other moleculesDelivering drugs to target cells; lubricants
Metal nanoparticles (Pt, Pd, Au)Huge surface area → highly catalyticCatalysts (e.g. catalytic converters, fuel cells)
Nanoparticles in cosmeticsSmall, smooth, absorbed easilyMoisturisers, deodorants

Possible Risks of Nanoparticles

  • Their small size means they could be breathed in or absorbed through the skin and pass into cells and the bloodstream, where they might cause damage (e.g. lung inflammation).
  • Their high surface area / reactivity could make them toxic or catalyse harmful reactions in the body.
  • They can be released into the environment (e.g. silver from washed clothes, sun cream into the sea) and may harm aquatic organisms or useful bacteria.
  • Their long-term effects are not yet known, because they have only been used widely for a short time — scientists disagree, so more research and regulation (e.g. labelling) is needed.
For "evaluate the use of nanoparticles" questions, give both sides: the benefit (linked to a specific property, e.g. large SA:V → better catalyst; transparent UV-blocker in sun cream) and the risk (unknown long-term effects; can enter cells / the environment), then reach a justified conclusion.
