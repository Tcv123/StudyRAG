C5.1 Monitoring Chemical Reactions

Chemists monitor reactions by measuring amounts: the concentration of solutions, the volume of gases and the mass of products. All of these link back to the mole — the unit of amount of substance (not mass, not a number of particles).

Before you start, remember the key mole equation from C3: amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ Mr. Almost every calculation in this topic is a chain of steps that goes through moles.

Concentration in mol/dm³ (HT) — C5.1a

The concentration of a solution is the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution. In mol/dm³ it is the number of moles of solute in 1 dm³ (1000 cm³) of solution.
concentration (mol/dm³) = amount of solute (mol) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
and amount (mol) = mass of solute (g) ÷ Mr    ·    volume in dm³ = volume in cm³ ÷ 1000

So the concentration depends on both the mass of solute and the volume of solution: dissolving more solute in the same volume increases the concentration; dissolving the same solute in a larger volume decreases it.

4.0 g of sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Mr = 40) is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³. Find the concentration in mol/dm³.
amount = 4.0 ÷ 40 = 0.100 mol  ·  volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³
concentration = 0.100 ÷ 0.250 = 0.400 mol/dm³

Making a standard solution (a solution of accurately known concentration): weigh the solid accurately on a balance, dissolve it in a little distilled water in a beaker, transfer the solution and all the washings into a volumetric flask, make up to the graduation mark with distilled water (bottom of the meniscus on the line) and invert several times to mix.

Concentration in g/dm³ (HT) — C5.1f

concentration (g/dm³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
To convert: concentration (g/dm³) = concentration (mol/dm³) × Mr
The 0.400 mol/dm³ NaOH solution above: 4.0 g ÷ 0.250 dm³ = 16 g/dm³ (check: 0.400 × 40 = 16 g/dm³).
What mass of NaOH is in 50.0 cm³ of it? mass = 16 × 0.0500 = 0.80 g.
Rearrange rather than memorise: mass = concentration (g/dm³) × volume (dm³). The most common error is forgetting to divide cm³ by 1000.

Titration — C5.1b

A titration is used to find the exact volume of one solution that reacts completely with a known volume of another — for example, the volume of acid that exactly neutralises 25.0 cm³ of alkali. The point at which the reaction is just complete is the end-point, shown by a colour change of an indicator.
  1. Use a pipette (with a pipette filler) to transfer exactly 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask. Add a few drops of indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein or methyl orange).
  2. Fill the burette with acid (using a funnel, then remove it). Make sure the jet below the tap is full. Record the initial reading.
  3. Add acid while swirling the flask. Near the end-point add it drop by drop until the indicator just changes colour permanently. Record the final reading.
  4. Titre = final reading − initial reading. The first run is a quick rough titration.
  5. Repeat until you get concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and take the mean of these.

Titration Calculations (HT) — C5.1c

The volume and concentration of one solution give you its amount in moles. The balanced equation gives the mole ratio. That ratio gives the moles of the other substance, from which you can find its concentration or volume.

amount (mol) = concentration (mol/dm³) × volume (dm³)
25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ NaOH is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of HCl.   NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
1. moles NaOH = 0.100 × (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00250 mol
2. ratio 1 : 1, so moles HCl = 0.00250 mol
3. concentration HCl = 0.00250 ÷ (20.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.125 mol/dm³
25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol/dm³ NaOH reacts with 18.75 cm³ of sulfuric acid.   H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
moles NaOH = 0.200 × 0.0250 = 0.00500 mol → moles H₂SO₄ = 0.00500 ÷ 2 = 0.00250 mol
concentration H₂SO₄ = 0.00250 ÷ 0.01875 = 0.133 mol/dm³ (3 s.f.)
Always use the mole ratio from the balanced equation — diprotic acids such as H₂SO₄ need half as many moles as the NaOH. Give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data (usually 3).

Moles and Gas Volumes (HT) — C5.1d, C5.1e

At the same temperature and pressure, equal amounts (moles) of any gas occupy the same volume. At room temperature and pressure (RTP) one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) — the molar gas volume.
volume of gas (dm³) = amount (mol) × 24     amount (mol) = volume (dm³) ÷ 24    (at RTP)

This means that for reacting gases the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the balanced equation. In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, 30 dm³ of hydrogen reacts with 10 dm³ of nitrogen to make 20 dm³ of ammonia.

What volume of CO₂ (at RTP) is made when 5.00 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess acid?
CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂    (Mr CaCO₃ = 100)
moles CaCO₃ = 5.00 ÷ 100 = 0.0500 mol → moles CO₂ = 0.0500 mol (1 : 1)
volume = 0.0500 × 24 = 1.20 dm³ (1200 cm³)

In the lab you measure gas volumes with a gas syringe or by collecting over water in an inverted measuring cylinder (PAG C8). Dividing the measured volume by 24 000 cm³ gives the amount in moles.

Theoretical Mass of Product — C5.1g

The balanced equation tells you how many moles of product form from a given amount of reactant. Three steps: mass → moles → use ratio → mass.

What mass of magnesium oxide forms when 6.0 g of magnesium burns completely?   2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO   (Ar Mg = 24, Mr MgO = 40)
moles Mg = 6.0 ÷ 24 = 0.25 mol → ratio 2 : 2 → moles MgO = 0.25 mol
mass MgO = 0.25 × 40 = 10 g (this is the theoretical maximum mass)

Percentage Yield — C5.1h

percentage yield = (actual mass of product ÷ theoretical maximum mass) × 100
In the experiment above only 8.0 g of MgO was collected.   % yield = (8.0 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80%.

Yields are less than 100% because: the reaction may be reversible and not go to completion; some product is lost when it is separated (filtering, transferring, evaporating); some reactants may undergo side reactions giving unwanted products; the reactants may be impure.

Atom Economy — C5.1i, C5.1j

The atom economy of a reaction is a measure of the proportion of the atoms in the reactants that end up in the desired product (rather than in waste by-products).
atom economy = (Mr of desired product ÷ sum of Mr of all reactants) × 100
Use the balanced equation — multiply each Mr by its coefficient.
Ethanol by fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂   (Mr: glucose 180, ethanol 46)
atom economy = (2 × 46) ÷ 180 × 100 = 92 ÷ 180 × 100 = 51.1%
Ethanol by hydration of ethene: C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH   atom economy = 46 ÷ (28 + 18) × 100 = 100% (only one product).
Atom economy and percentage yield are different. Atom economy is calculated from the equation (theory); percentage yield comes from the experiment. A reaction can have 100% atom economy but a low yield.

Choosing a Reaction Pathway (HT) — C5.1k

Industry chooses the route to a product by weighing up several factors, not just one:

  • Atom economy — high means less waste and better use of resources.
  • Percentage yield — high means more product from the raw materials.
  • Rate — a fast reaction gives more product per hour.
  • Equilibrium position — for reversible reactions, how far the reaction goes under the chosen conditions.
  • Usefulness of by-products — a by-product that can be sold or used raises the effective atom economy.
  • Cost and availability of raw materials, energy needed, and safety/environmental impact.

C5.2 Controlling Reactions

The rate of reaction is how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed.
mean rate of reaction = quantity of reactant used (or product formed) ÷ time taken
Units: g/s (mass) or cm³/s (gas volume)  ·  If a reaction takes time t to reach a fixed point, rate ∝ 1/t

Practical Methods for Measuring Rate — C5.2a

MethodExample reactionWhat you measure

Keep all other variables constant (control variables) — e.g. same volume and concentration of acid, same mass of solid, same temperature — so it is a fair test. Repeat readings and calculate a mean.

Interpreting Rate Graphs — C5.2b

  • The steeper the line, the faster the rate. The gradient is greatest at the start, when reactant concentration is highest.
  • The line levels off when one reactant (the limiting reactant) is used up. If the same amounts of reactants are used, the final volume is the same — a faster reaction just gets there sooner.
  • Mean rate over a period = change in volume ÷ time taken (e.g. 60 cm³ in 30 s = 2.0 cm³/s).
  • The rate at a particular instant = the gradient of the tangent to the curve at that time.
  • For "time to reach a fixed point" experiments (disappearing cross), plot 1/t as a measure of rate: a cross that disappears in 40 s gives 1/t = 0.025 s⁻¹.

Factors Affecting Rate — C5.2c, C5.2d

Collision theory: particles react only when they collide with each other with at least a minimum amount of energy — the activation energy. The rate depends on the frequency of collisions and the energy of the collisions (the proportion that are successful).
ChangeEffect on rateExplanation (collision theory)
Increase temperatureIncreasesParticles have more kinetic energy and move faster, so they collide more frequently AND a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ activation energy (more successful collisions).
Increase concentration (solutions)IncreasesMore reactant particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions.
Increase pressure (gases)IncreasesSame number of gas particles in a smaller volume, so more frequent collisions.
Increase surface area (smaller pieces / powder)IncreasesMore particles of the solid are exposed to the other reactant, so more frequent collisions.
Add a catalystIncreasesProvides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, so more collisions are successful.
Say "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second" — not just "more collisions". Only temperature changes the energy of collisions; concentration, pressure and surface area change only the frequency.

Surface Area to Volume Ratio — C5.2e

Cutting a solid into smaller pieces does not change its volume, but it increases the total surface area. So the surface area : volume ratio increases and more particles are available to collide.

Same mass of marble: large chips vs powder in excess acid. The powder has the larger surface area : volume ratio, so the reaction is faster (steeper graph) — but the same total volume of CO₂ is produced because the same mass of CaCO₃ reacts.

Catalysts — C5.2f, C5.2g, C5.2h

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being used up (it is chemically unchanged and has the same mass at the end). Only a small amount is needed, and it can be reused.
  • A catalyst works by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful.
  • Catalysts are specific — a catalyst for one reaction will not necessarily work for another.
  • A catalyst does not change the amount of product formed, or the energy change (ΔH) of the reaction.
  • Identifying a catalyst: it is written above the arrow (or appears on both sides of the overall equation) and is not in the net change; its mass is the same before and after.
ReactionCatalyst
Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂Manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂
Zinc + sulfuric acid → zinc sulfate + hydrogenCopper (powder or copper(II) sulfate solution)
Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃Iron
Contact process: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃Vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅
Cracking long-chain alkanesZeolites / aluminium oxide
On a reaction profile the catalyst lowers the peak only. The reactant and product levels — and therefore ΔH — stay the same. Don't say a catalyst "gives particles more energy".

Enzymes — C5.2i

Enzymes are biological catalysts (proteins) that speed up reactions in living organisms, e.g. amylase breaks down starch, and yeast enzymes catalyse fermentation. They work at mild temperatures, are very specific, and stop working (denature) if too hot or at the wrong pH. Industry uses enzymes to save energy (e.g. biological washing powders, food processing).

C5.3 Equilibria

Reversible Reactions — C5.3a

A reversible reaction is one where the products can react to re-form the reactants. The direction can be changed by altering the conditions. The sign ⇌ is used.
hydrated copper(II) sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (white) + water
CuSO₄·5H₂O ⇌ CuSO₄ + 5H₂O    (heat → forward; add water → reverse)

ammonium chloride ⇌ ammonia + hydrogen chloride    NH₄Cl ⇌ NH₃ + HCl

If a reversible reaction is endothermic in one direction it is exothermic by the same amount in the other. Adding water to white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate releases heat.

Dynamic Equilibrium — C5.3b

In a closed system (nothing can get in or out), a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. Both reactions are still happening, but the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant.
At equilibrium the concentrations are constant, not equal, and the reactions have not stopped — they continue at equal rates.

Changing the Position of Equilibrium (HT) — C5.3c

Le Chatelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose the change.

Example: the Haber process, N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g); the forward reaction is exothermic. There are 4 molecules of gas on the left and 2 on the right.

ChangeEquilibrium shifts…Effect on yield of NH₃
Increase concentration of N₂ or H₂To the right, to use up the added reactantIncreases
Remove NH₃ as it formsTo the right, to replace the productIncreases
Increase pressureTowards the side with fewer gas molecules (right: 2 < 4)Increases
Increase temperatureIn the endothermic direction (left) to take in heatDecreases
Decrease temperatureIn the exothermic direction (right)Increases (but rate falls)
Add a catalystNo change — speeds up forward and reverse equallyNo change (equilibrium reached sooner)
  • If both sides have the same number of gas molecules, pressure has no effect on the position.
  • To get as much product as possible: choose the temperature that favours the product side (low T if forward is exothermic), high pressure if the product side has fewer gas molecules, and remove product as it forms.
  • Colour changes show a shift: e.g. 2NO₂ (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄ (colourless), forward exothermic — cooling the mixture makes it paler.
Always give the direction of the shift AND the reason (e.g. "shifts right because there are fewer gas molecules on the right, which reduces the pressure"). The compromise conditions used in industry are covered in C6.1.
