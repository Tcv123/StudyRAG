C4.1 Predicting Chemical Reactions

In the Periodic Table, elements are arranged in order of atomic (proton) number. Elements in the same group (vertical column) have the same number of outer-shell electrons, so they have similar chemical properties. The period (horizontal row) tells you the number of occupied electron shells.

Because reactions involve the outer electrons, you can predict how an unfamiliar element will behave from its group, and how strongly it will react from its position in the group (C4.1d).

Group 1 — the alkali metals (C4.1a, C4.1b)

Physical propertiesChemical properties
  • Soft — can be cut with a knife; shiny when freshly cut, quickly tarnish in air
  • Low densities (Li, Na, K float on water)
  • Low melting points for metals, decreasing down the group (Li 181 °C, Na 98 °C, K 63 °C)
  • Good conductors of heat and electricity
  • React vigorously with water → metal hydroxide (alkaline solution) + hydrogen
  • React with chlorine → white metal chloride (ionic salt)
  • React with oxygen → metal oxide
  • Form 1+ ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) in compounds, which are white and dissolve to give colourless solutions
  • Stored under oil to keep air and water out
2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)
2K(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2KCl(s)     4Li(s) + O₂(g) → 2Li₂O(s)
MetalObservations with cold water
LithiumFloats, fizzes steadily, slowly gets smaller
SodiumFloats, melts into a silvery ball, fizzes rapidly, whizzes around the surface
PotassiumFloats, melts, fizzes violently; the hydrogen ignites with a lilac flame; may spit

Universal indicator added to the water turns purple/blue because the hydroxide formed is alkaline.

Explaining the trend. All Group 1 atoms have one outer electron, which they lose to form a 1+ ion with a full outer shell. Going down the group the atoms have more shells, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons. The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker, so the electron is lost more easily and the metal is more reactive.

Predict how rubidium (below potassium) reacts with water.
It reacts even more violently than potassium (possibly explosively), forming rubidium hydroxide and hydrogen: 2Rb + 2H₂O → 2RbOH + H₂. Its melting point will be lower than potassium's (below 63 °C) and it forms Rb⁺ ions.

Group 7 — the halogens (C4.1a, C4.1b)

HalogenFormulaState at room temperatureColour
FluorineF₂GasPale yellow
ChlorineCl₂GasPale green (yellow-green)
BromineBr₂LiquidRed-brown liquid, orange vapour
IodineI₂SolidGrey-black solid, purple vapour
  • Non-metals made of diatomic molecules (X₂); toxic and corrosive.
  • Melting and boiling points increase down the group (larger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces), and the colour gets darker.
  • React with metals to form metal halides (salts containing 1− halide ions): 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl; 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃.
  • React with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides (e.g. HCl), which dissolve in water to form acids.
  • Reactivity decreases down the group.

Explaining the trend. Halogen atoms have seven outer electrons and react by gaining one electron to form a 1− ion. Down the group the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so the nucleus attracts an incoming electron less strongly. An electron is gained less easily, so reactivity decreases.

Group 1 and Group 7 trends go in opposite directions, but the explanation is the same idea: distance and shielding weaken the attraction between nucleus and outer electrons. Group 1 → losing gets easier (more reactive); Group 7 → gaining gets harder (less reactive). Always say "outer electron" and "nucleus", not just "the atom is bigger".

Predict whether astatine (below iodine) would react with potassium iodide solution.
Astatine is less reactive than iodine (reactivity decreases down Group 7), so it cannot displace iodine — no reaction. We would also predict astatine is a dark solid with a higher melting point than iodine.

Group 0 — the noble gases (C4.1a, C4.1b)

  • Colourless gases at room temperature, made of single atoms (monatomic): He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn.
  • Very unreactive (inert) because they have a full outer shell (helium 2, the others 8). They do not need to lose, gain or share electrons, so they rarely form compounds or molecules.
  • Boiling points increase down the group (heavier atoms, stronger forces between them); density also increases.
  • Uses that depend on unreactivity/low density: helium in balloons and airships; argon in filament lamps and as a shield gas in welding; neon in advertising signs.

Three metals X, Y and Z were added to dilute acid. X: rapid bubbling, temperature rose 12 °C. Y: no bubbles. Z: slow bubbling, temperature rose 3 °C. Then Z was placed in a solution of Y's sulfate and became coated. Deduce the order.

Predicting reactions from the Periodic Table (C4.1d)

  • Metals (left and centre) tend to lose electrons and form positive ions; their oxides are basic. Non-metals (right) tend to gain or share electrons; their oxides are usually acidic.
  • Elements in the same group react in the same way and form compounds with similar formulae (NaCl, KCl, RbCl; MgO, CaO).
  • Reactivity of metals increases down a group and decreases across a period (Na > Mg > Al). Reactivity of non-metals in Group 7 decreases down the group.
  • A metal and a non-metal far apart in the table (e.g. K and F) react most vigorously.
Caesium is in Group 1 and bromine in Group 7. Predict the formula of the product and the type of reaction.
Cs forms Cs⁺; Br forms Br⁻ → caesium bromide, CsBr, an ionic white solid. As caesium is very low in Group 1 it reacts extremely vigorously: 2Cs + Br₂ → 2CsBr.

Why acidify? Carbonate ions would also form a precipitate with Ag⁺ or Ba²⁺ (a false positive), so the acid removes them as CO₂ first. Use nitric acid for the silver nitrate test (HCl would add Cl⁻ ions) and hydrochloric acid for the barium chloride test (H₂SO₄ would add SO₄²⁻ ions).

Flame tests (C4.2c, C4.2e)

  1. Clean a nichrome (or platinum) wire loop by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and holding it in a hot, blue Bunsen flame until it gives no colour.
  2. Dip the clean loop in the acid and then into the solid sample (or the solution).
  3. Hold the loop in the edge of a blue (non-luminous) flame — a yellow flame would mask the colour.
  4. Record the colour; clean the loop again before the next sample.
Metal ionFlame colour
Lithium, Li⁺Crimson (red)
Sodium, Na⁺Yellow (orange-yellow)
Potassium, K⁺Lilac
Calcium, Ca²⁺Orange-red (brick red)
Copper, Cu²⁺Green (blue-green)
Flame tests only work well for a single metal ion. In a mixture, one colour (especially sodium's intense yellow) can mask the others. Lithium's crimson and calcium's orange-red are easy to confuse — instrumental methods solve both problems.

Identifying species from test results (C4.2d) — PAG C5

PAG C5 — Identification of species: identify an unknown compound using cation tests, anion tests and flame tests. Test separate small portions of the solution so that one test does not interfere with another, and test for carbonate before adding acid for other anion tests.

A white solid dissolves in water. Flame test: lilac. Add dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate: yellow precipitate. Identify it.
Lilac → K⁺. Yellow precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ → I⁻. The compound is potassium iodide, KI.
A green solution gives a green precipitate with sodium hydroxide. Another portion, acidified with HCl and treated with barium chloride, gives a white precipitate. Identify it.
Green precipitate → Fe²⁺. White precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ → SO₄²⁻. The compound is iron(II) sulfate, FeSO₄.
Solid Q fizzes with dilute acid, giving a gas that turns limewater milky. A solution of Q in nitric acid gives a white precipitate with NaOH that dissolves in excess. Identify Q.
Fizzing + CO₂ → CO₃²⁻. White precipitate soluble in excess NaOH → Zn²⁺. Q is zinc carbonate, ZnCO₃.

Instrumental methods of analysis (C4.2f)

Chemical tests use reagents and observations by eye. Instrumental methods use machines, e.g. flame emission spectroscopy (identifies metal ions from the exact wavelengths of light they emit, and measures their concentration) and mass spectrometry (measures relative molecular mass and helps identify compounds).

AdvantageMeaning
SensitiveDetect and identify very small amounts or very low concentrations of a substance; only a tiny sample is needed
AccurateGive reliable, objective identification and precise quantitative values (no judging colours by eye); can analyse mixtures
Rapid (speed)Results in seconds or minutes; can be automated to run many samples

Disadvantages: the instruments are expensive, need trained operators, and results must be compared with reference data from known samples.

Interpreting instrumental results (C4.2g)

An instrumental result is interpreted by comparing it with a reference set of data presented in the same form (chart or table). If the pattern matches, the substance is identified.

Flame emission spectra. Each metal ion gives its own pattern of coloured lines (a "fingerprint"). Lines in an unknown spectrum that match a reference show which ions are present — even in a mixture.

Mass spectra. A mass spectrometer turns molecules into positive ions and sorts them by their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Features of the chart:

  • x-axis: m/z (for ions with a 1+ charge this is the mass of the ion). y-axis: relative abundance (%).
  • The molecular ion peak (M⁺) is the peak with the highest m/z (furthest right). Its m/z value equals the relative molecular mass, Mᵣ, of the compound.
  • Peaks at lower m/z are fragments formed when the molecule breaks up. The pattern of fragments is a "fingerprint" which can be matched to a reference database.
  • The tallest peak (the base peak, 100%) is the most common fragment — it is not necessarily the molecular ion.
An unknown liquid gives a mass spectrum whose highest-m/z peak is at 58. Reference data: propanone Mᵣ 58, butane Mᵣ 58, ethanol Mᵣ 46, propan-1-ol Mᵣ 60. What can you conclude?
The molecular ion gives Mᵣ = 58, which rules out ethanol and propan-1-ol. Propanone and butane both have Mᵣ 58, so the fragmentation pattern must be compared with the reference spectra to decide: the compound is whichever reference spectrum matches peak-for-peak.
