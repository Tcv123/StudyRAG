Topic C6 is synoptic: it applies ideas from C1–C5 (reactivity, electrolysis, rates, equilibrium, bonding) to real-world problems — making metals and fertilisers, choosing materials, organic chemistry and the Earth's atmosphere and water supply.

C6.1 Improving Processes and Products

Extracting Metals Using Carbon — C6.1a

Most metals are found in the Earth's crust as compounds in ores (rocks containing enough metal compound to make extraction worthwhile). Extraction is a reduction — the metal compound loses oxygen. The method depends on the metal's position in the reactivity series relative to carbon.
  • Iron (a ferrous metal) is extracted in a blast furnace. Coke (carbon) burns to make carbon monoxide, which reduces iron(III) oxide:
    C + O₂ → CO₂  ·  CO₂ + C → 2CO  ·  Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
  • Non-ferrous metals below carbon are extracted the same way, e.g. copper and zinc:
    2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂    ZnO + C → Zn + CO
  • Carbon is used because it is cheap, plentiful and more reactive than these metals, so it takes the oxygen from them. The carbon is oxidised; the metal oxide is reduced.
PAG C1: heat a mixture of black copper(II) oxide and carbon powder strongly in a crucible or ignition tube. Brown/pink copper forms, showing that carbon is more reactive than copper. Tip the cooled product into water to separate the dense copper from unreacted carbon.

Extracting Metals by Electrolysis — C6.1b

Metals more reactive than carbon (e.g. aluminium, sodium, magnesium) cannot be reduced by carbon, so they are extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound. This uses a lot of electrical energy, so it is expensive.

  • Aluminium oxide (from the ore bauxite) has a very high melting point (about 2050 °C). Dissolving it in molten cryolite lowers the operating temperature to about 950 °C, saving energy. The ions must be free to move, so the compound must be molten or dissolved.
  • Positive Al³⁺ ions move to the negative cathode and gain electrons; negative O²⁻ ions move to the positive anode and lose electrons.
  • The oxygen reacts with the carbon anodes to form carbon dioxide, so the anodes must be replaced regularly.
  • Electrolysis of aqueous solutions (PAG C2: aqueous sodium chloride or copper(II) sulfate, testing the gases formed) is also used industrially, e.g. to purify copper and to make chlorine and sodium hydroxide.
Extraction by electrolysis costs more than reduction with carbon because of the large amount of electricity needed to melt the compound and to decompose it. That is why aluminium is worth recycling.

The Haber Process — Rate vs Equilibrium (HT) — C6.1d, C6.1e, C6.1f

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    forward reaction exothermic (ΔH = −92 kJ/mol)
Conditions: about 450 °C, about 200 atmospheres, iron catalyst

The trade-off (C6.1d): the conditions that give the highest yield are not the ones that give the fastest rate, so industry uses compromise conditions.

ConditionEffect on equilibrium yieldEffect on rateCompromise chosen
TemperatureForward reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature gives a higher yieldLow temperature makes the reaction too slow~450 °C: reasonable yield at a reasonable rate
Pressure4 gas molecules → 2, so high pressure gives a higher yieldHigh pressure increases the rate too~200 atm: very high pressures need expensive, thick-walled plant and lots of energy, and are more dangerous
Catalyst (iron)No effect on yieldIncreases rate — equilibrium reached faster at a lower temperatureAlways used; saves energy

Interpreting graphs (C6.1e): on graphs like this, read across at a fixed pressure to compare temperatures (lower temperature → higher yield), or along one curve to see the effect of pressure (higher pressure → higher yield). On a graph of rate against temperature, rate rises steeply with temperature. Combining both explains the compromise.

The Contact Process (HT) — C6.1d, C6.1f

Sulfur burned in air: S + O₂ → SO₂
2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)    forward reaction exothermic
Conditions: about 450 °C, about 1–2 atm, vanadium(V) oxide (V₂O₅) catalyst
SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated sulfuric acid (forming oleum, H₂S₂O₇), then diluted with water to give H₂SO₄
  • Temperature: low favours SO₃ (exothermic) but is slow; ~450 °C is a compromise.
  • Pressure: 3 gas molecules → 2, so high pressure would help, but the equilibrium yield is already very high (over 95%) at atmospheric pressure, so the extra cost of high pressure is not worth it.
  • SO₃ is not added directly to water because the reaction is so exothermic it forms a corrosive mist.

Commercial conditions (C6.1f) depend on:

  • Availability and cost of raw materials — N₂ from air is free, H₂ from natural gas costs money; recycling unreacted gases means no raw material is wasted.
  • Energy supplies — high temperatures and pressures need energy (cost and CO₂ emissions); the exothermic reaction's heat is used to preheat incoming gases.
  • Control of equilibrium position and rate — chosen to give an acceptable yield quickly, as in the tables above; removing product (liquefying NH₃) pulls the equilibrium to the right.

Fertilisers — C6.1g, C6.1i

Plants need three main elements for healthy growth. Fertilisers replace the elements removed from the soil by crops, increasing crop yield.

ElementWhy plants need itExample compounds
Nitrogen (N)To make proteins — healthy leaf and stem growthAmmonium nitrate, ammonium sulfate, urea
Phosphorus (P)Healthy root growthAmmonium phosphate, superphosphate
Potassium (K)Healthy flowers and fruit; resistance to diseasePotassium chloride, potassium sulfate

NPK fertilisers are formulations containing all three in chosen proportions.

Importance of the Haber process (C6.1g): plants cannot use nitrogen gas from the air directly. The Haber process "fixes" atmospheric nitrogen as ammonia, which is turned into nitrogen fertilisers. Without it, there would not be enough food for the world's population — around half the world's food is grown using fertilisers made from Haber-process ammonia.

Recycling — C6.1m, C6.1n

Some materials are recycled for a different use (C6.1m):

  • Glass bottles are crushed and melted to make new glass, or crushed for use in road surfaces ("glasphalt") and as building aggregate — viable because glass melts at a lower temperature than making it from sand, saving energy.
  • PET plastic bottles are shredded, melted and spun into polyester fibres for fleece clothing and carpets — viable because PET is a thermoplastic that can be remelted, and it reduces landfill and crude oil use.
  • Scrap steel is added to the furnace when making new steel; aluminium cans are melted and recast — recycling aluminium uses about 5% of the energy needed to extract it by electrolysis.

Factors affecting decisions on recycling (C6.1n):

  • Energy needed to collect, sort and reprocess compared with extracting new material.
  • Cost of collection, transport and separation (mixed materials are hard to separate).
  • Value of the material and scarcity/finite nature of the raw material.
  • Environmental impact: reduced mining and quarrying, less landfill, fewer emissions.
  • Whether the quality of the recycled material is good enough for the new use.

Corrosion — C6.1p

Corrosion is the destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with substances in its environment. It is an oxidation. The corrosion of iron and steel is called rusting; it needs both water and oxygen.
iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)     4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O
  • Rusting is faster in salt water (e.g. near the sea or on gritted roads) and at higher temperatures.
  • Other metals corrode too, but not all are damaged: aluminium forms a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide that sticks to the surface and protects the metal underneath. Copper slowly forms a green coating (patina). Rust is flaky and falls off, exposing fresh iron, so iron keeps corroding.

Sacrificial protectionA more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) is attached; it is oxidised instead of the iron, so it corrodes ("is sacrificed") firstZinc or magnesium blocks on ship hulls, pipelines, oil rigs
GalvanisingCoating of zinc — a barrier and sacrificial protection; still works if scratchedBuckets, car bodies, fences, roofing
Tin plating (food cans) is only a barrier. Tin is less reactive than iron, so if the coating is scratched the iron rusts even faster. Zinc (galvanising) still protects when scratched because zinc is more reactive than iron.

C6.2 Organic Chemistry

Functional Groups and Homologous Series — C6.2a

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula. Successive members differ by CH₂; they have similar chemical reactions and a gradual trend in physical properties (e.g. boiling point increases with chain length). A functional group is the atom or group of atoms that determines how the molecule reacts.
Homologous seriesFunctional groupGeneral formulaName endingExample
AlkanesC–C single bonds only (saturated)CₙH₂ₙ₊₂-aneEthane, C₂H₆
AlkenesC=C double bond (unsaturated)CₙH₂ₙ-eneEthene, C₂H₄
Alcohols–OH (hydroxyl)CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH-olEthanol, C₂H₅OH
Carboxylic acids–COOH (carboxyl)CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH-oic acidEthanoic acid, CH₃COOH

Name stems give the number of carbon atoms: meth- 1, eth- 2, prop- 3, but- 4, pent- 5. (For carboxylic acids the carbon in –COOH is counted.)

Displayed Formulae — C6.2b

A displayed formula shows every atom and every bond. Carbon always forms 4 bonds, hydrogen 1, oxygen 2.

Count bonds on every carbon — it must have exactly four (a double bond counts as two). In a carboxylic acid the –COOH carbon has a C=O and a C–O–H. Methanoic acid has only one carbon.

Reactions of Alkanes, Alkenes and Alcohols — C6.2c

Combustion. All three series burn. Complete combustion (plenty of oxygen) gives carbon dioxide and water; incomplete combustion gives carbon monoxide and/or soot (carbon). Alkenes burn with a smokier flame than alkanes.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O   ·   2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O
C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O   ·   C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Addition reactions of alkenes. The C=C double bond opens and atoms add across it, making a saturated product.

  • Bromine: C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ (1,2-dibromoethane). Bromine water turns from orange to colourless — the test for an alkene (C=C). Alkanes do not decolourise it.
  • Hydrogen: C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ (nickel catalyst, about 150 °C) — the alkene becomes an alkane. Propene + hydrogen → propane.
  • Other members react the same way: propene + bromine → 1,2-dibromopropane, C₃H₆Br₂.

Oxidation of alcohols. Warming an alcohol with acidified potassium manganate(VII) oxidises it to the carboxylic acid with the same number of carbon atoms. The purple solution turns colourless.

ethanol → ethanoic acid    CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O    ([O] = oxygen from the oxidising agent)
propanol → propanoic acid   ·   butanol → butanoic acid

Addition Polymerisation — C6.2d, C6.2g

In addition polymerisation, many small alkene monomers join together. The C=C double bond in each monomer opens and the monomers link into a long saturated chain. The polymer is the only product.
  • Monomer → polymer: draw the two C=C carbons with a single bond between them, keep their other atoms/groups in place, add bonds sticking out through brackets on each side and write n.
  • Polymer → monomer: take the repeat unit, remove the extension bonds and brackets, and put a double bond between the two carbons.
  • Other examples: chloroethene → poly(chloroethene) (PVC); tetrafluoroethene → poly(tetrafluoroethene) (PTFE).

Condensation Polymerisation (HT) — C6.2e

In condensation polymerisation, monomers with two functional groups each (the minimum needed so the chain can keep growing at both ends) react together, and a small molecule such as water (or HCl) is given off every time a link forms. Usually there are two different monomers, each with two of the same functional group.
Addition polymerisationCondensation polymerisation
MonomersOne type, containing C=CUsually two types, each with two functional groups
ProductsPolymer onlyPolymer + small molecule (e.g. H₂O) for every link formed
Repeat unitSame atoms as the monomerContains one of each monomer minus the small molecule(s)
For n molecules of each monomer, about 2n small molecules are lost, because each repeat unit contains two new links. Make sure the ends of each block keep the right number of bonds.

Making Nylon in the Lab (HT) — C6.2f

  1. Pour a solution of hexane-1,6-diamine (in water) into a small beaker.
  2. Carefully pour a solution of decanedioyl dichloride (in cyclohexane) down the side, so it floats on top without mixing.
  3. Nylon forms where the two layers meet. Grip it with tweezers and pull it out slowly, winding it onto a glass rod; the polymer keeps forming as fresh solutions meet.
  4. Wash the nylon with water before handling. The small molecule given off here is HCl. Wear gloves and eye protection; the reagents are corrosive/harmful and cyclohexane is flammable — use a fume cupboard.

Generality of Reactions — C6.2i

The reactions of an organic compound are determined by its functional group, not by the length of its carbon chain. So if you know how ethene reacts with bromine, you can predict that any alkene will react the same way; if ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid, propanol is oxidised to propanoic acid. This is why compounds are grouped into homologous series.

Crude Oil and Fractional Distillation — C6.2j, C6.2k, C6.2l

Crude oil is a mixture of many hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes (CnH2n+2). It is separated into fractions — groups of hydrocarbons with similar numbers of carbon atoms and similar boiling points — by fractional distillation.

Describe (C6.2j): crude oil is heated until it vaporises and the vapour enters the bottom of the column. The column is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Vapours rise; each fraction condenses at the level where the temperature is just below its boiling point and is piped off. Very large molecules (bitumen) do not vaporise and are run off at the bottom; the smallest stay as gases and leave at the top.

Explain (C6.2k): larger alkane molecules have stronger intermolecular forces between them (more contact area), so more energy is needed to separate them — they have higher boiling points and condense lower down. Smaller molecules have weaker intermolecular forces, lower boiling points, and rise higher. The covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken.

Crude Oil as a Resource — C6.2m, C6.2n

  • Crude oil is the main source of hydrocarbons and the feedstock (raw material) for the petrochemical industry, which makes plastics, solvents, lubricants, detergents, dyes and medicines.
  • Modern life depends on hydrocarbons: fuels for transport, heating and electricity generation, and the materials listed above.
  • Crude oil is a finite (non-renewable) resource: it formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient marine organisms and is being used far faster than it forms. Burning it also releases CO₂. This is why alternatives (biofuels, electric vehicles, recycling of plastics) matter.

Cracking — C6.2o

Cracking is the thermal decomposition of long-chain alkanes into shorter-chain alkanes (useful as fuels such as petrol) and alkenes (used to make polymers and other chemicals).
C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄    (decane → octane + ethene)   ·   C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₄H₈ + 2C₂H₄
  • Conditions: the long-chain hydrocarbons are heated to vaporise them, then passed over a hot catalyst (zeolite, or silica/aluminium oxide) at about 500 °C — catalytic cracking; or mixed with steam and heated to a very high temperature (over 800 °C) — steam cracking.
  • Reasons: fractional distillation gives more long-chain hydrocarbons than are needed and not enough short-chain ones (petrol is in high demand). Cracking matches supply to demand, and produces alkenes, which are reactive and used to make polymers (e.g. poly(ethene)) and ethanol.
To show the gas contains alkenes, shake it with bromine water: it turns from orange to colourless. Liquid paraffin itself (alkanes) does not decolourise bromine water.

Chemical Cells and Fuel Cells — C6.2p, C6.2q

A chemical cell (battery) produces a potential difference from a chemical reaction between its contents. It produces a voltage until one of the reactants is used up; then it is "flat". (Rechargeable cells can have the reaction reversed by an external current.)

A fuel cell is a cell supplied continuously with a fuel and oxygen (or air); the fuel is oxidised electrochemically to produce a potential difference. It keeps working as long as fuel is supplied.
Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell: advantagesDisadvantages
Only product is water — no CO₂ or pollutants at the point of useHydrogen is a flammable gas, hard to store (high-pressure tanks) and transport
Does not run down or need recharging — refuel in minutesMost hydrogen is made from fossil fuels (or electrolysis using electricity that may come from fossil fuels)
More efficient than burning fuel in an engine; few moving parts, quietFuel cells are expensive (platinum catalyst); few refuelling stations
No toxic metal waste at end of life (unlike many batteries)Performance can drop at low temperatures

Other fuel cells and cells for given uses: e.g. methanol fuel cells are easier to refuel (liquid fuel) but produce CO₂. For a mobile phone, a rechargeable lithium-ion battery is more practical (small, no fuel supply needed). For a bus or a spacecraft that needs long range and quick refuelling, a hydrogen fuel cell may be better — in spacecraft the water produced is drunk by astronauts.

C6.3 Interpreting and Interacting with Earth Systems

The Early Atmosphere — C6.3a

Evidence about the atmosphere billions of years ago is limited (there were no observers; evidence comes from rocks, fossils and comparison with other planets and volcanoes today), so the theories are uncertain. One accepted model:

  • In the first billion years, intense volcanic activity released gases that formed the early atmosphere: mainly carbon dioxide, with water vapour, nitrogen and small amounts of methane and ammonia. There was little or no oxygen — similar to the atmospheres of Mars and Venus today.
  • As the Earth cooled, water vapour condensed to form the oceans.
  • Carbon dioxide dissolved in the oceans; carbonates precipitated forming sediments, and later marine organisms locked carbon into shells and skeletons (limestone) and fossil fuels. So the amount of CO₂ in the atmosphere decreased.

Development of an Oxygen-Rich Atmosphere — C6.3b

  • About 2.7 billion years ago, algae (and later plants) evolved and produced oxygen by photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. This also removed more CO₂.
  • Oxygen levels rose gradually over hundreds of millions of years, allowing animals to evolve. An ozone layer formed, protecting land life from UV.
  • Nitrogen built up (released by volcanoes, from ammonia, and by denitrifying bacteria) and, being very unreactive, stayed in the atmosphere.

The Greenhouse Effect — C6.3c

  • The Sun emits mostly short-wavelength radiation, which passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, warming it.
  • The warm Earth emits longer-wavelength infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases — carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour — absorb this infrared (their bonds vibrate) and then re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the surface, which warms the lower atmosphere.
  • The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth's mean temperature about 33 °C warmer than it would otherwise be — essential for life. Increasing greenhouse gas concentrations enhance it.

Evidence for Human-Caused Climate Change — C6.3d

  • Evidence: atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm today. The rise correlates with increasing consumption of fossil fuels (and deforestation). Global mean temperature has risen by over 1 °C over the same period. Ice cores show past CO₂ levels and temperatures changing together.
  • Uncertainties: a correlation does not on its own prove cause; past data (from ice cores, tree rings) are indirect and less precise; there are natural causes of variation (volcanic eruptions, changes in the Sun's output, ocean cycles); the climate system is very complex, so computer models make assumptions; data were collected with different methods over time and location.
  • Despite uncertainties, the great majority of climate scientists, using peer-reviewed evidence, agree that human activities are the main cause of recent warming.
CM6.3ii: use orders of magnitude. CO₂ is only ~0.04% of the air, but a rise from 280 to 420 ppm is a 50% increase — small percentages of a strong absorber still matter.

Potable Water — C6.3g

Potable water is water that is safe to drink: it has low levels of dissolved salts and microbes. It is not chemically pure — it still contains some dissolved substances.
  • Ground and surface water (rain collected in reservoirs, rivers, aquifers) is the easiest to treat: sedimentation and filtration remove solids; chlorination kills microbes.
  • Waste water contains organic matter and harmful microbes, so it needs more stages (screening, sedimentation, biological treatment) before it can be safely returned to the environment or reused.
  • Salt water needs desalination, which uses a great deal of energy, so it is expensive — used mainly in dry countries with plentiful energy but little fresh water.
