C1.1 The Particle Model

The particle model says that all matter is made of very small particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are constantly moving. Whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas depends on how its particles are arranged, how they move and how strongly they are held together.

The model is a model: a simplified picture used to explain and predict observations. In the simple version the particles are drawn as small, solid spheres. That is enough to explain melting, boiling, diffusion and compression, but (as you will see under the limitations below) it leaves a lot out.

The particle model explains these bulk properties. A gas can be squashed because there is lots of empty space between its particles. A liquid flows because its particles can move past one another. A solid keeps its shape because its particles are held in fixed positions by strong forces.

When drawing particle diagrams, keep the particles the same size in all three states. In a liquid, most particles should still be touching (no big gaps) — a very common mistake is to draw a liquid that looks like a gas. Particles do not get bigger when heated; they just move faster and (usually) further apart.

Changes of State

A change of state happens when energy is transferred to or from a substance. Heating gives the particles more kinetic energy, so they can overcome more of the forces holding them together. Cooling removes energy, so forces of attraction pull the particles closer together.

ChangeFrom → toEnergyWhat happens to the particles
Meltingsolid → liquidtaken inParticles vibrate so much that they break free of fixed positions; enough forces are overcome for them to move around each other.
Freezingliquid → solidgiven outParticles slow down; forces of attraction hold them in fixed positions.
Boiling / evaporatingliquid → gastaken inParticles gain enough energy to overcome almost all the forces and escape. Boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point; evaporation happens only from the surface, at any temperature.
Condensinggas → liquidgiven outParticles slow down and forces pull them close together again.
Sublimingsolid → gastaken inParticles go straight from fixed positions to moving freely, e.g. solid carbon dioxide (dry ice), iodine.

Changes of state are physical changes: no new substance forms, the particles themselves are unchanged and the change is reversible. The mass is conserved because the number of particles stays the same — they are only rearranged. (Ice that melts in a sealed flask has the same mass as the water it becomes.)

Heating and Cooling Curves

If a pure substance is heated steadily, its temperature rises until it reaches its melting point. The temperature then stays constant while the substance melts, even though energy is still being supplied, because the energy is being used to overcome the forces between particles rather than to make them move faster. The same happens at the boiling point.

The flat section on a cooling curve is where the substance freezes. Its temperature tells you the melting point (melting point = freezing point for a pure substance). A pure substance gives a sharp, flat plateau; an impure one solidifies over a range of temperatures (see C2.1).

Melting Point, Boiling Point and State at a Given Temperature

  • Below its melting point a substance is a solid.
  • Between its melting point and boiling point it is a liquid.
  • Above its boiling point it is a gas.

The size of the melting and boiling points depends on how strong the forces between particles are. Stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so the melting point is higher. Topic C2.3 explains this in terms of bonds and intermolecular forces.

Diffusion and Gas Pressure (Using the Model)

The particle model also explains:

  • Diffusion — the net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to lower concentration because of their random motion. Smells spread across a room; a purple crystal of potassium manganate(VII) slowly colours still water. Diffusion is fastest in gases (fast particles, large spaces) and very slow in liquids.
  • Gas pressure — gas particles collide with the walls of their container. Heating the gas makes the particles move faster, so they collide more often and harder — the pressure increases.

Physical Changes and Chemical Changes

The particle model gives a clear way to tell physical and chemical changes apart:

In a physical change the particles stay the same — they are only rearranged or moved further apart / closer together. No new substance forms. Examples: melting, boiling, dissolving.

In a chemical change (a chemical reaction) the atoms are rearranged into new substances: bonds between atoms are broken and new bonds are made. Examples: burning, rusting, neutralisation.

When magnesium ribbon is heated in air it burns with a bright white light and becomes a white powder. Is this a physical or chemical change? Explain using the particle model.
Chemical change. The magnesium atoms react with oxygen molecules from the air: the bonds in O₂ are broken and new bonds between magnesium and oxygen form, giving a new substance, magnesium oxide (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO). The white powder cannot be turned back into magnesium by cooling. Compare this with melting wax, where the same wax molecules simply move further apart and the wax re-solidifies on cooling.
Dissolving is a physical change, not a chemical reaction: salt can be recovered by evaporating the water. Don't say the salt "disappears" — its particles spread out between the water particles, and the total mass of the solution = mass of salt + mass of water.

Elements, Compounds and Mixtures (Particle Diagrams)

The particle model on its own does not explain why different materials have different properties — to do that we need to look at the particles themselves. Different substances are made of different particles:

  • An element contains only one type of atom (e.g. Fe, O₂, He). It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means.
  • A compound contains atoms of two or more different elements chemically bonded in fixed proportions (e.g. H₂O, NaCl, CO₂).
  • A mixture contains two or more substances not chemically joined; they can be separated by physical methods (C2.1).

Limitations of the Particle Model (HT)

In the simple particle model, particles are drawn as small, solid, inelastic spheres — like bowling balls. This is useful, but it is not how real particles behave. You need to be able to explain the limitations of this model when it is used to explain changes of state.

Limitation (HT)Why it matters for changes of state
It does not take into account the forces of attraction between particlesChanges of state happen when these forces are overcome (melting, boiling) or formed (condensing, freezing). Without forces, the model cannot explain why energy is needed to melt or boil a substance, or why different substances melt at different temperatures.
It does not take into account the size of the particlesReal particles differ in size and shape (a helium atom vs a large wax molecule), which affects how strongly they attract each other and so their melting / boiling points. The spheres are all drawn the same size.
It does not take into account the space between the particlesDiagrams are not to scale. In a gas the particles are much further apart than shown (about 10 particle diameters apart at room temperature), and particles themselves are mostly empty space, not solid.
Particles are treated as inelastic (solid, hard) spheresReal particles are not solid balls; they do not have fixed edges and can be distorted. Molecules can also vibrate and rotate.
Particles are shown still in a diagramReal particles are always moving (even in a solid they vibrate), and the amount of movement depends on temperature.
(HT) The three limitations named in the specification are: the model ignores the forces of attraction between particles, the size of the particles, and the space between them. In a 2- or 3-mark question, name the limitation and link it to the change of state, e.g. "the model doesn't show the forces between particles, so it can't explain why energy has to be supplied to melt the solid".
Practical opportunity (C1.1): observe changes of state and compare them with chemical changes — e.g. heat ice in a beaker and record the temperature every 30 s to plot a heating curve; melt and re-solidify wax or stearic acid (physical, reversible) and compare with heating magnesium ribbon or copper carbonate (chemical: new substance, not reversed on cooling). There is no assessed PAG attached to Topic C1, but this work develops the measuring skills used in PAG C4 and PAG C8.

C1.2 Atomic Structure

An atom is the smallest part of an element that still has the chemical properties of that element. Atoms are made of three sub-atomic particles: protons and neutrons in a tiny central nucleus, and electrons arranged in shells (energy levels) around it.

How the Model of the Atom Has Changed

Our model of the atom has been developed over 200 years. Each time new experimental evidence appeared that the old model could not explain, the model was changed or replaced. This is a key example of how science works: models are tested, and kept only while they fit the evidence.

Scientist(s)Model / discoveryEvidence and reason for change
John Dalton (early 1800s)Atoms are tiny, solid, indivisible spheres. Atoms of the same element are identical; different elements have different atoms. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to make compounds.Based on the fixed ratios in which elements combine (laws of chemical combination). First modern atomic theory.
J. J. Thomson (1897)Discovered the electron. "Plum pudding" model: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.Cathode-ray experiments showed atoms contain much smaller, negatively charged particles — so atoms are not indivisible. Atoms are neutral, so there must be positive charge to balance the electrons.
Hans Geiger & Ernest Marsden (1909, working with Rutherford)Alpha-particle scattering experiment: fired positive alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil.Most alpha particles passed straight through; a few were deflected; a very small number (about 1 in 8000) bounced back. This could not happen with the plum pudding model, where the positive charge is spread out.
Ernest Rutherford (1911)Nuclear model: almost all the mass and all the positive charge is concentrated in a tiny central nucleus; electrons orbit around it; the atom is mostly empty space.Explained the scattering results (see below).
Niels Bohr (1913)Electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed shells (energy levels) at specific distances. Electrons can move between shells by absorbing or emitting energy.Rutherford's model could not explain why orbiting electrons do not spiral into the nucleus, or why elements give out light of specific colours (line spectra). Bohr's calculations agreed with experimental observations.
James Chadwick (1932)Discovered the neutron in the nucleus.Explained why the mass of nuclei was bigger than the mass of their protons alone, and explained isotopes.

"Some alpha particles bounced back from the gold foil, which the plum pudding model could not explain, so Rutherford proposed that the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus."

The Structure of the Atom Today

The modern model describes the atom as a positively charged nucleus surrounded by negatively charged electrons.

  • The nucleus contains protons (positive) and neutrons (no charge).
  • The radius of the nucleus is much smaller than the radius of the atom — about 1/10 000 (10⁻⁴) of it.
  • Almost all of the mass of the atom is in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons are about 1836 times heavier than electrons.
  • The electrons occupy shells (energy levels) at different distances from the nucleus.
  • An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it has no overall charge.

Size and Scale of Atoms

Atoms are extremely small. You need to recall the typical size (order of magnitude):

Typical atomic radius ≈ 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (0.1 nm)  ·  Typical bond length ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m
Radius of a nucleus ≈ 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m (about 1/10 000 of the atomic radius)

Some ways to relate atomic sizes to the everyday world:

  • A human hair is about 1 × 10⁻⁴ m wide — roughly 1 million carbon atoms side by side (10⁻⁴ ÷ 10⁻¹⁰ = 10⁶).
  • If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea in the centre.
  • A 1 cm line of atoms would contain about 1 × 10⁸ atoms (10⁻² ÷ 10⁻¹⁰).
A gold atom has a radius of 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Its nucleus has a radius of 7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. How many times bigger is the atom than the nucleus?
1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ ÷ 7 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 0.2 × 10⁵ = 2 × 10⁴ (20 000 times).
A nanoparticle has a diameter of 20 nm. About how many atoms (diameter 0.2 nm) would fit across it?
20 nm ÷ 0.2 nm = 100 atoms — so nanoparticles contain a few hundred to a few thousand atoms in total (see C2.3).

Atomic Number, Mass Number and Standard Notation

Atomic number (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus. Every atom of the same element has the same atomic number, and it is different for every element. (It is also the number of electrons in a neutral atom.)

Mass number (A) = total number of protons + neutrons in the nucleus.

Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number = A − Z.

On the Periodic Table, the larger number given for each element is usually the relative atomic mass (an average over its isotopes, C2.1) and the smaller number is the atomic number. For chlorine, Ar = 35.5 — not a whole number, because chlorine is a mixture of isotopes.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers.
  • Isotopes have the same chemical properties, because chemical reactions depend on electrons, and isotopes have the same number of electrons (and the same electron arrangement).
  • They can have slightly different physical properties (e.g. density) because their masses are different. Some isotopes are radioactive (e.g. carbon-14).
  • Chlorine has two common isotopes: chlorine-35 (17 p, 18 n) and chlorine-37 (17 p, 20 n).
  • Hydrogen has three: ¹H (no neutrons), ²H deuterium (1 neutron), ³H tritium (2 neutrons).
A common misconception is that a neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and neutrons. It has equal numbers of protons and electrons. The number of neutrons can be anything (sodium-23 has 11 protons but 12 neutrons; uranium-238 has 92 protons and 146 neutrons).

Ions

An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom (or group of atoms) loses or gains electrons.
• Losing electrons → more protons than electrons → positive ion (e.g. Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺).
• Gaining electrons → more electrons than protons → negative ion (e.g. Cl⁻, O²⁻, N³⁻).
The nucleus does not change — the number of protons (and neutrons) stays the same.

An ion has 16 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons. Identify it and write it in standard notation.
16 protons → atomic number 16 → sulfur. Mass number = 16 + 18 = 34. Electrons − protons = 18 − 16 = 2 extra electrons, so the charge is 2−.
Answer: ³⁴₁₆S²⁻ (a sulfide ion of the isotope sulfur-34).
Two particles, X and Y, both have 20 protons. X has 20 neutrons and 20 electrons; Y has 22 neutrons and 18 electrons. Explain how X and Y are related.
Both have 20 protons, so both are calcium. They have different numbers of neutrons, so they are different isotopes (calcium-40 and calcium-42). Y has 2 fewer electrons than protons, so Y is a Ca²⁺ ion, while X is a neutral atom.
A positive ion has lost electrons, not gained protons. In "explain" answers, say e.g. "a magnesium atom loses two electrons to form Mg²⁺; it still has 12 protons but only 10 electrons, so the overall charge is 2+".
