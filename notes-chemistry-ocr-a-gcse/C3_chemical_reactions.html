C3.1 Introducing Chemical Reactions

A chemical reaction makes new substances. The atoms in the reactants are rearranged — bonds are broken and new bonds are made — but no atoms are created or destroyed. A chemical equation shows this change in symbols.

Formulae of elements (C3.1a, C3.1c)

You will be given a Periodic Table in the exam. You should be able to use it to write symbols and formulae for at least the first 20 elements, the elements of Groups 1, 7 and 0, and any other common elements met in the course (e.g. Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Pb).

Type of elementHow it is writtenExamples

Giant covalent non-metalsJust the symbolC (diamond, graphite), Si
Noble gases (Group 0, single atoms)Just the symbol (monatomic)He, Ne, Ar
Diatomic moleculesSymbol with subscript 2H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂
Remember the seven diatomic elements: H₂, N₂, O₂ and the halogens F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. Writing "Cl" for chlorine gas, or "O" for oxygen gas, in an equation loses the mark.

Simple covalent compounds are made of small molecules. Their formula tells you exactly how many atoms of each element are in one molecule. Learn these common ones:

Formulae of ionic compounds from ions (C3.1a, C3.1d)

An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. Use the charges of the ions to work out how many of each you need.

Ion charges for simple ions follow from the group number: Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, Group 3 → 3+, Group 6 → 2−, Group 7 → 1−. Transition metals can form more than one ion, which is why names include Roman numerals: iron(II) = Fe²⁺, iron(III) = Fe³⁺.

Write the formulae of (a) aluminium oxide, (b) calcium hydroxide, (c) ammonium sulfate.
(a) Al³⁺ and O²⁻. Lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6 → 2 Al³⁺ (+6) and 3 O²⁻ (−6) → Al₂O₃.
(b) Ca²⁺ and OH⁻ → one Ca²⁺ needs two OH⁻. A compound ion that appears more than once goes in brackets → Ca(OH)₂.
(c) NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ → two NH₄⁺ for one SO₄²⁻ → (NH₄)₂SO₄.
Never change the formula of a compound ion: sulfate is always SO₄²⁻. Use brackets when you need more than one of it, e.g. Al₂(SO₄)₃ — not Al₂S₃O₁₂.

Writing balanced equations (C3.1b, C3.1c)

Because atoms are only rearranged, there must be the same number of each type of atom on both sides. This is the principle of conservation of mass written in symbols.

  1. Write the word equation.
  2. Write the correct formula for every reactant and product (do not change these later).
  3. Count atoms of each element on each side.
  4. Balance by putting numbers in front of formulae (coefficients) — never by changing subscripts.
  5. Check every element again; leave elements that appear on their own (e.g. O₂) until last.
Balance: aluminium + oxygen → aluminium oxide.
Formulae: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃. Oxygen: 2 on left, 3 on right → LCM 6 → 3O₂ and 2Al₂O₃. Now Al: 4 on right → 4Al.
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃   (Check: Al 4 = 4, O 6 = 6 ✓)

Water produced in a reaction is H₂O(l), not (aq). "Aqueous" means dissolved in water, so water itself can never be (aq). A molten ionic compound is (l).

Half equations (HT) (C3.1b)

A half equation shows what happens to one species in a reaction, with electrons (e⁻) shown explicitly. They are used for redox reactions and electrolysis (see C3.3 and C3.4). Both atoms and charge must balance.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻     (loss of electrons: oxidation)
Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻     (gain of electrons: reduction)
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu     2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻

To check the charge: in Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ the left side has total charge 2− (two electrons) and the right side has 2 × (1−) = 2−. ✓

Ionic equations (HT) (C3.1e)

In reactions in solution, some ions do not change — they are still dissolved, unchanged, at the end. These are spectator ions. An ionic equation leaves them out and shows only the particles that react.

  1. Write the balanced full equation with state symbols.
  2. Split every (aq) ionic compound into its ions. Do not split solids, liquids, gases or covalent molecules.
  3. Cross out ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
  4. Check atoms and charges balance.
Silver nitrate solution reacts with sodium chloride solution to form a white precipitate.

The mole and the Avogadro constant (HT) (C3.1g)

The Avogadro constant is the number of particles in one mole of a substance: 6.02 × 10²³ per mole.
One mole of a substance is the amount that contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons — you must say which). Equivalently (classical definition), it is the amount containing as many particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. OCR accepts either definition.

The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its relative formula mass (Mᵣ) — or relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) for an element made of atoms. So 1 mol of carbon atoms has mass 12 g, and 1 mol of water molecules has mass 18 g. Both contain 6.02 × 10²³ particles.

Number of particles = amount (mol) × 6.02 × 10²³
Mass of one particle (g) = Mᵣ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³
(a) Mass of one carbon atom: 12 ÷ (6.02 × 10²³) = 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g.
(b) Mass of one water molecule (Mᵣ = 18): 18 ÷ (6.02 × 10²³) = 2.99 × 10⁻²³ g.
(c) Number of molecules in 0.25 mol of CO₂: 0.25 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.51 × 10²³ molecules. Each molecule has 3 atoms, so this is 4.52 × 10²³ atoms.
Use your calculator's ×10ˣ (EXP) key for standard form. Give answers in standard form to 3 significant figures unless the question says otherwise — the data (6.02) is given to 3 s.f.

Mass and amount in moles (HT) (C3.1h)

Relative formula mass Mᵣ is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in the formula. For example Mᵣ(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100, and Mᵣ(Ca(OH)₂) = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74.

amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ Mᵣ    n = m ÷ Mᵣ
mass (g) = amount (mol) × Mᵣ
(a) How many moles in 22 g of carbon dioxide? Mᵣ(CO₂) = 12 + 2 × 16 = 44. n = 22 ÷ 44 = 0.50 mol.
(b) What is the mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate? m = 0.25 × 100 = 25 g.
(c) How many molecules in 9.0 g of water? n = 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.50 mol → 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.

Conservation of mass (C3.1i)

The law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, no atoms are lost or made, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
12.0 g of magnesium burns completely to form 20.0 g of magnesium oxide. What mass of oxygen reacted?
Mass of oxygen = 20.0 − 12.0 = 8.0 g.

Observed mass changes in non-enclosed systems (C3.1j)

Sometimes the mass on a balance appears to change during a reaction. Mass is still conserved — the change happens because the system is open (non-enclosed), so gas particles can enter or leave.

Observation (open container)ExampleParticle explanation
Mass increases2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s); rusting of ironA gas from the air is a reactant. Its atoms become part of the solid product, but the gas was never weighed at the start.
Mass decreasesCaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g); acid + carbonate; acid + metal (H₂)A gas is a product. Its particles spread out and escape from the container, so they are not weighed at the end.
No changePrecipitation: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)No gases are involved, so all particles stay in the container.
If asked "why did the mass go down?", do not say "mass was lost" or "the substance disappeared". Say "a gas (named) was produced and escaped from the open flask, so the mass of the gas was not recorded". In a sealed container the total mass is always constant.

Deducing stoichiometry from reacting masses (HT) (C3.1k)

The stoichiometry of a reaction is the ratio of the numbers of moles of each substance in the balanced equation. You can work it out from experimental masses:

  1. Convert the mass of each reactant and product into moles (n = m ÷ Mᵣ).
  2. Divide every value by the smallest number of moles.
  3. If necessary, multiply up to get whole numbers. These are the coefficients in the balanced equation.
5.60 g of iron reacts exactly with 10.65 g of chlorine, Cl₂, to form 16.25 g of iron chloride, FeCl₃. Deduce the balanced equation. (Aᵣ: Fe = 56, Cl = 35.5)
n(Fe) = 5.60 ÷ 56 = 0.100 mol  ·  n(Cl₂) = 10.65 ÷ 71 = 0.150 mol  ·  n(FeCl₃) = 16.25 ÷ 162.5 = 0.100 mol
Ratio 0.100 : 0.150 : 0.100 = 1 : 1.5 : 1 = 2 : 3 : 2  →  2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃
(Check conservation of mass: 5.60 + 10.65 = 16.25 g ✓)

Limiting reactants (HT) (C3.1k)

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely used up. It limits (decides) the amount of product formed. Any other reactant is in excess — some of it is left over at the end.
4.0 g of hydrogen is mixed with 48.0 g of oxygen and ignited: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Which is limiting, and what mass of water forms?
n(H₂) = 4.0 ÷ 2 = 2.0 mol  ·  n(O₂) = 48.0 ÷ 32 = 1.5 mol.
2.0 mol H₂ needs 1.0 mol O₂ (ratio 2 : 1). We have 1.5 mol, so O₂ is in excess (0.5 mol, 16 g, left over) and H₂ is limiting.
n(H₂O) = n(H₂) = 2.0 mol → mass = 2.0 × 18 = 36 g.

Calculating reacting masses from equations (HT) (C3.1l)

mass of A → moles of A (÷ Mᵣ) → moles of B (use the ratio in the equation) → mass of B (× Mᵣ)
What mass of magnesium oxide forms when 6.0 g of magnesium burns? 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Aᵣ Mg = 24, O = 16)
n(Mg) = 6.0 ÷ 24 = 0.25 mol. Ratio Mg : MgO = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1, so n(MgO) = 0.25 mol.
Mᵣ(MgO) = 40 → mass = 0.25 × 40 = 10 g.
What mass of calcium oxide can be made by heating 1.0 tonne of calcium carbonate? CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
Ratio 1 : 1 by moles. Mᵣ(CaCO₃) = 100, Mᵣ(CaO) = 56. So 100 g CaCO₃ → 56 g CaO, and 1.0 tonne → 0.56 tonnes (560 kg). Using the same mass units on both sides saves converting to grams.

C3.2 Energetics

Exothermic reaction: energy is transferred to the surroundings — the temperature of the surroundings rises.
Endothermic reaction: energy is taken in from the surroundings — the temperature of the surroundings falls.

Energy is never "used up" or "lost" — it is transferred between the chemicals (the system) and the surroundings (the solvent, container, air, your hand). The thermometer measures the surroundings.

Exothermic or endothermic? (C3.2a)

Exothermic (temperature rises)Endothermic (temperature falls)
Combustion of fuels (e.g. methane, candle wax)Thermal decomposition (e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)
Neutralisation (acid + alkali)Citric acid + sodium hydrogencarbonate
Metals with acids; displacement reactionsPhotosynthesis
Respiration; oxidation (e.g. hand warmers)Electrolysis (needs an energy supply)
A burning candle is exothermic even though you need a match to light it — the match only supplies the activation energy. Overall, far more energy is given out than taken in.

PAG C8 link: measuring temperature changes

The temperature change of a reaction in solution is measured in an insulated polystyrene cup (OCR lists "Measuring the temperature change in reactions" under PAG C8).

  1. Measure a known volume (e.g. 25 cm³) of one solution into the cup with a measuring cylinder; record the starting temperature.
  2. Add a known volume/mass of the other reactant, put on the lid, stir.
  3. Record the highest (exothermic) or lowest (endothermic) temperature reached.
  4. Repeat and calculate a mean. Change one variable (e.g. mass of metal, concentration) to investigate its effect.

Reaction profiles and activation energy (C3.2b, C3.2c)

Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur — the energy colliding particles must have to break bonds and start the reaction. On a reaction profile it is the height from the reactants to the top of the "hump".
  • Label: reactants, products, activation energy (arrow from reactants up to the peak) and energy change (arrow from reactants level to products level).
  • Exothermic: products are lower — the overall energy change is negative (ΔH < 0).
  • Endothermic: products are higher — the overall energy change is positive (ΔH > 0).
  • A catalyst lowers the activation energy (a lower hump) but does not change the energy of reactants or products (see C5).

Bond breaking and bond making

During a reaction, bonds in the reactants are broken and new bonds in the products are made:

  • Breaking bonds is endothermic — energy must be taken in.
  • Making bonds is exothermic — energy is given out.

If more energy is released making the new bonds than is needed to break the old ones, the reaction is exothermic overall. If more energy is needed to break bonds than is released making them, it is endothermic.

A very common wrong answer is "energy is released when bonds break". It is the other way round: breaking takes in, making gives out.

C3.3 Types of Chemical Reactions

Oxidation and reduction — oxygen (C3.3a)

Oxidation is the gain of oxygen. Reduction is the loss of oxygen. Reactions where both happen are called redox reactions.
An oxidising agent gives oxygen to another substance (and is itself reduced). A reducing agent removes oxygen from another substance (and is itself oxidised).

Oxidation and reduction — electrons (HT) (C3.3b)

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
The reducing agent donates electrons (it is oxidised); the oxidising agent accepts electrons (it is reduced).
In Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂, identify what is oxidised and what is reduced.
Ionic: Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂. Half equations: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ (gain → chlorine reduced, oxidising agent); 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ (loss → iodide ions oxidised, reducing agent).

Acids and alkalis (C3.3c)

Acids form hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), when they dissolve in water.
Alkalis are soluble bases; their solutions contain hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq).
A base is any substance that neutralises an acid (metal oxides, metal hydroxides, carbonates, ammonia). An alkali is a base that dissolves in water.

Common acids: hydrochloric HCl (→ H⁺ + Cl⁻, makes chlorides), sulfuric H₂SO₄ (→ 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻, sulfates), nitric HNO₃ (→ H⁺ + NO₃⁻, nitrates), ethanoic CH₃COOH (partially → H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻, ethanoates).

Common alkalis: sodium hydroxide NaOH (Na⁺ + OH⁻), potassium hydroxide KOH, calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)₂ (limewater), and ammonia solution NH₃(aq).

Neutralisation (C3.3d, C3.3e)

acid + alkali → salt + water     acid + base (metal oxide / hydroxide) → salt + water
HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
H₂SO₄(aq) + CuO(s) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
Ionic equation for every aqueous neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

The metal ion (or ammonium ion) in the salt comes from the base; the negative ion comes from the acid. All the other ions (e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻) are spectator ions, which is why every acid–alkali neutralisation can be generalised to hydrogen ions reacting with hydroxide ions to form water.

Acids with metals and carbonates (C3.3f)

acid + metal → salt + hydrogen     Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)
acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide     Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
  • Only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series react with dilute acids (Mg, Zn, Fe do; Cu, Ag, Au do not). Very reactive metals (Na, K) react dangerously.
  • Observations: fizzing (effervescence), solid disappearing, solution warming. Test: H₂ gives a squeaky pop; CO₂ turns limewater milky.
Predict the products and write a balanced equation for magnesium carbonate + nitric acid.

PAG C7 link: production of a pure, dry salt

An insoluble base (metal oxide or carbonate) is added in excess to an acid. Example: copper(II) oxide + sulfuric acid → copper(II) sulfate + water.

Why use excess base? To make sure all the acid is used up, so the salt is not contaminated with acid; the unreacted solid is easy to remove by filtration. Why not evaporate to dryness? Heating strongly can decompose hydrated salts and you get a powder, not crystals.

Dilute vs concentrated, strong vs weak (HT) (C3.3g)

Concentration = the amount of acid (in moles, or mass) per unit volume of solution (mol/dm³ or g/dm³). Concentrated: a large amount of acid in a given volume. Dilute: a small amount of acid in a given volume.
Strength = the degree of ionisation. A strong acid is completely (fully) ionised in water. A weak acid is only partially ionised.
Strong: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)    (also HNO₃, H₂SO₄)
Weak: CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)    (also citric acid, carbonic acid)
concentration (mol/dm³) = amount (mol) ÷ volume (dm³)   ·   1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
"Weak" does not mean "dilute". A concentrated solution of a weak acid can have a lower pH than a very dilute strong acid. At the same concentration, a strong acid has a higher H⁺ concentration and so a lower pH than a weak acid, and reacts faster with metals and carbonates.

Measuring acidity: pH (C3.3h)

The pH scale (0–14) measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. pH < 7 is acidic, pH 7 is neutral, pH > 7 is alkaline. The further from 7, the more acidic or alkaline.

pH and hydrogen ion concentration (HT) (C3.3i, C3.3j)

  • The higher the concentration of H⁺ ions, the lower the pH (more acidic).
  • In a neutral solution the concentrations of H⁺ and OH⁻ are equal (pH 7).
  • In an alkaline solution the H⁺ concentration is very low (OH⁻ is in excess), so the pH is above 7.
  • The scale is logarithmic: each time the H⁺ concentration increases by a factor of 10, the pH decreases by 1.

(a) Solution X has pH 2; solution Y has pH 5. How many times greater is [H⁺] in X? Difference = 3 pH units → 10³ = 1000 times.
(b) A strong acid of pH 1 is diluted by a factor of 10 (10 cm³ made up to 100 cm³). [H⁺] falls by a factor of 10, so the new pH = 2. Diluting 100-fold would give pH 3.

(HT) pH titration curves. If a pH probe records pH while alkali is added from a burette to an acid, the pH rises slowly at first, then very steeply near the end point (when the acid is exactly neutralised), then levels off.

C3.4 Electrolysis

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by passing an electric current through it. The liquid or solution is the electrolyte.
Cathode = negative electrode  ·  Anode = positive electrode
Cations = positive ions, attracted to the cathode  ·  Anions = negative ions, attracted to the anode

Solid ionic compounds do not conduct: the ions are held in a fixed lattice and cannot move. When molten or dissolved, the ions are free to move and carry the charge. In the electrolyte the current is carried by moving ions, not electrons; electrons only flow in the external wires.

Memory aid: PANIC — Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode. Cations go to the cathode.

Products at the electrodes with inert electrodes (C3.4a)

  • Metals (or hydrogen) are formed at the cathode — positive metal ions (or H⁺) gain electrons.
  • Non-metals are formed at the anode — negative non-metal ions lose electrons.
  • Inert electrodes (graphite or platinum) do not react themselves.

Electrolysis of molten binary ionic compounds (C3.4b)

A binary compound contains just two elements. When molten, there are only two kinds of ion, so the products are simply the metal at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.

Electrolysis of aqueous solutions: competing reactions (C3.4c)

In an aqueous solution there are extra ions from water: a few water molecules ionise, H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. So two cations compete at the cathode and two anions compete at the anode. Only one of each is discharged.

PAG C2: electrolysis of aqueous solutions

Set up the cell above with graphite electrodes and a low-voltage d.c. supply (4–6 V); collect gases in inverted test tubes and test them (H₂: squeaky pop; O₂: relights a glowing splint; Cl₂: bleaches damp litmus). Observe any coating on the cathode. Chlorine is toxic — use a well-ventilated room, short run times and eye protection.

Equations at the electrodes (C3.4d)

At the cathode, positive ions gain electrons (reduction). At the anode, negative ions lose electrons (oxidation). Overall, the electrolysis of NaCl(aq) can be summarised as:

2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) + Cl₂(g)

Inert and non-inert electrodes (C3.4e)

With inert electrodes (graphite, platinum) the electrodes just carry the current. With non-inert (active) electrodes, the electrode itself takes part. The key example is copper(II) sulfate solution with copper electrodes:

Compare with inert electrodes in CuSO₄(aq): copper is still deposited at the cathode, but oxygen bubbles off at the anode, the blue colour fades and the solution becomes acidic. With copper electrodes there is no gas, the colour stays the same, and the cathode gain equals the anode loss. Copper electrodes are used to purify copper, and the same set-up (object to be coated as the cathode) is used for electroplating.
