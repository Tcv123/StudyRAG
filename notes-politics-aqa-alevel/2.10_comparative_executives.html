2.10 Comparative — The Executives

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 2 (Government and Politics of the USA and Comparative Politics)

Comparing the UK Prime Minister with the US President is the most common comparative essay on Paper 2 — and the most commonly answered badly, because students try to decide which is "more powerful" overall. The correct answer is that each is more powerful in different dimensions, and the dimensions run in opposite directions.

2. Structural analysis

Why a President struggles to legislate

  • Separation of powers — separately elected, with no guaranteed legislative majority; divided government has been the norm for much of the post-war period.
  • Multiple veto points — the House, the Senate, the filibuster's effective 60-vote threshold, and judicial review. The President controls none directly.
  • Weak party discipline — even under unified government, legislators nominated in primaries and funding their own campaigns may defy the President without penalty. Obama's difficulties passing the ACA despite Democratic control of both chambers illustrate this.

Why a Prime Minister legislates almost automatically

Fusion of powers. The PM is Prime Minister because they command a Commons majority. With the whips, the payroll vote and control of the timetable, with the Lords able only to delay for a year, and with no court able to strike the resulting statute down, legislation is close to guaranteed.

The veto is evidence of presidential weakness, not strength — a counter-intuitive point that marks out a strong answer. A PM needs no veto because a PM who opposes a bill simply prevents it from being introduced. The President needs one precisely because Congress can legislate without them.

3. The reversal: security of tenure

Everything above suggests the PM is more powerful. Tenure reverses it completely.

  • A President cannot be removed for unpopularity, for losing legislative votes, or by their party. Only impeachment and conviction (two-thirds of the Senate) — or, for incapacity, the Twenty-Fifth Amendment (the Vice-President plus a cabinet majority, sustained by two-thirds of both chambers) — can end the term early, and neither has ever removed a President. They can therefore pursue unpopular policies and outlast poor polling.
  • A Prime Minister holds office only while their party permits it, and both main parties can remove a leader at any time. Thatcher (1990), May (2019), Johnson (2022) and Truss (2022) all left office without losing a general election.
This is the single most important comparative fact in the topic, and it has no US parallel whatsoever. It also explains a behavioural difference: a weakened PM is removed, whereas a weakened President simply governs by executive order until the term expires. That is why UK premierships end abruptly and failing presidencies limp on.

4. Rational analysis

  • Why US legislators defy their own President. They win nomination in primaries, raise their own money, and cannot be deselected by the national party. The career risk is a primary challenge from the ideological flank, not presidential displeasure — so supporting an unpopular President can cost them their seat while defying one costs nothing.
  • Why UK MPs rarely rebel. They depend on the party for selection, for the label that delivers most of their vote, and for promotion, which the whips influence. Rebellion risks deselection and ends ministerial prospects. Fusion raises the stakes further: a major rebellion can bring down the government and trigger an election that costs them their seat.
  • Why PMs appoint rivals to cabinet. Dangerous figures are safer inside the government, bound by collective responsibility, than outside it — a rational calculation that is itself evidence of constraint operating before any appointment is made.

5. Cultural analysis

  • Personalisation of the presidency — combining head of state and head of government gives the office a symbolic national role drawing on American patriotic tradition, which no PM commands. The President embodies the nation in a way the UK deliberately separates into the monarchy.
  • American individualism — a tradition of suspicion towards centralised authority supports a politics in which legislators build personal followings and are rewarded for independence.
  • British party government — a parliamentary tradition in which voters choose a programme and a party rather than an individual, supporting disciplined collective government.

6. Similarities

  • Both are head of government, directing national policy, representing the country abroad and commanding the armed forces.
  • Both have grown more powerful over the twentieth century — the expansion of the state, the demands of war and crisis, and media focus on a single national leader.
  • Both can act unilaterally without new legislation — executive orders and the royal prerogative are functionally comparable.
  • Both have seen those unilateral powers constrained by the courts — Miller I and Miller II; Youngstown (1952), DHS v Regents (2020, blocking the DACA rescission) and Biden v Nebraska (2023, striking down student-loan cancellation). The travel ban is a counter-example: the Supreme Court upheld it in Trump v Hawaii (2018).
  • Both depend on large personal staff organisations — EXOP and the PM's Office — which have grown substantially.
  • Both face their weakest checks in foreign and security policy.

7. Are both "imperial"?

Imperial presidency (Schlesinger)Presidentialisation of the premiership (Foley)
War without declarations since 1942; executive orders and agreements; executive privilege; surveillancePersonalised mandates and leaders' debates; "sofa government"; growth of the centre; spatial leadership
Counter: routine legislative failure, midterm losses, reversed orders, impeachmentsCounter: tenure is conditional; no veto; no personal mandate; cabinet can end a premiership
The strongest unified conclusion: both executives appear imperial chiefly in foreign and security policy, where legislative checks are weakest in each system, and both face real constraints their critics understate domestically. But the constraints differ in kind — the President is constrained by other institutions, the PM by their own party.

Essential examples

  • Thatcher 1990, May 2019, Johnson 2022, Truss 2022 — four PMs removed without losing an election; no US parallel.
  • ACA repeal failure 2017 — a President defeated under unified government.
  • May's 230-vote defeat, 2019 — a PM weaker than most presidents.
  • DACA / Iran deal / Paris accord — executive action reversed by successors.
  • Miller I and II vs Youngstown — courts constraining unilateral executive power in both systems.
  • 2013 Syria vote — the Commons blocking military action; the War Powers Resolution rarely achieving the same.
  • Trump 2025– — unified Republican control; over 200 executive orders in 2025; Trump v United States (2024) immunity; Trump v CASA (2025) curbing nationwide injunctions against executive action. But the Court struck down his emergency-power tariffs in Learning Resources v Trump (2026), 6–3.
  • Starmer 2025 — despite a 411-seat majority, a backbench rebellion forced the government to gut its welfare bill (July 2025): even a landslide PM is constrained by party.
