1.7 Elections and Referendums

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 1 (Government and Politics of the UK)

The UK uses four different electoral systems across its various elections, which makes it an unusually good natural experiment: the same electorate produces two-party politics under FPTP and multi-party politics under proportional systems. That contrast is the single most useful piece of evidence in this topic.

1. First Past the Post (Westminster)

How it works

  • 650 single-member constituencies; one vote each, marked with an X.
  • The candidate with the most votes wins — a plurality, not necessarily a majority.
  • No second round, no transfers.
  • The party with most seats normally forms the government.

Strong, decisive government — usually manufactures a single-party majority, so a manifesto can be implemented without post-election bargainingDisproportional — seat share diverges sharply from vote share; governments take office on 35–44 per cent of the vote

Simple — easily understood; results known quicklySafe seats — most results are effectively predetermined, depressing turnout and concentrating campaigning on marginals
Constituency link — one identifiable MP accountable for each areaTactical voting — voters pushed away from genuine first preferences
Excludes extremists with dispersed supportBarrier to new parties — entrenches the two main parties
The 2015 general election is the definitive illustration and should appear in any FPTP answer. UKIP won around 3.9 million votes and one seat; the SNP won around 1.5 million votes and 56 seats. The difference is not how many votes but where they were: FPTP rewards geographically concentrated support and penalises support spread evenly.

2. The other systems

Additional Member System (AMS) — Scottish Parliament, Senedd

  • Hybrid: two votes — one for a constituency member elected by FPTP, one for a regional party list.
  • List seats are allocated by a corrective formula accounting for constituency seats already won, so under-represented parties receive proportionately more.
  • Strength: retains a single identifiable constituency member while producing broadly proportional overall results.
  • Weakness: creates two classes of representative; coalition or minority government is the norm.

Single Transferable Vote (STV) — Northern Ireland Assembly and local elections, Scottish local elections

  • Multi-member constituencies; voters rank candidates in order of preference.
  • Candidates reaching a quota are elected; surpluses and eliminated candidates' votes transfer.
  • Strength: highly proportional; very few wasted votes; voters can choose between candidates of the same party. Chosen for Northern Ireland precisely to guarantee both communities representation.
  • Weakness: large multi-member constituencies dilute the link to a single representative; counting is complex and slow.

Supplementary Vote (SV)

  • Voters mark a first and second preference. If no candidate wins an absolute majority, all but the top two are eliminated and second preferences transferred.
  • Formerly used for the Mayor of London, other directly elected mayors and Police and Crime Commissioners — changed to FPTP by the Elections Act 2022.
  • Strength: the winner has broader support than a bare plurality. Weakness: not proportional; second preferences for eliminated candidates are discarded.

3. Comparing outcomes

The key analytical point: electoral systems shape party systems rather than merely reflecting them.

  • Westminster under FPTP: two-party dominance, single-party majority government.
  • Scotland and Wales under AMS: multi-party systems, coalition and minority governments, representation for Greens and others.
  • Northern Ireland under STV: representation for both communities and for smaller parties.

The same voters behave differently under different rules — splitting tickets and supporting smaller parties where the system rewards it. That is strong evidence against the argument that FPTP simply reflects an inherently two-party electorate.

4. The electoral reform debate

For reformAgainst reform

The 2011 AV referendum

  • Held under the coalition agreement. Result: roughly 68 per cent No, on a turnout of around 42 per cent.
  • AV is not a proportional system, so reformers were divided and campaigned unenthusiastically.
  • Effect: removed electoral reform from the agenda for a generation.
The most persuasive explanation for why reform has stalled is rational, not philosophical: the parties with the power to legislate are precisely those FPTP advantages. Neither main party has an incentive to abolish the system that delivers it majorities on a minority of the vote.

5. Referendums

Referendum — a vote in which the electorate accepts or rejects a particular proposal, usually on a single constitutional or sovereignty question. In the UK the result is advisory in law, because Parliament is sovereign — but politically binding in practice.

Arguments for

  • Direct legitimacy for constitutional change — changes to the framework of politics arguably require explicit popular consent, particularly in an uncodified constitution where nothing else prevents a government altering it by ordinary legislation.
  • High engagement — 2014 and 2016 both far exceeded contemporaneous general election turnout.
  • Resolves cross-cutting issues — Europe divided both main parties for decades, so no general election could deliver a clear verdict on it.

Arguments against

  • Binary framing — the 2016 vote asked whether to leave without specifying what leaving meant, so it produced a majority for a direction but not for any outcome. Parliament then spent years unable to agree on implementation.
  • Clash with parliamentary sovereignty — a legally advisory vote acquired binding political authority, while a majority of MPs had favoured Remain. The constitution offers no mechanism for resolving that conflict; the result was prorogation, litigation and an early general election.
  • No threshold — a narrow majority on a single day can deliver irreversible constitutional change.
  • Campaign effects — funding, media framing and differential turnout can distort outcomes.
  • They can entrench division — neither 2014 nor 2016 settled the question it asked.
  • Motive — referendums have often been called to manage internal party division rather than because the constitutional moment demanded it. Both 1975 and 2016 arose substantially from splits within the governing party.

6. Campaigns and voting behaviour

  • Manifestos and the mandate — a winning party claims authority to implement its programme; this underpins the Salisbury Convention.
  • Regulation — the Electoral Commission enforces statutory spending limits; paid political advertising on television is prohibited, which constrains the role of money far more than in the USA.
  • Dealignment — the weakening of habitual class-based and partisan loyalties since the 1970s, making campaigns and leaders more decisive.
  • Valence — voters increasingly judge parties on perceived competence (especially economic) and leadership rather than on ideological position.

Essential examples

  • 2015 general election — UKIP 3.9m votes/1 seat vs SNP 1.5m votes/56 seats.
  • 2011 AV referendum — ~68% No on ~42% turnout; reform off the agenda.
  • 2014 Scottish referendum — ~85% turnout; engagement responds to stakes.
  • 2016 EU referendum — 52/48; advisory in law, binding in practice.
  • 2010 coalition — FPTP failing to deliver a majority; post-election bargaining.
  • Elections Act 2022 — mayors and PCCs moved from SV to FPTP; voter ID introduced.
