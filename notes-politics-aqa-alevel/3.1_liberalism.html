3.1 Liberalism

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 3 (Political Ideas)

Liberalism is the dominant ideology of the modern Western world — so pervasive that its assumptions are often mistaken for common sense. Its central value is individual freedom, and its internal history is the story of a single disagreement: whether freedom is best secured by restraining the state or by using it.

Specification requirement. AQA names five key thinkers for liberalism: John Locke, Mary Wollstonecraft, John Stuart Mill, John Rawls and Betty Friedan. T.H. Green is not a named thinker, but he is useful context for modern liberalism and positive freedom.

1. Core ideas

Individualism

The individual is the central unit of moral and political value. Society exists to serve individuals, not the reverse, and is best understood as a collection of individuals rather than an organic whole. Two forms:

  • Egoistical individualism — individuals are self-interested and self-reliant; associated with classical liberalism.
  • Developmental individualism — individuals have potential to fulfil, which society should help them realise; associated with Mill and modern liberalism.

Freedom

The supreme political value — but contested:

  • Negative freedom — the absence of external constraint. A person is free to the extent that no one prevents them acting.
  • Positive freedom — the actual capacity to act and develop. Poverty, ignorance and ill health are constraints even where no one interferes.

Rationalism

Human beings are capable of reason, so they can identify their own interests better than any authority can. This is the premise from which almost everything else follows: consent, rights, toleration, free expression and democracy all depend on it. It is also liberalism's most vulnerable claim.

Equality and social justice

  • Foundational equality — all individuals are born with equal moral worth and equal natural rights.
  • Formal (legal) equality — equality before the law; no legal privilege.
  • Equality of opportunity — a fair starting point, with unequal outcomes accepted as legitimate where they reflect effort and talent. Liberals reject equality of outcome.

The state

Necessary but dangerous. Locke argued that in the state of nature rights exist but are insecure, so a state is needed to adjudicate impartially — yet because it holds coercive power it is also the chief threat to liberty. Hence constitutionalism: limited government, the rule of law, separation of powers, checks and balances, and government by consent.

Liberal democracy — with reservations

Liberals support democracy as most compatible with consent and accountability, but are wary of unconstrained majority rule. Mill's fear of the tyranny of the majority explains the liberal insistence that democracy be constrained by protections for minorities and individual rights.

2. Key thinkers

John Locke (1632–1704) — Two Treatises of Government (1689)

  • Natural rights — to life, liberty and property, existing prior to and independently of government.
  • State of nature — unlike Hobbes's, it is tolerable but inconvenient: rights exist but there is no impartial judge to resolve disputes.
  • Social contract and consent — government is legitimate only where it rests on the consent of the governed.
  • Fiduciary (trust) power — government holds authority in trust for the people. If the trust is broken, they may withdraw it — a right of rebellion.
  • Limited government — with separated powers and the rule of law.

Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797) — A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792)

  • Women are rational — the apparent inferiority of women results from denied education, not from nature.
  • Formal equality — if rights derive from rationality and women are equally rational, denying them rights is inconsistent with liberalism's own foundations.
  • Education as the route to independence and genuine citizenship.
  • The "revolution controversy" — she defended the French Revolution's principles against Burke, arguing that reason and rights, not inherited tradition, should found political authority.
Wollstonecraft demonstrates liberalism's radical internal logic. She did not attack liberalism — she convicted it of inconsistency with itself. The same argument was later extended to other excluded groups, which is why liberalism has been an engine of extension despite starting from a narrow base.

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) — On Liberty (1859)

  • The harm principle — the only legitimate ground for restricting liberty is to prevent harm to others. A person's own good is never sufficient warrant, which rules out state paternalism and the legal enforcement of morality.
  • Freedom of expression — even a false opinion is valuable, because truth held without being contested becomes dead dogma.
  • Tyranny of the majority — democratic majorities and social opinion can oppress dissenters as effectively as any despot.
  • Developmental individualism — human beings are progressive beings capable of growth, and individuality should be cultivated.
  • The bridge — his defence of negative freedom is classical; his developmental strand pointed towards modern liberalism.

T.H. Green (1836–1882) — additional (non-specified) thinker

  • Positive freedom — freedom is the actual capacity for self-development, not merely the absence of interference.
  • The enabling state — because poverty, ignorance and ill health are themselves constraints, state action to remove them expands liberty rather than restricting it.
  • Social liberalism — individuals are socially embedded and have obligations to one another; a modified view of the atomised individual.

John Rawls (1921–2002) — A Theory of Justice (1971)

  • Justice as fairness — principles of justice are those that would be chosen under fair conditions.
  • The original position and veil of ignorance — choosers do not know their own class, talents, gender, race or conception of the good.
  • First principle: equal basic liberties for all.
  • The difference principle: social and economic inequalities are justified only where they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged.
  • Reasoning: rational self-interested choosers behind the veil would not risk being at the bottom of a harshly unequal society.

Betty Friedan (1921–2006) — The Feminine Mystique (1963)

  • Cultural conditioning — women were constrained not primarily by law but by socialisation persuading them that domesticity should fulfil them: "the problem that has no name".
  • Equal rights and opportunity — access to education, careers and public life on the same terms as men; she co-founded the National Organization for Women.
  • Liberal, not radical — she sought women's inclusion in existing structures rather than their transformation, which is precisely why later feminists criticised her.

Neo-liberalism is a twentieth-century revival of classical liberal economics — free markets, deregulation, privatisation, a minimal state — which fed into the New Right. Note that it belongs to the classical lineage, not a development of modern liberalism.

The key analytical point: classical and modern liberals share the same ultimate value and reach opposite conclusions. Both prioritise individual freedom; they disagree about what freedom requires. That is why liberalism contains both the welfare state and its fiercest critics, and why "is liberalism coherent?" is a live question.

The Berlin objection

Isaiah Berlin warned that positive freedom is dangerous: it permits others to define what a person's "real" interests are and to coerce them accordingly, in the name of their own liberty. Nozick attacks redistribution on a different ground: self-ownership and his entitlement theory, under which redistributive taxation treats some people's labour as a resource for others. Berlin's warning means the concept that rescued liberalism from irrelevance also opened it to the charge of licensing the very paternalism Mill rejected.

5. Is liberalism coherent?

The charge: classical and modern liberalism disagree about the state, the economy and equality so fundamentally that they cannot be one ideology.

The reply: all liberals share individualism, rationalism, foundational equality, the primacy of freedom, constitutional and limited government, and toleration. The disagreement is about means, and follows logically from a genuine ambiguity in the concept of freedom itself. Compared with conservatism — which contains both Burke's inherited obligation and Rand's rational egoism — liberalism is the more internally consistent tradition.
