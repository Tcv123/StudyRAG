2.8 Civil Rights

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 2 (Government and Politics of the USA and Comparative Politics)

US civil rights have advanced through three routes — constitutional amendment, judicial interpretation and legislation backed by mass mobilisation — and each has a different security profile. Understanding which route a right rests on tells you how vulnerable it is, and that is the analytical spine of this topic.

1. Constitutional foundations

The Reconstruction amendments (13th–15th) lay substantially dormant for nearly a century. Text alone achieves nothing without institutional will: Plessy (1896) upheld segregation under the very Fourteenth Amendment that Brown (1954) later used to strike it down. The words did not change — the Court did.

2. Segregation and its dismantling

  • Plessy v Ferguson (1896) — upheld "separate but equal", legitimising Jim Crow segregation across the South.
  • Jim Crow — state and local laws enforcing segregation, backed by literacy tests, poll taxes, grandfather clauses and violence to suppress black voting.
  • Brown v Board of Education (1954) — unanimous ruling that segregated public schools violate equal protection, because separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. Overturned Plessy.
  • Implementation was slow and resisted — a decade of further litigation, federal enforcement and federal troops at Little Rock. A ruling is not self-executing.

3. The civil rights movement

Methods and why the combination mattered

MethodWhat it achievedWhat it could not
Litigation (NAACP Legal Defense Fund)Established that segregation was unconstitutional — a nationwide ruling no legislature of the period would have deliveredCould not compel implementation, nor reach private discrimination
Non-violent direct action (Montgomery bus boycott, sit-ins, freedom rides, the 1963 March on Washington)Generated the political pressure litigation could not — televised images of peaceful protesters met with violence shifted northern opinion decisivelyCould not itself change the law
LegislationProvided enforcement machinery and reached private actors in employment, housing and public accommodationRequired the political conditions the movement had to create first

The landmark statutes

  • Civil Rights Act 1964 — prohibited discrimination on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin in employment, education and public accommodation, with federal enforcement.
  • Voting Rights Act 1965 — outlawed literacy tests and similar devices, and introduced pre-clearance: designated jurisdictions with a history of discrimination had to obtain federal approval before changing voting rules.
  • Fair Housing Act 1968 — discrimination in housing.

4. Voting rights today

Shelby County v Holder (2013)

The Court struck down section 4(b) of the Voting Rights Act — the formula determining which jurisdictions were subject to pre-clearance — holding it was based on decades-old data and no longer reflected current conditions.

Effect: pre-clearance survived in principle but became inoperative, since no valid coverage formula existed. Congress has not enacted a replacement.

Consequence: several formerly covered states introduced stricter voter ID requirements, reduced early voting, closed polling places and purged rolls. These now take effect immediately and can only be challenged afterwards through litigation — slower, more expensive, and placing the burden on challengers rather than on the jurisdiction.

The debate here is precise and worth reproducing: critics say the ruling removed the most effective enforcement mechanism in US civil rights law on the reasoning that it had worked. Defenders say the coverage formula genuinely was outdated and Congress could have updated it — so the failure is legislative, not judicial. Both are defensible; saying which you find stronger, and why, is the evaluative move.

Rucho v Common Cause (2019)

Held that partisan gerrymandering claims are non-justiciable in federal courts, leaving remedy to state constitutions and ballot initiatives.

6. Rights beyond race

  • LGBT rights — Obergefell v Hodges (2015) established a constitutional right to same-sex marriage; Bostock v Clayton County (2020) held that Title VII's prohibition on sex discrimination in employment covers sexual orientation and gender identity.
  • Abortion — Roe v Wade (1973) recognised a constitutional right; Dobbs v Jackson (2022) overturned it, returning regulation to the states and producing sharply divergent state laws.
  • Disability — the Americans with Disabilities Act 1990.
  • Black Lives Matter — decentralised, social-media-driven mobilisation that shifted the national agenda on policing without a formal hierarchy or single leadership.

7. The security of rights — the central evaluation

RouteStrengthVulnerability
AmendmentMost secure — beyond legislative reversal and beyond judicial reinterpretation awayPractically unattainable today; the Reconstruction amendments lay dormant for a century without enforcement
Judicial interpretationFastest — nationwide effect with no legislative majority needed, invaluable where elected branches are hostileWhat one Court grants another can withdraw — Dobbs reversed a fifty-year precedent after three appointments
LegislationReaches private discrimination; supplies enforcement machinery and funding; carries democratic legitimacyCan be weakened without repeal — Shelby County disabled the VRA's key mechanism and Congress has not restored it
For a 25-mark question on whether rights are adequately protected, the strongest structure is: legally entrenched and judicially enforceable (strength) — but defined by interpretation, so dependent on nine appointments (weakness) — and varying by state under federalism (weakness) — while formal equality has not delivered substantive equality in wealth, incarceration and policing outcomes (the deepest problem).

Essential examples

  • Plessy (1896) → Brown (1954) — the same amendment, opposite readings.
  • Little Rock 1957 — federal troops enforcing Brown; rulings need enforcement.
  • Civil Rights Act 1964 / Voting Rights Act 1965 — legislation reaching what rulings could not.
  • Shelby County (2013) — pre-clearance disabled; new state restrictions followed.
  • Obergefell (2015) and Bostock (2020) — rights still being extended.
  • Dobbs (2022) — a right withdrawn; federalism producing divergent state regimes.
  • SFFA v Harvard (2023) — affirmative action in admissions ended.
