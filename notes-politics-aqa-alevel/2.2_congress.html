2.2 Congress

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 2 (Government and Politics of the USA and Comparative Politics)

Congress is bicameral: the House of Representatives (435 voting members) and the Senate (100). Unlike the UK Parliament it is genuinely independent of the executive — it writes its own bills, sets its own agenda and routinely rejects presidential proposals. The examinable question is whether that independence now produces effective legislating or mostly obstruction.

The Speaker of the House is a partisan figure who leads the majority party and controls the floor agenda — the opposite of the scrupulously impartial UK Speaker. Getting this wrong is a common error in comparative questions.

3. Functions

  • Legislation — both chambers must pass an identical text.
  • Representation — of a district (House) or state (Senate), plus extensive constituency casework.
  • Oversight — committee hearings, investigations, subpoenas.
  • Power of the purse — all federal spending requires appropriation.
  • Confirmation and ratification (Senate).
  • Impeachment and proposing constitutional amendments.

4. The legislative process

  1. Introduction — only a member may introduce a bill, though the executive often drafts it.
  2. Committee stage — the crucial stage. Standing committees hold hearings, amend, and can kill a bill simply by not reporting it. Most bills die here.
  3. Floor action — in the House, the Rules Committee sets debate terms tightly; in the Senate, debate is open, which makes the filibuster possible.
  4. Conference committee — reconciles differing House and Senate versions into one text.
  5. Presidential action — sign, veto (overridable by two-thirds of both chambers), or take no action. If Congress adjourns within ten days and the President has not signed, the bill dies by pocket veto.

5. Committees

  • Standing committees — permanent, with jurisdiction over a policy area. Conduct detailed scrutiny with professional staff resources UK committees cannot match. Chairs are powerful gatekeepers.
  • Select committees — temporary, usually investigative (e.g. the January 6th committee).
  • Conference committees — reconcile the two chambers' versions.
  • Senate Judiciary Committee — conducts Supreme Court confirmation hearings.

6. The filibuster

Filibuster — use of the Senate's tradition of unlimited debate to delay or block a vote. Ending it requires a cloture motion, normally needing 60 of 100 senators.

Because 60 votes are rarely available, the filibuster functions as a de facto supermajority requirement for most legislation. It has been abolished for nominations — lower federal courts in 2013, Supreme Court nominees in 2017 — but retained for legislation. Governments therefore route what they can through budget reconciliation, which permits budget-related measures by simple majority but sharply restricts what can be included.

7. Incumbency and representation

Why incumbents almost always win

  • Name recognition, franked mail, staff resources and a record of constituency casework and federal funding secured.
  • Fundraising — donors prefer likely winners and want continued access, so challengers are typically heavily outspent.
  • Safe districts — drawn deliberately through gerrymandering, or produced by voters sorting geographically.

House re-election rates routinely exceed 90 per cent. Senate incumbents are less secure, since statewide electorates cannot be gerrymandered.

The consequence of safe districts is the crucial analytical link in this topic. Where the general election is a formality, the only real threat is a primary challenge from the ideological flank. Members are therefore rewarded for purity and punished for compromise — which is the principal institutional driver of polarisation, and connects this topic to 2.5 and 2.6.

Distortions in representation

  • Senate malapportionment — Wyoming and California have equal representation despite vastly different populations, so a minority of the national population can command a Senate majority.
  • Partisan gerrymandering — House seat shares can diverge sharply from statewide vote shares. Rucho v Common Cause (2019) held such claims non-justiciable in federal courts, leaving remedy to state constitutions and ballot initiatives.

8. Oversight of the executive

  • Committee investigations — compelled testimony, subpoenas, public hearings. Watergate and the January 6th committee are the landmark examples.
  • The power of the purse — refusing funds, attaching conditions, or declining to fund government at all (a shutdown). The most concrete lever, since it needs no executive cooperation.
  • Confirmation hearings — scrutiny of nominees and of the policies they will implement.
  • Impeachment — the ultimate sanction. Andrew Johnson (1868), Clinton (1998) and Trump (2019, 2021) were impeached by the House; none was convicted by the Senate, which requires two-thirds.
The decisive point about oversight: its vigour tracks partisan advantage, not constitutional duty. Under divided government committees investigate aggressively; under unified government the same powers go largely unused, because members have no incentive to damage their own administration. Effective oversight is therefore a function of election results rather than of institutional design.

Essential examples

  • Affordable Care Act 2010 — passed the Senate with exactly 60 votes to overcome a filibuster under unified government; amendments were then passed through reconciliation. The repeal attempt failed in the Senate in 2017.
  • Filibuster reform — abolished for lower-court nominations (2013) and Supreme Court nominations (2017).
  • Merrick Garland 2016 / Amy Coney Barrett 2020 — the Senate's confirmation power exercised on nakedly partisan grounds.
  • Government shutdowns — the power of the purse used as leverage.
  • Trump impeachments (2019, 2021) — House impeachment without Senate conviction; the two-thirds threshold in practice.
  • Rucho v Common Cause (2019) — partisan gerrymandering held non-justiciable federally.
