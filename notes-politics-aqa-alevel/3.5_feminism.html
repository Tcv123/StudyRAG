3.5 Feminism

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 3 (Political Ideas)

Feminism holds that women are systematically disadvantaged because of their sex, that this disadvantage is socially produced rather than natural, and that it should be ended. Its strands disagree sharply about the cause and the remedy — which is why "is feminism a single ideology?" is a standard exam question.

Optional ideology. Students study one of nationalism, feminism, multiculturalism, anarchism or ecologism.
AQA key thinkers for feminism: Charlotte Perkins Gilman, Simone de Beauvoir, Kate Millett, Sheila Rowbotham and bell hooks.

1. Core ideas

Sex and gender

The foundational analytical distinction.

  • Sex — biological difference between males and females; given.
  • Gender — the social roles, behaviours and expectations attached to those categories (masculinity, femininity); culturally constructed and varying across societies and over time.

Why it matters: it undermines the argument that women's subordinate position is natural and therefore inevitable. If the roles are constructed, they can be changed. De Beauvoir's claim that one is not born but becomes a woman expresses this directly.

Patriarchy

Literally "rule by the father". Feminists use it to describe a system of social structures, practices and beliefs through which men as a group dominate women as a group. Its significance is that it identifies women's subordination as systemic rather than the product of individual prejudice — located in the family, the labour market, the law, culture and sexuality.

The personal is political

Relationships within the private sphere are structured by power and are therefore properly political. Who does the housework, how childcare is distributed, and the dynamics of marriage, sexuality and reproduction are instances of a wider system of male dominance, not merely private arrangements.

Equality and difference

  • Equality feminism — minimise the political significance of sex difference; seek identical treatment.
  • Difference feminism — there are genuine differences that should be recognised and valued, rather than erased by a standard of equality modelled on men's lives.

Intersectionality

Race, class and gender oppression interlock, producing distinct experiences that cannot be understood by analysing any one axis in isolation. The position of a poor black woman is not the sum of racism, poverty and sexism but a specific condition of its own.

2. Key thinkers

Charlotte Perkins Gilman (1860–1935) — Women and Economics (1898)

  • Economic dependence — women's subordination is rooted in their economic dependence on men, reinforced by confinement to the domestic sphere.
  • The "sexuo-economic relation" — women's survival depending on attracting and retaining a male provider distorts their development and character.
  • Socialising domestic labour — her solution: communal kitchens, laundries and childcare, freeing women to enter paid employment on equal terms.
  • Gender roles are learned, not natural — an early statement of the sex/gender distinction.

Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986) — The Second Sex (1949)

  • "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" — femininity is a socially constructed identity imposed through culture, not a natural consequence of biological sex.
  • Woman as "the Other" — man is treated as the norm and the universal human subject; woman is defined in relation to him, and therefore as deviation rather than as an autonomous subject.
  • Existentialist foundation — women must transcend the passive role assigned to them and assert themselves as free subjects.

Kate Millett (1934–2017) — Sexual Politics (1970)

  • Relations between the sexes are political — power relations, not private matters.
  • Patriarchy operates through culture — through literature, art, religion and the family, which condition both sexes into accepting male dominance as natural. It is therefore reproduced through consent as much as through force.
  • The family as the chief instrument of patriarchal socialisation.
  • A cultural revolution, not merely legal reform, is required.

Sheila Rowbotham (b. 1943) — Hidden from History (1973)

  • Socialist feminism — women's oppression is bound up with capitalism.
  • Unpaid domestic labour reproduces the workforce at no cost to employers; women also function as a reserve army of labour, drawn in when needed and pushed out when not, depressing wages generally.
  • "Hidden from history" — women's contributions have been systematically written out of historical record.
  • Against economic reductionism — she insisted socialist analysis must address women's specific oppression rather than treating it as a secondary consequence of class that would dissolve automatically after a revolution.

bell hooks (1952–2021) — Ain't I a Woman? (1981)

  • Intersectionality — race, class and gender oppression interlock and cannot be analysed separately.
  • Critique of mainstream feminism — it had been dominated by white, middle-class, educated women and presented their experience as universal. Friedan's account of frustrated suburban housewives did not describe black women who had always worked outside the home from economic necessity — for whom paid employment was obligation, not liberation.
  • Feminism as a movement to end sexist oppression, connected to anti-racist and class politics rather than separate from them.

Liberal feminism delivered most of the concrete legal gains of the twentieth century — suffrage, equal pay legislation, access to the professions — because it built on principles society already accepted. Its limitation is equally clear: formal legal equality has been substantially achieved in Western societies while substantive inequality persists in pay, domestic labour and representation. That gap is the strongest evidence for the structural critiques.

4. The public/private divide

A crucial dividing line. Liberal feminism largely accepts it, campaigning for equality in the public sphere and valuing the private sphere as a zone protected from state intrusion. Radical feminism rejects it, arguing the divide is itself the mechanism by which women's subordination is shielded from scrutiny.

What politicising the private achieved: it made domestic violence, marital rape, unpaid domestic labour, reproductive rights and sexual harassment into political questions requiring public remedy. Consciousness-raising groups rested on the insight that individual experiences shared across many women revealed a structural pattern.

The equality/difference debate is a genuine dilemma, not an error on either side, and it has concrete consequences. Identical treatment can ignore pregnancy and caring responsibilities; recognising difference risks reinforcing the essentialism historically used to justify exclusion. Maternity provision illustrates the resolution most policy reaches: treating women differently precisely in order to make equal participation possible.

6. Is feminism a single ideology?

NoYes
The strands rest on incompatible foundations — liberal individualism, Marxist class analysis, the primacy of patriarchyAll share the core claims: women are systematically disadvantaged because of their sex; this is socially produced; it should end
They diagnose different causes and prescribe different remediesAll accept the sex/gender distinction and reject biological determinism
Intersectionality and postmodernism question whether there is a single subject "woman" at allSolidarity can rest on a shared political project rather than an assumed identity of experience — arguably a firmer foundation than the false universalism it replaced
