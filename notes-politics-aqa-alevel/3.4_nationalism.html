3.4 Nationalism

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 3 (Political Ideas)

Nationalism holds that humanity is naturally divided into nations, and that the nation should be the primary focus of political loyalty and the basis of political organisation. It is unusual among ideologies in being cross-cutting: it attaches to others rather than standing alone, which is why it has produced both national liberation and genocide from the same starting premise.

Optional ideology. Students study one of nationalism, feminism, multiculturalism, anarchism or ecologism.
AQA key thinkers for nationalism: Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Johann Herder, Giuseppe Mazzini, Marcus Garvey and Charles Maurras.

1. Core ideas

The nation

A group sharing a common culture, language, history, traditions or identity, whose members see themselves as a distinct people. Crucially, a nation is not the same as a state: a nation can exist without one (the Kurds), and a state can contain several nations (the UK).

Self-determination

The core political demand: a nation has the right to govern itself rather than be ruled by another people or an external power. This connects the cultural claim that a nation exists to the political claim that it should have a state.

The nation-state

The ideal that political and national boundaries should coincide. This is nationalism's practical programme — and its central practical difficulty, since populations are rarely neatly distributed. The post-1918 redrawing of European borders on self-determination principles left substantial minorities stranded, which became a source of grievance and eventually war.

Culturalism

Emphasis on a nation's distinctive language, customs, folklore and history — Herder's Volksgeist. Cultural nationalism can exist without demanding statehood.

Racialism

Some nationalisms define the nation by descent and biology rather than culture or citizenship, producing exclusion of those without the relevant ancestry. This is the variant responsible for nationalism's worst outcomes.

This distinction largely determines whether a given nationalism tends towards liberal self-determination or towards expulsion and conflict. It is the single most useful analytical tool in the topic — apply it to any nationalism you are asked to evaluate.

3. Key thinkers

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) — The Social Contract (1762)

  • The general will — sovereignty resides in the collective will of the people, directed at the common good rather than at private interests.
  • Popular sovereignty — legitimate government derives from the people as a collective body, not from a monarch.
  • Why he matters — he predates nationalism proper, but his idea that a people is the source of authority provided the foundation for the claim that nations should rule themselves. Nationalism is in this sense a child of the idea of popular sovereignty.

Johann Herder (1744–1803)

  • Volksgeist — each people possesses a unique spirit or character, expressed in its language, folklore, customs and history.
  • Cultural nationalism — the priority is the preservation and celebration of that distinctiveness, not necessarily statehood.
  • Cultural pluralism — crucially, Herder celebrated the distinctiveness of all cultures, implying their equal worth rather than any hierarchy. He was not a chauvinist.
  • Against Enlightenment universalism — he denied that there is a single universal standard of civilisation against which peoples can be ranked.

Giuseppe Mazzini (1805–1872)

  • Liberal nationalism — every nation has an equal right to self-determination; nations are like individuals, each entitled to freedom.
  • Nationalism and internationalism are compatible — a world of free, self-governing nations would cooperate peacefully.
  • Duty and action — nation-building requires active civic participation and moral duty, not merely sentiment.
  • Italian unification — his practical project, and the model for liberal nationalist movements across Europe.

Marcus Garvey (1887–1940)

  • Black nationalism and pan-Africanism — a response to slavery, colonialism and racial oppression.
  • Racial pride and self-reliance — black people should take pride in their African heritage and build their own institutions and economic power rather than seek acceptance from white society.
  • Separatism and return — he advocated a connection to, and for some a return to, an African homeland.
  • Significance — he shows nationalism functioning as a vehicle of liberation for an oppressed and dispersed people, which complicates any simple account of nationalism as reactionary.

Charles Maurras (1868–1952)

  • Integral nationalism — the nation is an organic whole to which the individual is entirely subordinate.
  • Authoritarian and anti-democratic — hostile to liberalism, democracy and parliamentary government, which he saw as dividing the nation.
  • Exclusive — the nation defined by descent and inherited culture; outsiders, particularly Jews, treated as alien and corrupting.
  • Hierarchy and tradition — a monarchist and reactionary, associated with Action Française.

5. Tensions between the thinkers

DisagreementOne sideThe other
Are nations equal?Mazzini — equal right to freedom; universalisableMaurras — the nation is superior; cannot be universalised without contradiction
Cultural or political?Herder — the nation is a cultural community; statehood not essentialRousseau, Mazzini — the nation is a political community exercising sovereignty
Inclusive or exclusive?Civic — membership by shared values, open to allEthnic — membership inherited; outsiders excluded
Relationship to the individualLiberal — the nation serves the freedom of its membersIntegral — the individual is subordinate to the nation
Mazzini and Maurras do not differ by degree but by logical form. Mazzini's principle can be granted to everyone; Maurras's cannot be universalised, because it asserts particular superiority. This is the strongest evidence that nationalism is not a single coherent ideology — the two positions share only the vocabulary of the nation.

6. Are nations natural or constructed?

  • Primordialism — nations are ancient, organic and pre-political. This is what nationalists themselves generally assume, and it grounds the claim that nations deserve self-government.
  • Modernism — nations were produced by print capitalism, mass education, industrialisation and state-building, and are "imagined communities" whose members never meet yet feel a shared bond.

This is the most serious intellectual challenge to nationalism: if nations are recent constructions, the claim that they represent a natural basis for political organisation is undermined. The nationalist reply is that constructed identities are entirely real in their effects — people demonstrably feel national loyalty and act on it, whatever its historical origin.

7. Liberation or oppression?

Nationalism as liberationNationalism as oppression
Freed peoples from imperial and autocratic rule — Italian and German unification, the end of the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empiresWhere the nation is ethnic and organic, and superiority is asserted, it has justified conquest, expulsion and genocide
Drove twentieth-century anti-colonial movements across Asia and AfricaMaurras's integral nationalism; the extreme nationalisms of the 1930s
Garvey's black nationalism asserted dignity against racial oppressionEthnic cleansing during the break-up of Yugoslavia
Extended self-government to people explicitly denied itDefining a nation necessarily identifies outsiders

Why both are possible: nationalism is cross-cutting. Combined with liberalism it produces Mazzini's universal self-determination; with reactionary conservatism, Maurras. The premise that peoples should govern themselves does not by itself determine who counts as the people — and that question is answered by whichever ideology nationalism has fused with.
