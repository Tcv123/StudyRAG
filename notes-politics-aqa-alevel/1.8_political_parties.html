1.8 Political Parties

Politics — AQA (7152) — Paper 1 (Government and Politics of the UK)

The specification requires you to study the origins, ideas and development of the Conservative, Labour and Liberal Democrat parties, how these shaped their current policies, and their structures and functions — along with party funding, minor parties and the nature of the UK party system.

Political party — an organised group that seeks to win political office in order to implement its programme, contesting elections under a common label. This distinguishes parties from pressure groups, which seek to influence whoever holds office rather than to hold it themselves.

1. Functions of parties

  • Representation — aggregating and articulating the interests of sections of society.
  • Participation and recruitment — a route into political activity; selecting candidates for office.
  • Policy formulation — developing programmes presented to voters in a manifesto.
  • Providing government and opposition — the governing team and the alternative.
  • Political education — informing and structuring public debate.

2. The Conservative Party

Origins and traditions

Descended from the Tories; the modern party dates from the 1830s. Two dominant traditions:

One NationNew Right (Thatcherism)
Paternalism and social obligation — Disraeli's "two nations" warning that a gulf between rich and poor threatens stabilityNeo-liberalism: free markets, privatisation, deregulation, lower direct taxation, control of inflation, restriction of union power
Acceptance of state intervention and the welfare stateNeo-conservatism: strong state on law and order, traditional morality, national sovereignty, the traditional family
Pragmatism and gradual reform to preserve stabilityWelfare dependency held to undermine self-reliance
Post-war consensus Conservatives; Macmillan; Cameron's early social liberalismThatcher; the 1980s privatisation programme; right-to-buy

Development

  • 1945–79: acceptance of the post-war settlement — the mixed economy, full employment, the NHS.
  • 1979–90: Thatcherism — privatisation of British Telecom and British Gas, right-to-buy, trade union legislation, reductions in the top rate of income tax.
  • 1990s: Major's government split by Europe, particularly over Maastricht.
  • 2005–16: Cameron's modernisation — social liberalism (same-sex marriage) combined with austerity after 2010.
  • 2016–: reshaped by Brexit; a new electoral coalition in 2019 built on Leave-voting former Labour seats; internal division over the size of the state.

3. The Labour Party

Origins

Founded in 1900 out of the trade union movement and socialist societies, to secure parliamentary representation for working people. The union link remains a defining structural feature — and a recurring source of controversy over funding and influence.

Old Labour and New Labour

Old Labour (social democracy)New Labour (the Third Way)
Common ownership — the original Clause IVClause IV revised in 1995, abandoning common ownership and endorsing a dynamic market economy
Nationalisation; a large public sectorAccepted privatisation and Conservative trade union legislation
Redistribution through progressive income taxRedistribution through tax credits and the minimum wage, not higher income tax rates
Strong union influencePower centralised around the leadership; union influence reduced
Equality of outcomeEquality of opportunity; "opportunity and responsibility"

Development since 2010: Miliband moved partly away from New Labour; Corbyn (2015–20) proposed extensive renationalisation and higher taxation, marking sharp ideological divergence from the Conservatives; Starmer moved back towards the centre.

The Clause IV revision of 1995 is the single most useful example in this topic. It works as evidence for ideological change, for leadership power over the party, and for the convergence thesis — three different essay questions from one fact.

4. The Liberal Democrats

  • Formed in 1988 by the merger of the Liberal Party and the Social Democratic Party (itself a 1981 breakaway from Labour).
  • Ideas: constitutional reform (electoral reform, a codified constitution, Lords reform), civil liberties, environmentalism, internationalism and pro-Europeanism, decentralisation.
  • Internal tension between classical liberal (economic, market-oriented) and social liberal (welfare-oriented) wings.
  • 2010–15 coalition: delivered a referendum on AV (lost) and a rise in the income tax threshold, but the tuition fees reversal was electorally catastrophic — the party fell from 57 seats to 8 in 2015.
  • A cautionary case study in what FPTP and coalition do to the third party.

5. Party structure and internal democracy

Leadership selection

  • Labour: one member one vote among members and registered supporters. Produced Corbyn in 2015 despite limited support among Labour MPs.
  • Conservative: MPs shortlist two candidates; the membership chooses between them. Produced Truss in 2022 against the parliamentary party's preference.
The examinable tension: party members are far more ideologically committed than the general electorate. Member-led selection enhances internal democracy but can produce leaders who command neither their parliamentary party nor broad public support — which is why both parties have repeatedly revisited these rules.

Candidate selection and policy

  • Local party members select parliamentary candidates, under national rules and approved lists — so central machines retain significant control.
  • Conference is formally significant for Labour but has in practice been managed by leaderships; New Labour in particular reduced the influence of both conference and the unions.

6. Party funding

Sources

  • Membership subscriptions and small donations.
  • Large donations from individuals and companies (Conservative) and affiliated trade unions (Labour).
  • Limited public funding: Short Money (Commons opposition parties) and Cranborne Money (Lords), plus policy development grants.
  • All donations above a threshold must be declared to the Electoral Commission; statutory limits apply to campaign spending.

7. Minor parties and the party system

Why minor parties struggle — and still matter

  • FPTP penalises dispersed support: UKIP's 3.9 million votes produced one seat in 2015.
  • But concentrated support succeeds: the SNP converted ~1.5 million votes into 56 seats the same year.
  • Minor parties shape the agenda even without seats — UKIP's electoral pressure was central to the calling of the EU referendum.
  • Proportional systems in the devolved nations give them a genuine platform.

What kind of party system?

The honest answer is that it depends on what you measure and where you look.

  • Two-party at Westminster by seats — Conservative and Labour have alternated in government throughout the post-war period.
  • Multi-party by votes — the two main parties' combined share has fallen well below post-war levels.
  • Multi-party in the devolved nations under proportional systems.
  • Dominant-party in particular periods — 1979–97 and 2010–24 — though that describes a phase rather than a structural feature.

8. Convergence and divergence

Evidence of convergenceEvidence of divergence
New Labour accepted privatisation, market mechanisms and Conservative spending limitsPost-2015: Corbyn proposed extensive renationalisation while the Conservatives pursued a hard Brexit
Cameron's Conservatives accepted NHS spending and social liberalismSharp disagreement on taxation, public ownership and the size of the state
Both competing for the median voter under FPTP — the "catch-all party" thesisBrexit cut across parties rather than along them, fracturing both

The defensible conclusion is that convergence was a phase rather than a permanent condition, and that the underlying problem voters identify may be trust rather than lack of choice.

Essential examples

  • Clause IV revision, 1995 — ideological change and leadership power.
  • 1980s privatisations — British Telecom, British Gas; right-to-buy.
  • 2010–15 coalition — Lib Dem tuition fees reversal; 57 seats to 8 in 2015.
  • Corbyn 2015 and Truss 2022 — member-led selection against the parliamentary party's preference.
  • UKIP 2015 — 3.9m votes, one seat, decisive agenda influence.
  • SNP 2015 — 1.5m votes, 56 seats; concentration beats volume under FPTP.
