Eduqas GCSE Geography B (C112QS) — Component 2: Problem Solving Geography

4.1 Problem Solving Geography

1. What Is Component 2?

Component 2 is a 1 hour 30 minute written exam worth 30% of the GCSE. It is a decision-making paper: you are given a Resource Folder in the exam (there is no pre-release) about an unfamiliar geographical issue, and you work through it step by step until you choose and justify a solution.

The issue can come from any of the three themes you studied for Component 1, and questions deliberately link them together. The June 2024 paper, for example, was about desertification in Bahrain, but also tested climate graphs, development indicators (Theme 1), the water cycle, over-abstraction and climate change (Themes 2 and 3).

2. Using the Resource Folder

The folder contains maps, graphs, tables, photographs, diagrams and short articles. You will be told which figure to use for each question.

  1. Skim the whole folder first (2–3 minutes) so you know what evidence exists — you will need it again in Part C.
  2. For each figure, check the title, key, units, scale and dates before answering.
  3. Quote evidence: figures, place names and dates. "Use data" questions are capped if you don't.
  4. Watch for the source and date of articles — an advert or campaign group may be biased.
  5. Don't copy long chunks of text from the folder — mark schemes warn examiners about direct copying, and you gain marks by explaining or evaluating the information.

Development data — limitations

Problem-solving papers often include a table of development indicators (GNI per capita, literacy, life expectancy, % of GNI from farming). Be ready to explain the limits of using data to measure development:

  • A single indicator shows only one aspect of a complex idea.
  • National averages hide inequality between regions, rich and poor, or men and women.
  • Data can be inaccurate, out of date or manipulated by governments.
  • Some aspects of quality of life, such as happiness or freedom, are hard to measure.

4. Part B — Evaluating Possible Solutions

Part B introduces solutions, often each tried in a different country. In 2024 there were three for desertification:

SolutionExample in the folderAdvantagesDisadvantages
Advanced technology (satellites, drones, soil-moisture sensors, genetic engineering)California, USAWater used precisely, so less is wasted and over-abstraction is avoided; crops survive and soil stays coveredExpensive; needs training, infrastructure and maintenance; some research takes decades (tree crops need 20+ years to mature)
Sand damsZimbabweCheap local materials; low-tech and community-maintained; stored water is protected from evaporation; solar pump uses no fossil fuelNeed seasonal sandy rivers; pumps must be manufactured, transported and repaired; may reduce flow downstream
Drought-tolerant cropsGuatemalaSurvive dry years, so soil is protected and families have food and income; less irrigation neededMay give lower yields or prices; seeds must be obtained; do not tackle wider causes such as over-abstraction
Sustainability means meeting the needs of people today without preventing future generations from meeting theirs. Evaluate solutions against all three strands:
Social — health, food security, education, communities staying together.
Economic — cost to build and maintain, jobs, incomes, debt.
Environmental — soils, water, biodiversity, pollution, carbon emissions.

"Evaluate how … could be considered sustainable" (6 marks)

The mark scheme caps answers with no evaluation at 5 marks. To reach the top band:

  • Explain how the solution is sustainable in at least two strands, with chains of reasoning.
  • Consider the short and long term.
  • Include at least one way it could be unsustainable, then weigh up.
Model paragraph — sand dams: Sand dams are environmentally sustainable because water stored in the sand is protected from evaporation, so farmers have water in the dry season without pumping groundwater, and crops protect the soil from erosion. They are economically sustainable because walls are built from cheap local materials and maintained by the community without specialist training. However, the solar pump must be manufactured and transported, and if it breaks and nobody can repair it, the system stops working; trapping water could also reduce the river's flow for communities downstream. Overall they are highly sustainable where there are seasonal sandy rivers, provided local people are trained to maintain the pumps.

Attitudes and viewpoints

Expect a question on why people hold different views — for example why attitudes to climate change vary: the problem feels global so individual action seems pointless; some people are not yet affected; change is inconvenient or expensive; natural climate change in the past leads some to doubt human causes; influential people or media deny it; people struggling now find it hard to prioritise the future. Give two reasons and develop each.

5. Part C — Making and Justifying a Decision

Part C asks you to choose one option for the place in the question and justify it, considering social, economic and environmental sustainability. It is marked in four bands out of 12, with 4 more marks for SPaG.

BandWhat examiners look for
10–12A substantiated decision with clear justification for the chosen option over the others; chains of reasoning covering social, economic and environmental effects; consistently detailed, using a range of evidence from the paper and Resource Folder
7–9A justified decision; some chains of reasoning on sustainability; detailed elaboration supported by evidence
4–6A decision simply justified; limited observations on sustainability; some evidence
1–3A simple, unsubstantiated decision that briefly refers to effects

A structure that works

  1. Decision — name your option in the first sentence.
  2. Why it suits this place — link to the place's own data: wealth, physical geography, the main cause of the problem. In 2024, Bahrain's GNI per capita of $40,730 made technology affordable, while its lack of rivers made sand dams unsuitable.
  3. Sustainability — a developed paragraph each on social, economic and environmental effects.
  4. Drawbacks — admit a weakness of your option and say how it could be reduced.
  5. Why not the others — a sentence or two on each rejected option, using evidence.
  6. Conclusion — restate the decision; you may suggest combining it with another option, but you must still make a clear choice.
Exam Tip — there is no right answer: Any option can reach full marks if it is well justified. Choose the option you can argue best with evidence from the folder, not the one you think the examiner wants. What separates the top band is explaining why your choice is better for this particular place than the alternatives.
Opening of a top-band answer (2024): "Bahrain should choose advanced technology. Unlike Zimbabwe or Guatemala, Bahrain is a wealthy NIC with a GNI per capita of $40,730, so it can afford drones and soil-moisture sensors. Its main cause of desertification is over-abstraction of groundwater on a small, hot island where temperatures have risen from about 26.5 °C to 29 °C since 1901, and precise irrigation would cut water use, protecting its aquifers from salt-water intrusion…"

6. Skills Checklist

Component 2 tests geographical skills heavily. Make sure you can:

  • Describe patterns on world maps, choropleths and isoline maps, using compass directions and lines of latitude.
  • Read and complete line graphs, bar charts, climate graphs and dual-axis graphs (e.g. population and water use).
  • Extend a line on a graph to a forecast value.
  • Calculate mean, median, mode, range, percentage change and ratios — and say which average suits the data.
  • Interpret photographs and annotated diagrams.
  • Spot bias and limitations in data and sources.
  • Define key terms precisely — NIC, LIC, HIC, sustainability, over-abstraction, desertification, interception, infiltration.
