Eduqas GCSE Geography B (C112QS) — Component 3: Applied Fieldwork Enquiry

4.2 Applied Fieldwork Enquiry

1. What Is Component 3?

Component 3 is a 1 hour 30 minute written exam worth 30% of the GCSE. There is no coursework: everything you learned on your fieldwork days is assessed through questions on this paper. In June 2024 the paper carried 76 marks, including 4 marks for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terminology (SPaG) on the final extended question.

You must carry out at least two fieldwork enquiries in contrasting environments — normally one with a physical focus (for example a river, a beach or weather and microclimate) and one with a human focus (for example a town centre, regeneration, traffic or environmental quality).

PartWhat it testsJune 2024 marks
Part AAn enquiry theme (in 2024, investigating change over time). Starts with data about an unfamiliar enquiry, then asks about your own fieldwork.18
Part BA second enquiry theme (in 2024, investigating mitigating risk). Same pattern: unfamiliar data first, then your own fieldwork.18
Part CThe wider UK dimension: a UK-scale issue (in 2024, river flooding) using a Resource Folder. Builds to a 12-mark evaluation plus 4 SPaG.36 + 4
Exam Tip — two kinds of question: Every part mixes questions about an unfamiliar enquiry (someone else's data, maps and methods) with questions that say "This question is about your own experience…". For the second kind you must name your actual location, equipment, data and results. Generic answers ("we used a questionnaire and it was good") are capped in the lowest band.

2. The Enquiry Process

The exam is built around the six stages of geographical enquiry. Questions are labelled in the mark scheme against these stages, so learning them gives you a framework for every answer.

StageWhat you doTypical exam command
1. Enquiry questionChoose a question or hypothesis linked to geographical theory and a real place."Suggest one strength of your enquiry question."
2. Data collectionCollect primary and secondary data using appropriate methods and sampling, safely."Give one reason why your primary data collection was accurate."
3. PresentationProcess and present data using appropriate maps, graphs and diagrams."Which graph would be suitable…? Give one reason."
4. AnalysisDescribe patterns, calculate statistics, identify anomalies, link results to theory."Calculate the median…", "Describe the trend…"
5. ConclusionsAnswer the enquiry question using evidence."Suggest one conclusion… Use data in your answer."
6. EvaluationJudge the reliability and accuracy of methods, data and conclusions; suggest improvements."Evaluate one strength and one weakness of your conclusions."

Writing a good enquiry question

  • Focused and testable — "How does river velocity change downstream on the River X?" not "What is the river like?"
  • Linked to theory — e.g. the Bradshaw model (rivers), longshore drift (coasts), Burgess land-use model or environmental quality decreasing towards the centre (urban). Theory lets you predict a result and then prove or disprove it.
  • Located precisely — a named river, beach or town, so the results can be compared with other places.
  • Manageable — possible to collect enough data safely in the time available.
  • Often broken into sub-questions so each data set answers one part.
Hypothesis: a statement that can be tested and accepted or rejected — e.g. "Pedestrian counts decrease with distance from the town centre." A null hypothesis states there is no relationship.

3. Primary and Secondary Data

Primary dataSecondary data
DefinitionCollected first-hand by you in the fieldCollected by someone else and used by you
ExamplesRiver width, depth and velocity; beach profiles; pebble size; pedestrian and traffic counts; questionnaires; environmental quality surveys; land-use maps; field sketches; photographsCensus data, Met Office climate records, Environment Agency flood maps and river levels, OS and historic maps, Google Street View, council reports, house-price websites, newspaper articles
StrengthsYou know exactly how it was collected; tailored to your question; up to dateCovers long time periods and large areas; saves time; allows comparison and context

Exam Tip — "Why was secondary data useful?": Credit is given for explaining or backing up primary patterns, giving context to the enquiry question, adding a time dimension (before and after, long-term trends), increasing validity, and covering places you could not visit. Always name the source: "Met Office records for 1991–2020 showed that…". With no secondary source identified, answers are capped at 2 marks out of 4.

Common physical methods

MethodHowAccuracy issues and improvements
River width and depthTape measure bank to bank; metre rule at equal intervals across the channelTape sagging; rule sinking into soft bed or moved by current. Keep tape taut at water level; take depth at regular, close intervals.
River velocityTime a float over 10 m, or use a flow meterFloats caught in eddies or wind; reaction-time error. Repeat three times and average; a flow meter is more precise.
DischargeCross-sectional area × velocity (cumecs)Errors in width, depth and velocity multiply together.
Beach profileRanging poles and clinometer at each break of slopePoles not vertical on soft sand; tides limit time. Use consistent intervals and the same observer.
Sediment size and shapeMeasure long axis with a ruler/calipers; Powers' roundness indexChoosing pebbles by eye is biased — use random sampling within a quadrat.
Microclimate / weatherThermometer, anemometer, rain gauge, recording sheet at fixed timesReadings change through the day; shade the thermometer; record at the same time at every site.

Common human methods

MethodHowAccuracy issues and improvements
Pedestrian / traffic countTally for a fixed time (e.g. 5 minutes) at each siteVaries with time of day, day of week, weather. Count at the same time at all sites; repeat on other days.
QuestionnaireClosed and open questions to the publicSmall or biased samples (only approaching friendly-looking people); leading questions. Use systematic sampling (every 5th person) and pilot the questions.
Environmental quality surveyScore factors (litter, noise, building condition) on a bi-polar scale, e.g. −3 to +3Subjective — group members score differently. Agree clear criteria; average several scorers.
Land-use mappingRecord the use of every building on a base mapTime-consuming; categories unclear. Use a standard key (e.g. Goad categories).
Field sketch / annotated photoRecord features with labels and a title, direction and dateSelective. Add a scale, north arrow and annotations that explain, not just label.
Air qualityLichen surveys, diffusion tubes or a handheld meterShort sampling periods may not represent normal conditions.

4. Sampling

TechniqueHow it worksStrengthWeakness
RandomSites/people chosen by random numbersEverything has an equal chance of being chosen — no biasMay miss important areas; sites can be hard to reach
SystematicRegular intervals — every 50 m along a transect, every 5th personCovers the whole area evenly; good for showing change along a line or roadMay miss features between intervals; can be biased if the pattern repeats at the interval
StratifiedSample in proportion to sub-groups — e.g. each land use, age group or zoneRepresents all groups fairly (e.g. different land uses and traffic)Needs prior knowledge of the groups; more complicated
OpportunisticWhoever or wherever is availableQuick and easyMost likely to be biased and unrepresentative
Exam Tip — matching questions: The 2024 paper asked candidates to match each technique to its strength: systematic → useful for features that change along a road or within an area; stratified → takes account of different land uses and traffic; random → everything has an equal chance of being measured. Learn these phrases.

Risk assessment

Fieldwork questions can ask about safety. Typical hazards are fast or deep water, slippery rocks, tides and cliff falls, traffic, extreme weather and talking to strangers. Reduce risk with wellies and a buddy system, checking tide times, staying out of water above knee depth, high-visibility jackets near roads, and working in groups.

Good presentation includes a title, labelled axes with units, a key, an appropriate scale, and on maps a north arrow and scale. Exam questions often ask you to suggest improvements to a figure — these features are the usual answers, along with making symbols proportional and giving exact dates and values instead of "up to" or "now/later".

Exam Tip — median versus mean: When data contains an extreme outlier (e.g. +200 among values around −16), the median is more appropriate because one extreme value skews the mean. Always show your working — there is usually 1 mark for the method and 1 for the answer, and negative signs and units matter.

Reading maps

  • Six-figure grid references: eastings (along the corridor) first, then northings (up the stairs), estimating tenths of each square — e.g. 097213.
  • Area: count grid squares — on a 1:25 000 or 1:50 000 OS map each square is 1 km², so a study area inside a town ring road is likely to be about 1 km², not 10 m² or 10 km².
  • Describing patterns: give the general trend, then use data and name places; mention exceptions (anomalies).

7. Conclusions and Evaluation

Drawing conclusions

  1. Answer the enquiry question directly — was the hypothesis supported, partly supported or rejected?
  2. Support it with specific data: "velocity increased from 0.12 m/s at site 1 to 0.41 m/s at site 6".
  3. Explain the result using geographical theory.
  4. Note anomalies and suggest reasons for them.
  5. State the significance — what the result means for the place, for theory, or for people.

Evaluating — accuracy, reliability and validity

Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true value (affected by equipment and human error).
Reliability: whether repeating the method would give the same results (affected by sample size, repeats and timing).
Validity: whether the data actually answers the enquiry question.
WeaknessEffectImprovement
Single visit on one dayResults may reflect unusual conditions (weather, flow, day of week)Repeat at different times / seasons and average
Small sampleUnrepresentative; anomalies have a large effectMore sites, more questionnaire responses, more repeats
Human errorInaccurate measurements (reaction times, misread instruments)Use digital equipment (flow meter); the same person takes each reading
Subjective scoringResults vary between observersClear criteria; average several observers
Biased samplingSome groups over- or under-representedRandom, systematic or stratified sampling
Sites too few or poorly spacedPattern unclearSystematic intervals along the whole transect
Exam Tip — "Evaluate one strength and one weakness of your conclusions" (4 marks): Make a point and develop it twice for each side. Example: "A strength was that my conclusion directly answered my question, showing that velocity increased downstream as the Bradshaw model predicts, and was supported by data from all six sites. A weakness was that we only visited once in dry weather, so discharge was low and the pattern might be different in winter, which makes the conclusion less reliable."

8. Your Own Fieldwork — Prepare These Before the Exam

Write a one-page summary of each enquiry and learn it. Questions on your own fieldwork can appear in Parts A and B whichever theme is set, so you need the facts ready to adapt.

HeadingWhat to learn (for each enquiry)

Primary methodsEquipment, sampling technique, number of sites, how you made it accurate

ConclusionThe answer to your question, with evidence
EvaluationTwo strengths and two weaknesses, each with an improvement
Worked example — physical enquiry (rivers): "How do channel characteristics change downstream on a small river?" Six sites chosen systematically every 500 m from source; width (tape), depth (metre rule every 20 cm), velocity (float timed over 10 m, three repeats); Environment Agency river-level data used to check the river was at normal flow. Presented as cross-sections and scatter graphs of distance against discharge. Conclusion: width, depth and discharge increased downstream, supporting the Bradshaw model, with an anomaly at a riffle where the channel was shallow. Evaluation: floats were caught in eddies, so a flow meter would be more accurate; one visit in summer limits reliability.
Worked example — human enquiry (urban): "Does environmental quality improve with distance from the town centre?" Ten sites along a transect, systematic every 200 m; environmental quality survey (−3 to +3 on eight factors), pedestrian counts for five minutes, 30 questionnaires; census deprivation data as secondary data. Presented as a located bar chart on a map and a scatter graph. Conclusion: quality generally improved outwards, but a site near a main road scored low because of traffic noise. Evaluation: scoring was subjective, so averaging several scorers and visiting at different times would improve reliability.

9. Part C — The Wider UK Dimension

Part C uses a Resource Folder to explore a geographical issue at the scale of the whole UK. In June 2024 the issue was river flooding: a graph of UK rainfall since 1836, UK population projections to 2045, a flood-risk map of Marlow on the River Thames and a diagram of land-use zoning on a floodplain. The questions moved from reading graphs and maps to explaining processes, and finished with:

"Land use zoning is the best way to sustainably manage river flood risk in the UK. To what extent do you agree with this statement?" [12 + 4 SPaG]

Part C draws on your knowledge from Component 1 (rivers, coasts, weather, climate change, UK cities and population), so revise those themes at UK scale — not just your case studies.

Answering the 12-mark evaluation

  1. State your position in the opening sentence (agree, partly agree, disagree).
  2. Argument — two or three developed points for the statement, each using evidence from the Resource Folder (quote figures and place names).
  3. Counter-argument — limitations and alternatives, again with evidence.
  4. Sustainability — explicitly consider social, economic and environmental effects, short and long term. The top band asks for this.
  5. Wider knowledge — bring in examples from your studies (e.g. Pickering "Slowing the Flow", the Jubilee River, Storm Desmond).
  6. Conclusion — a justified judgement that follows from your argument; "it depends" must say on what.
Exam Tip — SPaG: The 4 SPaG marks reward accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar and a wide range of specialist terms. Use words such as impermeable surfaces, lag time, peak discharge, floodplain zoning, hard and soft engineering, sustainability, stakeholders accurately.
