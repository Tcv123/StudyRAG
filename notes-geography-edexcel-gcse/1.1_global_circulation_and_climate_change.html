1.1 Global Circulation and Climate Change

1. Global Atmospheric Circulation

Global atmospheric circulation: The large-scale movement of air around the Earth driven by unequal heating. The equator receives more solar radiation than the poles, creating temperature and pressure differences. This drives a system of convection cells (Hadley, Ferrel, and Polar cells) that redistribute heat energy around the globe and create the major wind belts and climate zones.

Surface winds

  • Trade winds blow from the sub-tropical high (30°) back towards the low pressure at the Equator. The Coriolis effect bends them, so they blow from the north-east in the Northern Hemisphere and south-east in the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Westerlies (south-westerlies over the UK) blow from the sub-tropical high towards the sub-polar low at 60°, bringing mild, wet Atlantic air and depressions to the UK.
  • Where trade winds meet at the ITCZ, warm moist air rises, giving heavy convectional rain — the same rising air that helps tropical storms form over warm oceans.

2. Ocean Currents and Heat Transfer

Edexcel B expects you to know how both the atmosphere and the oceans redistribute heat from the Equator towards the poles.

  • Surface ocean currents are driven by prevailing winds. Warm currents carry heat away from the tropics; cold currents bring cooler water back. The Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift carry warm water from the Gulf of Mexico to north-west Europe, keeping UK winters several degrees milder than places at the same latitude such as Labrador in Canada.
  • The thermohaline circulation ("global ocean conveyor belt") is driven by differences in water temperature and salinity. In the North Atlantic, cold, salty, dense water sinks and flows along the ocean floor; warmer surface water flows in to replace it. A full circuit takes around 1,000 years.
  • Together, atmospheric circulation and ocean currents transfer roughly equal amounts of heat polewards. Without them the tropics would be much hotter and high latitudes much colder.
Exam Tip: Melting Greenland ice adds fresh water to the North Atlantic, which could slow the thermohaline circulation. Linking ocean circulation to climate change is a good way to show synoptic understanding.

3. Defining Climate Change

Weather — the short-term atmospheric conditions at a specific time and place (temperature, rainfall, wind speed, cloud cover). Can change hour by hour.
Climate — the long-term average pattern of weather conditions for a region, calculated over at least 30 years. Changes slowly over decades or millennia.
Climate Change — a long-term shift in global average temperatures and precipitation patterns, beyond natural variability. The current concern is the unprecedented rate of warming since industrialisation (~1750) driven largely by human activities.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) — the UN scientific body that reviews global climate research — concluded in its 2021 report (AR6): "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land."

4. Evidence of Climate Change

The Quaternary period — the most recent geological period, covering roughly the last 2.6 million years to the present day. During the Quaternary, the global climate has swung between cold glacial periods (ice ages, when ice sheets covered much of Britain) and warmer interglacial periods such as the one we are in now, which began about 11,700 years ago. Evidence of these past changes tells us how the climate behaves naturally — and why the recent rate of warming is so unusual.

Exam Tip: When asked for evidence of climate change, use specific data (figures, dates, measurements) — not vague statements like "glaciers are melting." Examiners award marks for named examples with statistics. E.g. "Arctic sea ice extent has declined by 13% per decade since 1979 according to NASA satellite data."

5. Natural Causes of Climate Change

Milankovitch Cycles

Cyclical changes in Earth's orbit and orientation relative to the Sun drive natural climate cycles over tens of thousands of years:

  • Eccentricity (~100,000 years) — shape of Earth's orbit changes from nearly circular to more elliptical. When more elliptical, seasonal variation in solar energy received is greater.
  • Axial tilt (~41,000 years) — angle of Earth's axis varies from 22.1° to 24.5°. Greater tilt = more extreme seasons (hotter summers, colder winters). Current tilt is 23.5° and slowly decreasing.
  • Precession (~26,000 years) — Earth's axis wobbles like a spinning top. Changes which hemisphere experiences summer closest to the Sun, altering which seasons are more intense.

Together, these cycles explain the glacial and interglacial periods (ice ages) of the past 2.6 million years. However, they cannot explain the rapid warming since 1750 — Milankovitch cycles operate over far longer timescales and current orbital forcing actually points toward slight cooling over the next few thousand years.

Solar Variation

The Sun's energy output is not constant. It varies by approximately 0.1% over an 11-year sunspot cycle. Periods of high sunspot activity correspond to slightly higher solar output. Scientists have identified longer periods of very low solar activity — the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715) — which coincides with the coldest phase of the Little Ice Age. However, solar output has been flat or slightly declining since the 1980s, while global temperatures have continued to rise sharply. This rules out solar variation as a primary driver of current warming. The IPCC (2021) estimates that natural factors (solar and volcanic) changed global temperature by only about −0.1 to +0.1°C since 1850–1900, whereas greenhouse gases caused roughly 1.5°C of warming, partly offset by cooling from aerosol pollution.

Volcanic Eruptions

Large explosive eruptions inject sulphur dioxide (SO₂) into the stratosphere. This forms sulphate aerosols — tiny droplets that reflect incoming solar radiation back to space, causing short-term surface cooling. Key examples:

  • Mt Pinatubo, Philippines (1991) — second-largest eruption of the 20th century; injected 20 million tonnes of SO₂ into the stratosphere. Global average temperature fell ~0.5°C for 1–2 years before aerosols settled.
  • Laki, Iceland (1783) — fissure eruption releasing enormous SO₂ clouds; caused harvest failures across Europe and may have contributed to famine in Egypt by disrupting the Nile flood.
  • Tambora, Indonesia (1815) — the largest eruption in recorded history; caused 1816 to be known as the "Year Without a Summer" across the Northern Hemisphere; the eruption and its aftermath killed an estimated 71,000+ people in Indonesia, and 1816 crop failures caused famine in Europe and elsewhere.

Volcanic eruptions produce cooling, not warming, and effects are temporary (1–3 years). They cannot account for the sustained warming trend observed since 1750. Some volcanoes also emit CO₂, but human emissions are now estimated to be 40–100 times greater than all volcanic CO₂ output combined.

Exam Tip: A common exam question asks you to distinguish between natural and human causes of climate change, then explain why scientists believe human causes are dominant. Key arguments: (1) the rate of warming is far faster than any natural cycle — over 1°C in about 150 years vs. roughly 4–7°C over about 10,000 years at the end of the last ice age; (2) ice core data shows CO₂ levels are unprecedented in 800,000 years; (3) solar output has not increased but temperature has; (4) isotopic "fingerprinting" of CO₂ in the atmosphere confirms it comes from fossil fuels (different carbon isotope ratio to volcanic CO₂).

6. Human Causes — The Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

Natural Greenhouse Effect — essential for life on Earth. Without it, average global temperature would be −18°C (instead of current +15°C). GHGs (CO₂, water vapour, methane) in the atmosphere trap some of the longwave infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface after it absorbs incoming solar energy.

Enhanced Greenhouse Effect — human activities increasing the concentration of GHGs in the atmosphere. More GHG molecules means more infrared radiation is absorbed and re-emitted back toward Earth's surface before it can escape to space, causing global warming. The mechanism: GHG molecules vibrate when they absorb infrared energy, then release that energy in all directions — including back downward.

Current CO₂ levels: ~422 ppm (parts per million) in 2024 — the highest in at least 800,000 years (EPICA Dome C ice core data). Pre-industrial level was ~280 ppm. Rate of increase is ~2.5 ppm per year — faster than any natural rate seen in the ice core record.

Role of Deforestation

Forests act as carbon sinks — absorbing CO₂ from the atmosphere through photosynthesis. Deforestation contributes ~10% of global GHG emissions in two ways:

  1. Burning or decomposing trees releases stored carbon as CO₂.
  2. Fewer trees means less CO₂ is absorbed — reducing the natural sink capacity.

Amazon deforestation is a major concern — roughly 17–20% of the Amazon has been cleared since 1970. Some scientists warn it may reach a "tipping point" where it switches from a carbon sink to a carbon source. Parts of the south-eastern Amazon already emit more CO₂ than they absorb.

7. Impacts of Climate Change

The impacts of climate change are wide-ranging, affecting economies, societies, and natural environments. They are experienced unequally — Low Income Countries (developing countries), small island states, and communities in the Global South face the greatest risks despite contributing the least to emissions.

Economic Impacts

  • Agriculture losses — changing rainfall patterns, more frequent droughts and floods, and extreme heat reduce crop yields. Sub-Saharan Africa crop yields predicted to fall 8–22% by 2050 (IPCC). Coffee production in Ethiopia — one of the world's leading exporters — faces a 40–59% reduction in suitable growing area by 2100.
  • Extreme weather damage costs — insurance industry losses from extreme weather have grown dramatically. Munich Re estimated around $320 billion in economic losses from natural disasters in 2024, with extreme weather events accounting for the majority. The Caribbean economy loses an estimated 1–2% of GDP annually to hurricane damage — a figure set to rise.
  • Infrastructure damage — flooding, storm surges, and permafrost thaw damage roads, buildings, and pipelines. In Alaska, permafrost thaw is causing roads and buildings to subside; estimated repair costs run to billions of dollars. UK flooding in winter 2019–2020 caused hundreds of millions of pounds of damage.
  • Tourism losses — the Great Barrier Reef contributes AUD$6.4 billion to the Australian economy annually; coral bleaching threatens this. Alpine ski resorts losing viability as snowfall decreases — many lower-altitude Swiss resorts could become unviable by 2100 under high-emission scenarios.
  • Fisheries decline — ocean warming and acidification disrupt fish stocks. The North Sea cod fishery has declined by 80% since 1970, partly due to warming waters reducing food availability for cod larvae.
  • Energy costs — increased cooling demand (air conditioning) and reduced efficiency of fossil fuel power plants in extreme heat raise energy costs. US electricity demand for cooling is projected to rise significantly by 2050.

Social Impacts

  • Food insecurity — changing monsoons threaten rice and wheat harvests for 2 billion people in South Asia. Some estimates suggest climate change could push up to 183 million additional people into hunger by 2050.
  • Water stress — glacial retreat in the Himalayas threatens freshwater supply for over 1.9 billion people who depend on rivers fed by glacial meltwater (Ganges, Indus, Yangtze). Short-term increase in meltwater is followed by long-term reduction as glaciers disappear.
  • Health impacts — malaria mosquitoes (Anopheles) moving to higher altitudes in Africa and Asia as temperatures rise; now found in Ethiopian Highlands where the population has no immunity. Heatwave mortality: European heatwave (2003) killed ~70,000 people; UK heatwave (July 2022, 40.3°C — the first time 40°C was recorded) caused an estimated 3,000 excess deaths. Flood-driven cholera outbreaks are increasing (e.g. Cyclone Idai, Mozambique, 2019).
  • Climate refugees and displacement — World Bank estimates 216 million internal climate migrants by 2050. Bangladesh: 17 million people could be displaced by a 1m sea level rise. Kiribati and Tuvalu face complete inundation. Syria's 2006–2010 drought (linked to climate change) contributed to internal displacement that preceded the Syrian conflict — an example of climate change acting as a "threat multiplier."
  • Mental health — "eco-anxiety," trauma after extreme weather events, and loss of cultural identity for communities displaced from ancestral lands are growing concerns, particularly among young people.

Environmental Impacts

  • Arctic sea ice loss — Arctic sea ice extent has declined by 13% per decade since 1979. The Arctic is warming 4x faster than the global average due to the albedo feedback loop (see Section 4 diagram).
  • Ocean acidification — oceans absorb ~26% of human CO₂ emissions; CO₂ dissolves to form carbonic acid. Ocean pH has fallen from 8.2 to 8.1 since pre-industrial times — a 30% increase in acidity. This weakens the shells of molluscs and corals. Great Barrier Reef has lost 50% of its coral cover since 1995; 2016 and 2017 bleaching events affected 91% of reefs surveyed.
  • Permafrost thaw — Arctic permafrost stores twice as much carbon as is currently in the atmosphere. Thawing releases methane (a powerful GHG), creating a dangerous positive feedback loop. Siberia's permafrost is thawing at an accelerating rate; sinkholes called "thermokarst" are appearing across Siberia.
  • Ecosystem disruption and biodiversity loss — IPCC (2022): at 3°C of warming, roughly 12% (possibly up to ~29%) of land species face a very high risk of extinction. Polar bears losing hunting habitat as sea ice retreats earlier each spring; body condition scores declining (thinner bears, fewer cubs). Coral triangle (SE Asia) supports 75% of world's coral species and 120 million people — severely threatened. Phenological mismatch — flowers blooming and insects emerging out of sync disrupts pollination.
  • Sea level rise — thermal expansion (40%) + land ice melt (60%). IPCC projects 0.28–1.01m of sea level rise by 2100 depending on emissions. Beyond 2100, even if warming stabilises, seas will continue to rise for centuries. Greenland alone holds enough ice to raise global sea levels by 7m if completely melted.
Exam Tip: Climate change impacts are geographically unequal — developing countries are most vulnerable despite contributing least to the problem. This "climate justice" argument is key for evaluation questions. Developed country carbon emissions cause disproportionate harm to developing country populations who lack resources to adapt. Always link economic, social, and environmental impacts together — they are interconnected (e.g. coral reef loss = environmental impact + economic impact on tourism and fisheries + social impact on food security for coastal communities).

8. Case Studies

Case Study: Arctic Sea Ice Loss — Impacts on Wildlife and Indigenous Communities

Location & Scale: The Arctic Ocean and surrounding tundra, including northern Canada, Alaska, Russia, Greenland, and Scandinavia. The Arctic is warming at 3–4 times the global average rate, making it the fastest-warming region on Earth.

Scale of Ice Loss:

  • Arctic sea ice extent has declined by approximately 13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979 (NASA).
  • Summer sea ice volume has declined by over 75% since the 1980s.
  • September 2012 saw the lowest Arctic sea ice extent ever recorded — 3.41 million km² (compared to ~7 million km² in the 1980s).
  • Scientists predict the Arctic Ocean could be essentially ice-free in summer by the 2050s under current emission trends.

Impacts on Wildlife:

  • Polar bears (Ursus maritimus) — depend on sea ice as a hunting platform to catch ringed seals. With ice forming later in autumn and breaking up earlier in spring, polar bears have a shorter hunting season. Western Hudson Bay polar bear population has roughly halved since the 1980s. Bears are coming ashore thinner and in poorer body condition; cub survival rates are falling. The IUCN lists polar bears as Vulnerable; some models predict a 30% population decline by 2050.
  • Walruses — use sea ice as a resting platform between foraging dives. Without ice, tens of thousands are forced ashore at once (a "haul-out"), causing deadly stampedes. In 2014, about 35,000 walruses hauled out near Point Lay, Alaska; such crowding can cause stampedes that kill calves.
  • Narwhals, beluga whales, and ringed seals — habitat and food sources disrupted. Killer whales are moving into newly ice-free Arctic waters, increasing predation on narwhals that were previously protected by sea ice.
  • Arctic cod — a key food source in the Arctic food web; its habitat is under pressure from warming and acidifying waters. Decline cascades through the food chain to seals, bears, and seabirds.

Impacts on Indigenous Communities:

  • Inuit peoples (Canada, Greenland, Alaska) have lived in the Arctic for thousands of years, developing deep cultural and practical knowledge of sea ice. An estimated 180,000 Inuit live in the Arctic.
  • Traditional hunting — Inuit hunt ringed seals, polar bears, walruses, and narwhals from sea ice. Unpredictable ice conditions make hunting more dangerous; hunters are falling through thinner ice more frequently, and search-and-rescue call-outs have increased in some communities as ice that was reliable for generations becomes unpredictable.
  • Food security — country food (traditional food obtained through hunting, fishing, foraging) provides a large share of the diet in many remote Inuit communities and is central to cultural identity. Declining wildlife availability is driving food insecurity; studies show increasing rates of food insecurity in Inuit communities.
  • Permafrost thaw — Inuit communities built on permafrost are seeing homes, roads, and water infrastructure sink and crack. Shishmaref, Alaska (600 Inuit residents) is losing its coastline rapidly to erosion; residents voted to relocate in 2016, at an estimated cost of around $180 million.
  • Cultural and spiritual loss — Inuit language has over 50 words for sea ice; entire vocabulary and knowledge systems tied to ice conditions are becoming obsolete. Elders describe a world that has become unrecognisable. The Inuit Circumpolar Council has submitted a petition (2005) to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, arguing that climate change violates their fundamental rights.
  • Positive economic impacts (contested) — the opening of the Northwest Passage as a shipping route and access to Arctic resources brings economic opportunity but also threatens the region with pollution and industrial development, compounding environmental pressures.

Conclusion: Arctic sea ice loss is a powerful example of how climate change disproportionately affects communities — particularly indigenous peoples — who have contributed least to GHG emissions. It also illustrates the concept of positive feedback loops: less ice → lower albedo → more absorption → more warming → less ice.

Case Study: Maldives and Pacific Islands — Sea Level Rise Threat

Location: The Maldives, an island nation in the Indian Ocean comprising 1,200 islands and 26 atolls; total population ~540,000. Also relevant: Kiribati, Tuvalu, Marshall Islands, and Funafuti (Tuvalu's capital, the most densely populated area anywhere in the Pacific).

The Physical Threat:

  • The Maldives has an average elevation of just 1.5 metres above sea level — 80% of land is less than 1 metre above sea level. It is one of the lowest-lying nations on Earth.
  • Sea level around the Maldives has risen by roughly 3–4mm per year since the 1990s, and the rate is accelerating.
  • IPCC projections: under a medium-emissions scenario, sea level could rise 0.4–0.6m by 2100 — enough to render most of the Maldives uninhabitable through storm surge, saltwater intrusion of freshwater, and coastal erosion even without permanent inundation.
  • High-tide and storm-surge flooding events are becoming more frequent on some atolls.
  • Tuvalu (area: 26 km²; 11,000 people) — storm surge events now regularly flood homes and contaminate the freshwater lens (thin layer of fresh groundwater). The country's highest natural point is 4.6m. In 2021, Tuvalu's foreign minister delivered a speech to COP26 standing knee-deep in seawater to illustrate the threat.

Social and Economic Impacts:

  • Tourism industry under threat — tourism accounts for 28% of Maldives' GDP. Coral reef bleaching (driven by ocean warming and acidification) is destroying the marine ecosystem that attracts visitors. Nearly all coral in the Maldives bleached in 1998 and again in 2016.
  • Freshwater crisis — saltwater intrusion into groundwater supplies contaminates the freshwater lens on which atolls depend. The Maldives now relies heavily on desalination — expensive and energy-intensive.
  • Forced relocation planning — in 2008 the Maldives' president proposed a fund to buy land abroad (e.g. in Sri Lanka, India or Australia) as a potential homeland of last resort. The government has announced plans to raise artificial islands (like Hulhumalé) and reclaim land to create higher ground.
  • Kiribati has purchased 6,000 acres of land in Fiji (the "Kiribati Purchase") which has been discussed as a possible relocation site for its ~120,000 citizens, although it is currently used mainly for food security.
  • Climate justice — the Maldives emits less than 0.01% of global CO₂. Its leaders have described sea level rise as an existential threat, and the Maldives has been a vocal advocate at COP summits for the 1.5°C target and for Loss and Damage funding.

Responses:

  • The Maldives government has invested heavily in sea walls, storm barriers, and elevated infrastructure, but these are short-term measures that cannot address long-term inundation.
  • The country aims to become carbon-neutral by 2030 — a symbolic gesture given its minimal emissions — to demonstrate international leadership.
  • The Alliance of Small Island States (AOSIS) — representing 44 countries — has been a powerful voice in international climate negotiations, advocating for the 1.5°C target enshrined in the Paris Agreement.

Exam point: The Maldives and Tuvalu are powerful examples for answering questions about climate justice, the differential impact of climate change, and the gap between those responsible for emissions and those who suffer the consequences.

9. Physical Impacts — Positive Feedback Loops

Positive feedback loops amplify warming and are one of the most alarming aspects of climate science. They mean that warming triggers further warming.

Other key positive feedback loops include:

  • Permafrost-methane feedback — as Arctic permafrost thaws, it releases methane and CO₂ stored for millennia (the permafrost carbon pool is estimated at 1,500 billion tonnes of carbon — nearly double the current atmospheric carbon stock). This additional CO₂ and methane causes further warming, thawing more permafrost.
  • Water vapour feedback — as temperatures rise, more water evaporates. Water vapour is itself a powerful greenhouse gas, amplifying warming. This approximately doubles the warming effect of CO₂ alone.
  • Forest dieback — warmer temperatures and changing rainfall patterns cause forest die-off and more wildfires. Dead/burning forest releases stored carbon, reducing carbon sink capacity and accelerating warming.

10. Future Projections and Uncertainty

  • The IPCC projects global average temperatures could rise by roughly 1.4°C to over 4°C above pre-industrial levels by 2100, depending on future emissions. Global temperatures in 2024 were already around 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels.
  • Sea levels rose by about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 and could rise by a further 0.3–1 m (or more) by 2100.
