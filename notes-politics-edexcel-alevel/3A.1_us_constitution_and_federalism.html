3A.1 US Constitution and Federalism

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3A: Comparative Politics — USA

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3A (USA); the alternative is 3B (Global Politics). You sit one, never both. The paper is Section A (one 12-mark from a choice of two), Section B (a compulsory 12-mark comparative question) and Section C (two 30-mark from a choice of three).

This topic underpins the whole of 3A: the Constitution's codification and entrenchment explain why the Supreme Court matters so much, why gridlock recurs, and why federalism has been contested for two centuries.

Codification — the constitution is collected in a single authoritative document.
Entrenchment — it can be amended only by a special and demanding procedure.
Enumerated powers — powers explicitly listed as belonging to the federal government.
Implied powers — powers inferred as necessary to carry out the enumerated ones.
Separation of powers — legislative, executive and judicial authority held by different institutions.
Checks and balances — each branch holds powers limiting the others.
Bipartisanship — co-operation between the two parties, which the system's supermajority requirements demand.
Limited government — government restricted by the Constitution to defined powers.
Federalism — power constitutionally divided between national and state governments.
Judicial review — the courts' power to declare acts unconstitutional.

The amendment process

Proposal: two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or a constitutional convention called by two-thirds of state legislatures (never used). Ratification: three-quarters of state legislatures, or state conventions (used once, for the 21st Amendment).

Only 27 amendments in over two centuries, and the first ten — the Bill of Rights — were effectively part of the original bargain. Later amendments of note: the 13th, 14th and 15th (Reconstruction); the 16th (income tax); the 19th (women's suffrage); the 22nd (two-term limit); the 26th (voting age 18).

The sharpest point about entrenchment is that difficulty of amendment does not prevent constitutional change — it determines who makes it. Because Article V is effectively unusable, the Supreme Court has become the de facto amending body, which is why confirmations carry the stakes they do.

Checks and balances in detail

  • Congress over the President: override a veto by two-thirds of both chambers; confirm or reject appointments (Senate); ratify treaties by two-thirds (Senate); appropriate all spending; investigate; impeach and try.
  • The President over Congress: the veto; the power to recommend legislation; executive orders and agreements; the bully pulpit.
  • The courts over both: judicial review of statutes and executive action.
  • The others over the courts: presidential nomination; Senate confirmation; impeachment of judges; constitutional amendment to reverse a ruling; Congress controls the size of the Court and most of its appellate jurisdiction.

Gridlock

Separate elections on different cycles produce divided government frequently, and no mechanism exists to resolve disagreement between institutions. Every significant power is shared, so each is a veto point. The Senate filibuster adds a sixty-vote threshold the Constitution nowhere requires. Polarisation removed the cross-party overlap that once supplied the coalitions the system demands.

Gridlock is therefore the intended consequence of a design meant to prevent tyranny by making concerted action difficult — but the framers did not anticipate disciplined national parties, which is what converted a check into a blockage.

PhaseCharacterDrivers
Dual federalism (to 1930s)Federal and state governments in largely separate spheres — "layer cake"Narrow reading of federal power
Co-operative federalism (1930s–60s)Overlapping responsibilities; federal grants shaping state policy — "marble cake"New Deal; Great Society; broad commerce clause readings
New Federalism (1970s–90s)Powers and discretion returned to states, notably through block grantsNixon, Reagan; the Rehnquist Court's revival of limits
ContemporarySharp policy divergence between states alongside continued federal reachPolarisation; Dobbs (2022); state-level divergence on drugs, elections, firearms

The constitutional machinery

  • Tenth Amendment — reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government nor prohibited to the states.
  • Necessary and proper clause — the basis of implied powers; McCulloch v Maryland (1819) upheld the national bank on it, establishing that the enumerated list is a foundation rather than a ceiling.
  • Commerce clause — read broadly from the 1930s to justify extensive federal regulation of economic and social life; it supplied part of the constitutional basis for the Civil Rights Act 1964. United States v Lopez (1995) reasserted a limit for the first time in decades.
  • Grants — categorical grants carry conditions tied to a defined purpose; block grants leave states discretion. Conditions attached to funding allow Washington to shape policy where it has no direct authority.

Debates about federalism

Federal power has grown too farStates retain real power
Fiscal capacity dwarfs the states, so the centre sets the termsStates control education, policing, family law, elections and much criminal law
Broad commerce and necessary-and-proper readings reached areas the enumerated powers never coveredLopez and later rulings revived limits
Grant conditions shape state policy indirectlyPolicy divergence between states is wider now than at any time since the New Deal
The change came through interpretation, which the states could not blockThe federal government cannot compel states to administer federal programmes

The judgement worth writing: federalism no longer protects a fixed sphere of state power, which is what the framers designed it to do. It now shelters policy divergence where no national majority exists — a substantial change in function. And it carries a cost the original conception did not: when fundamental rights vary by state, federalism protects the states at the expense of uniform citizenship, which is the tension the Fourteenth Amendment was adopted to resolve.

4. Interpretations and debates

Originalism holds that the Constitution means what it meant when adopted; it constrains judicial discretion but freezes meaning against social change. The living constitution approach holds that meaning evolves with contemporary values; it adapts but hands unelected judges what is effectively an amending power. The dispute is unresolvable within the terms of the document, which is why it maps onto political disagreement so neatly.
