1.2 Political Parties

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 1: UK Politics, Section A

Political parties are examined in Section A of Component 1. The spec divides the topic into four parts: what parties are and how they are funded; the three established parties; emerging and minor parties; and parties in context — which is where the evaluative marks sit. A 30-mark question here will almost always require you to connect party behaviour to the electoral system.

Political party — an organised group seeking to form government by putting up candidates and offering a programme.
Manifesto — the document setting out the policies a party pledges to enact if elected.
Mandate — the authority a governing party claims to implement the programme on which it was elected.
Old Labour (social democracy) — nationalisation, redistribution, strong trade unions, universal welfare.
New Labour (Third Way) — acceptance of the market economy combined with social investment and opportunity.
One Nation — the paternalist conservative tradition holding that the privileged owe obligations to the less fortunate.
New Right — neo-liberal economics combined with neo-conservative social policy.
Classical liberal — minimal state, negative freedom, free markets.
Modern liberal — enabling state, positive freedom, social justice.
Party systems — the number of parties with a realistic prospect of power and the pattern of competition between them.
Left wing / right wing — broadly, preference for equality and collective provision against preference for hierarchy, tradition and the market.

The surges matter analytically because they cut against a simple decline narrative. They were driven by a leadership contest open to members and by a constitutional question — which suggests people will join a party when membership visibly confers power, and will not when it does not.

The regulatory framework is the Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000, which created the Electoral Commission, required reporting of donations above a threshold, banned foreign donations and capped national campaign spending. It followed the Fifth Report of the Committee on Standards in Public Life (the Neill Report, 1998).

Why funding remains controversial

  • Loans rather than gifts (2006). Both major parties had accepted large loans that fell outside PPERA's disclosure rules because they were not, in law, donations. The Electoral Administration Act 2006 closed the gap by requiring loans to be reported on the same basis.
  • The "cash for honours" investigation (2006–07). A police investigation into whether peerages had been offered in return for loans concluded without charges, but the episode established the reputational problem: where a small number of large donors fund a party, the appearance of purchased access is difficult to dispel even where nothing unlawful occurred.
  • Trade union affiliation. The Trade Union Act 2016 required new members to opt in to a union's political fund rather than opt out, which was expected to reduce Labour's affiliated income. Whether this was a transparency measure or a partisan one was contested at the time.
  • Unincorporated associations. Money routed through associations whose own donors are not disclosed remains a recognised gap in the regime.

Funding questions reward specificity. Name PPERA 2000, name the Neill Report that produced it, name Short money, and note that the argument is not between "no state funding" and "state funding" but about how much more. That framing alone lifts an answer out of the generic.

Internal factions: One Nation members; free-market Thatcherites; a socially conservative and Eurosceptic right. The leadership rule — MPs narrow the field to two, members choose between them — gives the membership decisive influence and is one reason the parliamentary party and the membership can pull in different directions.

Internal structure: the National Executive Committee, the annual Conference, affiliated trade unions and the Parliamentary Labour Party each hold a share of power. The 2014 Collins review introduced one member one vote for leadership elections, which produced an outcome in 2015 that the reform's designers had not anticipated — a useful illustration that internal rules have unintended consequences.

Liberal Democrats

Formed in 1988 from the merger of the Liberal Party and the SDP. The party contains a classical liberal strand (the Orange Book tradition, market-oriented) and a social liberal strand (an enabling state). Constitutional reform — proportional representation, an elected Lords, entrenched rights — is its most consistent distinctive commitment.

Why minor parties struggle — and still matter

First-past-the-post rewards concentrated support and punishes dispersed support. The 2015 comparison is the standard illustration: UKIP took 12.6% of the vote for one seat; the SNP took 4.7% for fifty-six. Wasted-vote perceptions compound the effect, and broadcast allocation and funding follow past performance.

They matter nonetheless by shifting the agenda. Established parties adopt minor-party positions to stop losing votes, so influence appears in the policy of governments rather than in seat totals. UKIP is the decisive case: it never held more than two Commons seats, and it obtained a referendum on EU membership. That is a larger policy achievement than most governing parties manage in a term.

The UKIP example is the single most useful illustration in this topic because it works in three different questions: the failure of minor parties under FPTP, the influence of minor parties despite that failure, and the instability of the two-party system. Learn the two numbers — 12.6% and one seat — and the outcome.

4. UK political parties in context

Is the UK still a two-party system?

Still two-partyNo longer two-party
Labour and the Conservatives have alternated in office since 1945Coalition (2010) and minority government (2017) interrupted single-party rule
2015, 2019 and 2024 all produced single-party majoritiesThe SNP has dominated Scotland since 2015
Combined Con+Lab seat share remains overwhelmingCombined Con+Lab vote share fell from over 95% in 1951 to roughly 65–75%
Only those two parties can realistically form a governmentDevolved legislatures sustain genuine multi-party politics; Northern Ireland has an entirely separate system

The resolution worth writing: the UK now has multi-party competition at the level of votes and two-party dominance at the level of seats, and the gap between them is produced by the electoral system. That is a more precise answer than choosing one label, and it connects the topic to electoral systems, which is where the higher marks are.

Other party systems for comparison

  • Dominant-party system — one party wins repeatedly although others compete freely. Scotland since 2015 is a reasonable description.
  • Multi-party system — several parties hold power in coalition. The Senedd and the Scottish Parliament.
  • Two-and-a-half party system — two large parties plus a third whose support matters at the margin. A common description of the UK between the 1970s and 2010.

Factors affecting party success

  • Governing competence — the dominant valence consideration. Reputations lost through visible failure (the ERM crisis in 1992, the 2022 mini-budget) do not recover quickly.
  • Leadership — more consequential since partisan dealignment, as a shortcut for judging a party.
  • Unity — visible division signals inability to govern; the Conservatives in the 1990s and Labour after 2015.
  • Party funding and organisation — converts support into votes where it counts, through targeting and ground campaigns.
  • The media — agenda-setting determines the ground on which a campaign is fought.
  • The electoral system — efficient distribution matters more than raw vote share.

Have the parties converged?

Convergence was real between roughly 1994 and 2008: Labour accepted the market economy and the Conservatives accepted NHS spending, the minimum wage and equalities legislation, with both competing for the median voter as Downsian analysis predicts under FPTP. Divergence returned after 2015 over austerity, the size of the state, public ownership and Brexit — which cut across the conventional left–right axis entirely.

The deeper point is that the degree of convergence is itself determined by the electoral system and the distribution of opinion. FPTP rewards capturing the centre, so convergence is the default when opinion is unimodal, and divergence appears when a crisis or new cleavage splits the electorate in a way the centre cannot contain. Both parties therefore converge and diverge for the same underlying reason — the pursuit of a winning coalition.

For "To what extent have UK parties converged?", do not treat it as yes or no. Date the convergence (roughly 1994–2008), date the divergence (post-2015), and explain the mechanism. That converts a descriptive answer into an analytical one and gives you an AO3 judgement that follows from the analysis rather than being asserted at the end.
