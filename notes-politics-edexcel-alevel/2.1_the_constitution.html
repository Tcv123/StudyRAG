2.1 The Constitution

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 2: UK Government, Section A

This opens Component 2. The UK constitution is uncodified, unentrenched and unitary, and almost every question on the topic reduces to a consequence of one of those three words. Be precise about what each means before anything else.

Constitution — the set of rules determining where power lies, how it is exercised and what limits apply.
Uncodified — not collected into a single authoritative document. (Not "unwritten": most of it is written, just scattered.)
Unentrenched — constitutional law has no higher status than ordinary law, so it can be changed by a simple Act of Parliament.
Unitary — sovereignty is held centrally; regional power is devolved from the centre rather than constitutionally divided.
Parliamentary sovereignty — Parliament is the supreme law-making body; no Parliament binds its successors; no body may override an Act.
The rule of law — everyone is subject to the law, including government; law is applied equally and through fair process.
Statute law — Acts of Parliament, the highest source.
Common law — judge-made law built on precedent, plus the residual royal prerogative.
Conventions — unwritten rules of practice, binding in practice but not enforceable in court.
Authoritative works — texts of such standing that they guide practice.
Devolution — delegation of powers to subordinate bodies, with sovereignty retained at the centre.

Dicey identified parliamentary sovereignty and the rule of law as the twin pillars. The tension between them is examinable: if Parliament may legislate on anything, it may legislate contrary to the rule of law, and no court may stop it.

The pattern to state in an essay: reform dispersed power outwards (devolution), upwards (rights law) and sideways (a separate Supreme Court), but left the centre largely untouched — the executive's dominance of the Commons is essentially as it was.

Why the settlement is unstable

  • Asymmetry. Each settlement was negotiated separately in response to different demands, so the three differ in powers and structure. There is no general principle governing what is devolved, so each new demand is settled ad hoc.
  • The English question. England has no devolved legislature. The West Lothian question — whether MPs for devolved nations should vote on England-only matters — prompted English votes for English laws in 2015, abandoned in 2021 without resolving anything.
  • The ratchet. Each transfer has produced demands for more rather than settling the question; the Scotland Acts of 2012 and 2016 followed the 2014 referendum.
  • Unresolved sovereignty. Westminster retains the legal power to legislate in devolved areas and to repeal the settlements. The Sewel Convention says it will not "normally" do so without consent — a formulation tested repeatedly during the Brexit process, and held in Miller (2017) to be a convention the courts will not enforce.
"Quasi-federalism" is the term to use, and to justify: repealing devolution remains legally possible and politically near-unthinkable, so the powers behave as though entrenched without being so. That sentence does the analytical work in a devolution question.

4. Should the constitution be codified?

For codificationAgainst codification
Clarity — rules currently scattered across five sources collected in one documentFlexibility — the constitution has adapted continuously without any special procedure
Limits on executive power, addressing the "elective dictatorship" problemTransfers power to unelected judges, who would have the final word on contested political questions
Entrenched rights, protected from a simple majorityEntrenchment freezes arrangements against future adaptation
Could settle the territorial question and the English questionNo agreement on content — monarchy, Lords, electoral system, devolution are all contested
Courts could enforce constitutional limits directlyNo process exists for producing one with cross-party legitimacy

The strongest argument against is the practical one, and it is circular: a codified constitution would have to be agreed by the institutions whose own position it would determine. That is why codification has been discussed for decades without progressing.

Other reform debates

  • House of Lords — elected, partly elected, or reformed appointment. Failed in 2003 and 2012. Election supplies legitimacy and creates a rival mandate.
  • Electoral reform — settled for a generation by the 2011 referendum.
  • An English Parliament or regional assemblies — the 2004 North East referendum rejected regional devolution decisively; metro mayors have been the route taken instead.
  • A British Bill of Rights — proposed repeatedly as a replacement for the HRA; critics argue it would weaken protection rather than strengthen it.

5. Does parliamentary sovereignty still describe the UK?

Yes, legally. Parliament legislated to leave the EU, repealing the 1972 Act and demonstrating that even the deepest apparent constraint was reversible. It repealed the FTPA in 2022. No court may strike down primary legislation. Devolved powers rest on statutes Westminster could amend.

Not practically. Repealing devolution or ignoring a referendum result would be politically impossible. The HRA, international agreements and referendum expectations all constrain. Miller (2017) held prerogative could not remove statutory rights; Miller/Cherry (2019) held a prorogation frustrating Parliament's functions unlawful.

The judgement: sovereignty remains accurate as a description of legal authority and increasingly misleading as a description of how the constitution operates. The constraints are political rather than legal — real but unenforceable and unpredictable, since nobody can say in advance which formally available actions are politically impossible.

Quick recall

  • Uncodified, unentrenched, unitary — every question follows from one of these
  • Five sources: statute, common law, conventions, authoritative works, treaties
  • Dicey's twin pillars: parliamentary sovereignty and the rule of law — and they can conflict
  • Since 1997: devolution, HRA 1998, Lords Act 1999, FOI 2000, CRA 2005, FTPA 2011 (repealed 2022)
  • Reform dispersed power outwards, upwards and sideways — not at the centre
  • Quasi-federal: legally reversible, politically near-unthinkable
  • Sewel Convention is not enforceable — Miller (2017)
  • Codification's decisive obstacle is circular: the institutions it would bind must agree it
