3B.4 Power and Developments

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3B: Global Politics

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3B (Global Politics); the alternative is 3A (Comparative Politics: USA). You sit one, never both.

This topic covers the distribution of power in the international system and how it has changed. It supplies the raw material for most Section C essays on this route, because almost every question about global governance reduces to a question about who has power and what they do with it.

Hard power — military or economic coercion to change another actor's behaviour.
Soft power — obtaining preferred outcomes through attraction, resting on culture, values and perceived legitimacy.
Smart power — combining hard and soft instruments in a coherent strategy.
Structural power — the ability to shape the frameworks within which others operate.
Polarity — how power is distributed among the major states.
Hegemon — a state with sufficient preponderance to set and underwrite the rules of the order.
Failed state — one that can no longer perform the basic functions of government.
Rogue state — a state held to disregard international norms.
Emerging power — a state whose relative capability is rising significantly.
Human Development Index — composite measure of life expectancy, education and income.

1. Types of power

TypeInstrumentsLimits
HardArmed force, threat of force, sanctions, economic inducementExpensive; may fail to produce political outcomes — Afghanistan and Iraq
SoftCulture, political values, foreign policy perceived as legitimate, education, mediaCannot be directed at will; works slowly; rarely shifts vital interests; easily destroyed by conduct

StructuralSetting rules, standards, the reserve currency, technological normsConcentrated in very few states; slow to build, slow to lose

Nye's point about soft power is often misunderstood: its effect is to make others willing rather than coerced, so it appears as an absence of resistance rather than as a visible outcome. That makes it real but very hard to measure, which is its central analytical weakness.

2. Polarity

SystemDescriptionStability argument
UnipolarOne preponderant state — the US after 1991Stable if the hegemon underwrites the order; invites balancing and overreach
BipolarTwo dominant blocs — the Cold WarRealists argue it is most stable: a clear, calculable adversary reduces miscalculation
MultipolarSeveral comparable powers — Europe before 1914More flexible balancing, but shifting alignments increase uncertainty and the risk of miscalculation

Is the system now multipolar?

For: China's economic scale and sustained military modernisation; a reassertive Russia willing to use force; regional powers — India, Brazil, Turkey, Saudi Arabia — acting with increasing autonomy and declining to align, as responses to the war in Ukraine illustrated; relative American decline in share of global output.

Against: American military reach remains unmatched; the dollar's role as the reserve currency gives Washington financial leverage no rival possesses; its alliance network has no equivalent; it retains leadership in the technologies that matter most.

The judgement: power is now distributed unevenly across dimensions — the system is economically multipolar, militarily closer to unipolar, and institutionally somewhere between. No single label fits, and saying so is a better answer than choosing one.

3. The significance of states

  • Great powers — the P5, plus economically significant states without permanent seats (Germany, Japan, India).
  • Emerging powers — BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) and others; notable for building parallel institutions such as the AIIB and the New Development Bank rather than attacking existing ones.
  • Middle powers — influence through diplomacy, coalition-building and specialisation.
  • Failed and fragile states — generate consequences that cross borders.

Why rising powers have not overturned the order

They benefited from the trade and financial order they inherited, so they press for greater voice within institutions while building parallel ones, rather than confronting the order directly. That is an important corrective to simple power-transition narratives, and it is evidence for the liberal claim that institutions socialise as well as constrain.

Democratisation and its reversal

The "third wave" from the 1970s saw a large expansion in the number of electoral democracies. It has stalled and partly reversed, and the mechanism matters: contemporary democratic decline rarely takes the form of a coup. Elected leaders weaken courts, curtail media independence, alter electoral rules and hollow out institutions while retaining elections — which makes the process gradual, deniable and difficult to resist.

Contributing factors: the credibility of authoritarian alternatives delivering growth; the record of imposed democratisation in Iraq and Afghanistan; performance failures in established democracies after 2008; and reduced external pressure, since alternative sources of finance allow authoritarian governments to avoid democratic conditionality.

Development

Measured by GDP per capita, the HDI (life expectancy, education, income) and the Sustainable Development Goals. The examinable debate is whether development requires democracy: China's sustained growth is the standard counter-example, and it weakens the modernisation assumption that economic development produces democratisation.

5. Evaluation

Does economic interdependence prevent conflict?

Liberal claimRealist reply
War between integrated economies imposes severe costs, raising the threshold for forceBritain and Germany were each other's major trading partners before 1914
Trade creates constituencies with an interest in peaceDependence creates vulnerability: energy supply and export controls are coercive instruments
Institutions supply channels for managing disputesSecurity concerns override economic cost — the cost of the Ukraine war to Russia was foreseeable and did not deter
It reduces the probability of conflict at the marginMajor powers are now deliberately decoupling in strategic sectors, reversing the mechanism

Is power becoming harder to measure?

Yes, for three compounding reasons: power is distributed unevenly across dimensions; new domains — cyber capability, supply chain leverage, standard-setting, information influence — do not reduce to conventional metrics; and power is relational, so the capacity to achieve one objective does not transfer to another. Overwhelming military superiority did not deliver American objectives in Afghanistan. If power is the ability to produce intended outcomes, it can only be assessed after the event and against a specific objective — which is why confident claims about polarity should be treated sceptically.

Quick recall

  • Unipolar, bipolar, multipolar — realists generally argue bipolarity is most stable
  • The system is economically multipolar, militarily closer to unipolar — no single label fits
  • BRICS build parallel institutions (AIIB, NDB) rather than attacking existing ones
  • Democratic decline now happens through elected leaders hollowing out institutions, not coups
  • HDI = life expectancy + education + income
  • Pre-1914 interdependence is the standard case against the liberal peace claim
  • Power is relational: military superiority did not deliver outcomes in Afghanistan
