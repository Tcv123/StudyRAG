3A.3 US Presidency

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3A: Comparative Politics — USA

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3A (USA); the alternative is 3B (Global Politics). You sit one, never both.

Article II is remarkably short. The modern presidency rests far more on informal sources of power than on what the Constitution grants — which is why presidential power varies so sharply between holders and why the "imperial presidency" debate exists.

Formal powers — those set out in Article II.
Informal powers — authority derived from mandate, persuasion, media and party leadership.
Executive order — a directive to the federal bureaucracy with the force of law, issued without Congress.
Executive agreement — an international agreement not requiring Senate ratification.
Signing statement — a President's recorded interpretation of a statute on signing it.
Imperial presidency — Schlesinger's term for power accumulated beyond constitutional intention.
Imperilled presidency — the counter-claim that the office is too constrained to govern.
EXOP — the Executive Office of the President; the institutional centre.
Lame duck — a President whose authority has ebbed towards the end of a term.

Note what is absent: the President cannot introduce legislation, cannot dissolve Congress, and cannot compel it to act. Every significant formal power is shared.

2. Informal powers

  • Executive orders — allow significant policy without legislation, especially when Congress is deadlocked. Limits: a successor may revoke; courts may strike down; Congress may withhold funds.
  • Executive agreements — avoid the two-thirds Senate threshold; far more numerous than treaties, and equally reversible by a successor.
  • Signing statements — record how a statute will be interpreted and enforced.
  • The electoral mandate — a decisive win strengthens the claim to speak for the country and improves bargaining leverage.
  • The bully pulpit — command of national attention to shape the agenda and pressure Congress.
  • Party leadership — a President's party in Congress is far more supportive, so party control is decisive.
  • EXOP and the Cabinet — the institutional centre. The US Cabinet advises; it does not decide, and the President is not bound by it.

Neustadt: the power to persuade

Neustadt argued that the formal powers are too sparse to govern by command: Congress, the courts, the states and the bureaucracy are all actors a President cannot direct. Effective power therefore consists in bargaining — convincing others that what the President wants serves their own interest. A President reduced to issuing commands has already failed, because commanding shows persuasion was unavailable. The argument holds even in an era of executive action, since orders still depend on a bureaucracy to implement, courts to uphold and Congress to fund.

4. Limits on presidential power

  • Congress — the purse, legislation, confirmation, override, investigation, impeachment.
  • The courts — executive orders struck down; Youngstown as the constitutional benchmark.
  • Federalism — substantial policy areas belong to the states.
  • The bureaucracy — implementation friction.
  • Term limits — the 22nd Amendment confines a President to two elected terms; authority ebbs towards lame-duck status.
  • Public opinion and the media — leverage over Congress depends on standing with the electorate.

Why impeachment is a weak check

Impeachment by the House needs a simple majority; conviction in the Senate needs two-thirds. In a polarised Senate that threshold cannot be reached unless a substantial number of the President's own party defect, and senators face primary electorates loyal to the President. No President has ever been removed by the Senate; Nixon resigned before a vote he would probably have lost, which is the exception proving the rule — his party's support had collapsed. The ultimate constitutional check is therefore unusable precisely against a President who retains his party's support.

Strikes on Iranian nuclear sites (June 2025) without congressional authorisationLearning Resources v Trump (20 February 2026, 6–3): the Supreme Court struck down Trump's tariffs imposed under emergency powers (IEEPA)

The judgement: power has expanded unevenly. In war, national security and emergency, presidential authority is close to unchecked in practice. In domestic policy it depends entirely on party control of Congress. And the instruments Presidents have gained are weaker than legislation: orders are reversed, struck down or defunded, so each administration undoes its predecessor's work. The presidency is more powerful at any given moment and less able to achieve anything lasting — a change in the character of the office rather than a simple increase in its power.

For a UK/US comparison, the precise formulation is worth memorising: the UK Prime Minister is more powerful domestically and less secure in office; the US President is less able to legislate and cannot be removed by their own party. Fusion concentrates power and makes the leader dependent on the party; separation disperses power and makes the leader independent of it.

Quick recall

  • Article II: veto, Commander-in-Chief, appointments, treaties, pardon, take care, recommend measures
  • The President cannot introduce legislation or dissolve Congress
  • Informal: executive orders and agreements, signing statements, mandate, bully pulpit, party leadership
  • Neustadt: power is the power to persuade
  • Youngstown (1952) — the courts can stop executive action
  • 22nd Amendment: two elected terms
  • Impeachment: House majority, Senate two-thirds — no President ever removed
  • Judgement: expanded unevenly; instruments gained, durability lost
