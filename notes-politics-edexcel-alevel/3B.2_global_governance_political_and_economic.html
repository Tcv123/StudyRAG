3B.2 Global Governance: Political and Economic

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3B: Global Politics

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3B (Global Politics); the alternative is 3A (Comparative Politics: USA). You sit one, never both.

This topic covers the institutions that manage international peace, security and the world economy. The recurring analytical question is whether they constrain the powerful or merely reflect them — which is the realist–liberal dispute in concrete form.

Global governance — managing shared problems through rules and institutions without a world government.
Collective security — an attack on one is treated as an attack on all.
Veto — the power of each permanent Security Council member to block a substantive resolution.
Conditionality — policy conditions attached to loans.
Structural adjustment — liberalising reforms required of borrowing states.
Washington Consensus — privatisation, deregulation, fiscal discipline and liberalisation as a policy package.
North–South divide — the economic gap between wealthier industrialised states and poorer developing ones.
Dependency theory — underdevelopment caused by poorer states' structural position in a global economy organised around the wealthy core.
Global civil society — NGOs and transnational networks acting across borders.

Peacekeeping

The UN maintains no standing force and depends on member contributions. Mandates are usually limited by the consent required from parties to the conflict, and missions are frequently under-resourced. Successes exist — Namibia, Liberia, Sierra Leone — alongside the failures at Rwanda and Srebrenica that define the institution's reputation.

NATO

A collective defence alliance founded in 1949. Article 5 provides that an armed attack on one member is an attack on all — invoked once, after 11 September 2001, by the United States, for an alliance designed to protect Europe. It has outlasted the threat it was created to meet by adapting: enlarging eastward, taking on crisis management and counter-terrorism, and finding renewed purpose as great power competition returned.

The Bretton Woods critique

  • Weighted voting. Power is proportional to financial contribution, so wealthy states control institutions lending mainly to poorer ones. By convention the IMF is headed by a European and the World Bank by an American.
  • Structural adjustment. Liberalising conditions — privatisation, deregulation, devaluation, spending cuts — applied with limited regard to local circumstances, frequently falling on health, education and subsidies.
  • Ideological uniformity. The Washington Consensus treated one model as universally applicable, despite successful East Asian developers having used extensive state direction of the kind the conditions prohibited.
  • Accountability. Neither institution answers to the populations affected by its conditions.

The defence: they lend where private markets will not; conditionality is intended to ensure repayment and address the causes of crisis; and both have moderated, giving more weight to poverty reduction and local ownership since around 2000.

The WTO's difficulties

The Doha Round collapsed without agreement: consensus among 160-plus members with divergent interests makes comprehensive new agreements effectively impossible. More seriously, the Appellate Body ceased to function when the United States blocked appointments from 2019, so appeals cannot be heard and the binding character of WTO rulings has been substantially undermined. Frustrated states have turned to regional and bilateral deals, fragmenting the system the WTO was created to unify.

This is the strongest single piece of evidence for realism on this paper: the most legalised and enforceable part of global economic governance was disabled by one state withdrawing consent.

4. Global civil society and non-state actors

  • Agenda-setting — landmines, debt relief through Jubilee 2000, and climate all advanced through civil society campaigning before governments acted.
  • Expertise — Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and the International Crisis Group supply information states lack or will not collect about one another.
  • Monitoring — documenting violations and publicising non-compliance, which states will not do to each other for diplomatic reasons.
  • Delivery — humanitarian relief and development programmes, sometimes exceeding official agencies in scale.
  • Criticism — NGOs are not democratically accountable and are frequently funded by developed-world donors, so their priorities may reflect those donors rather than the populations they claim to represent.

5. Evaluation

Why economic governance has been more effective than security governance: economic institutions have rules, monitoring and dispute settlement backed by material incentives, while security decisions run through a Council where a single veto blocks action. Economic co-operation is also positive-sum, repeated and divisible, so states have ongoing reputational incentives to comply; security questions are frequently zero-sum, concern survival, and arise as one-off crises.

The realist qualification: the WTO's collapse suggests the distinction is less fundamental than it appears. Both work while the strongest states are content; the economic sphere merely took longer to reach the point where they were not.

  • Peacekeeping depends on member contributions and party consent — Namibia and Sierra Leone against Rwanda and Srebrenica
  • NATO 1949; Article 5 invoked once, after 9/11
  • IMF (stabilisation) / World Bank (development) / WTO (trade rules) — all weighted or consensus-bound
  • Washington Consensus; structural adjustment; dependency theory; world-systems
  • WTO Appellate Body paralysed since 2019 — the best evidence for realism on this paper
  • G7 advanced economies; G20 includes emerging powers, coordinated the 2008 response
  • NGOs: agenda-setting, expertise, monitoring, delivery — but not democratically accountable
