3B.5 Regionalism and the EU

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3B: Global Politics — spec topic 6: Regionalism and the European Union

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3B (Global Politics); the alternative is 3A (Comparative Politics: USA). You sit one, never both.
The 2017 spec predates withdrawal. The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020 and the transition ended on 31 December 2020. The EU remains fully examinable as the most developed case of regionalism — write UK membership in the past tense.

Regionalism is co-operation between states within a geographical area through shared institutions. The EU is the case study because it has gone furthest, and because it is the only body where states have accepted genuinely supranational authority.

Regionalism — co-operation between states within a geographical area through shared institutions.
Supranationalism — institutions can take decisions binding on members, including against an individual member's wishes.
Intergovernmentalism — states co-operate while retaining ultimate control, typically through unanimity.
Free trade area — tariffs removed between members; each sets its own external tariff.
Customs union — adds a common external tariff.
Single market — free movement of goods, services, capital and people, with harmonised regulation.
Monetary union — a shared currency and central bank.
Neofunctionalism — integration advances by spillover from one sector to adjacent ones.
Democratic deficit — decisions taken by bodies insufficiently accountable to citizens.
Subsidiarity — decisions should be taken at the lowest effective level.

PoliticalShared institutions and, at the furthest extent, supranational lawThe EU; to a far lesser degree the AU and ASEAN

Economic regionalism has spread most widely because trade liberalisation offers measurable mutual gains without requiring states to surrender authority over identity, security or law. That single sentence explains the pattern across every region.

The Court's doctrines of direct effect and supremacy are what made the EU legal order genuinely supranational: EU law became enforceable in national courts at the suit of individuals, converting treaty obligations into rights citizens could claim. No other regional body has this.

3. The democratic deficit

The chargeThe reply
The Commission is appointed yet holds the sole right of initiativeThe Commission is approved by the elected Parliament and appointed by elected governments
The Council meets with limited transparencyMinisters in the Council are accountable to national parliaments and electorates
The Parliament was historically the weakest institutionIts powers have grown substantially through successive treaties; it now co-legislates in most areas
Low turnout; elections fought on domestic issuesNational parliaments gained a subsidiarity scrutiny role under Lisbon
No European demos with shared media and parties competing for officeThis is a criticism of European society, not of the institutions

The disagreement underneath: whether accountability through twenty-seven national democracies is sufficient for decisions taken collectively, or whether supranational decisions require supranational democracy.

4. The eurozone crisis

The structural flaw was monetary union without fiscal union. Members shared a currency and a central bank but retained separate national budgets, so there was no mechanism for fiscal transfers of the kind that operates within a single state. Indebted members could not devalue, so adjustment had to occur through internal devaluation — austerity — which is slow and socially costly.

Resolution was difficult because both sides had democratic mandates: creditor electorates were unwilling to underwrite what they saw as others' irresponsibility; debtor electorates were unwilling to accept prolonged austerity. The no-bailout clause made the legal basis contested until the ECB's 2012 commitment to do "whatever it takes". Banking supervision has since been integrated; the fiscal question has not been resolved.

5. The EU as a global actor

DimensionStrengthLimit
EconomicOne of the world's largest economies and a single market of about 450 million; the Commission negotiates trade for all membersTrade deals are slow to ratify and can be held up by individual members
Structural / regulatoryThe Brussels effect: firms worldwide adopt EU standards, e.g. GDPR (2018) on data protectionRules can be resisted by the US and China, and critics say regulation weakens competitiveness
PoliticalSanctions on Russia since 2014 and repeated packages after 2022; Ukraine made a candidate state; the largest collective aid donorCommon Foreign and Security Policy needs unanimity, so one state (Hungary) can block or delay action
MilitarySmall crisis-management missions; defence spending rising since 2022No EU army; European security depends on NATO and the US

The classic judgement is that the EU is an “economic giant but a military dwarf”: highly significant as a trade and regulatory power, much less so where power depends on force. Its significance as an international actor therefore varies by issue, which fits a world where power is distributed unevenly across dimensions.

6. Evaluation

Does regionalism threaten sovereignty?

Members accepted binding law and majority voting in defined areas — a genuine constraint. But they chose it by treaty, and one has since left, which supports the description of pooled rather than surrendered sovereignty. The honest qualification: the distinction is clear in principle and harder to sustain the deeper integration goes, because the cost of exit rises until the option becomes theoretical.

The counter-intuitive point worth making: regionalism increases the effective sovereignty of smaller states. A medium-sized European state negotiating with the US or China alone has little leverage; acting through a bloc of 450 million consumers it shapes global regulatory standards. If sovereignty means the capacity to shape outcomes rather than formal freedom from constraint, the sovereignty objection is strongest for powerful states and weakest for small ones.

What Brexit shows about regionalism

  • Integration is reversible — a correction to neofunctionalist accounts implying a ratchet.
  • National politics constrains integration — a domestic coalition can override the economic logic of membership, supporting intergovernmentalist accounts.
  • But exit is costly — the complexity of withdrawal and the Northern Ireland border problem demonstrated how deep integration had become.
  • The Brussels effect persists — non-members align with a large bloc's standards to trade with it, so leaving recovers formal sovereignty while leaving much practical constraint in place.
  • No imitation followed — support for membership rose in remaining states, suggesting exit may strengthen rather than weaken a bloc.

Does regionalism strengthen global governance?

Yes: regional bodies solve collective action problems at a scale where agreement is achievable, and aggregate smaller states into blocs that can negotiate globally. Given the paralysis of the WTO and the Security Council, they now do much of the work global institutions were designed for.

No: overlapping and inconsistent agreements fragment the global trading system; blocs may become competing power centres; and the strongest regional bodies are those of already-wealthy regions.

Judgement: regionalism succeeds at what global governance finds easy and cannot substitute for it where global governance is genuinely necessary — a bloc that decarbonises alone bears the cost and shares the benefit. Its growth is therefore evidence of the problem rather than a solution to it.
