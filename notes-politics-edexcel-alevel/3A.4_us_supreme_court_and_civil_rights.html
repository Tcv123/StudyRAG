3A.4 US Supreme Court and Civil Rights

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3A: Comparative Politics — USA

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3A (USA); the alternative is 3B (Global Politics). You sit one, never both.

The Supreme Court is the most powerful judiciary in the democratic world, and the contrast with the UK Supreme Court — which cannot strike down an Act of Parliament — is the single most examinable comparison on this paper.

Judicial review — the power to declare acts of Congress, the executive or the states unconstitutional and void.
Judicial activism — willingness to strike down law, revisit precedent and give effect to broad principle.
Judicial restraint — deference to the elected branches; narrow rulings; close adherence to precedent.
Originalism / strict constructionism — the Constitution means what it meant when adopted.
Living constitution / loose constructionism — meaning evolves with contemporary values.
Stare decisis — adherence to precedent.
Incorporation — applying the Bill of Rights against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.
Affirmative action — preference for members of groups historically subject to discrimination.
Imperial judiciary — the charge that the Court has taken powers properly belonging to elected bodies.

1. Nature and role

Nine justices, appointed for life during good behaviour. The Court hears roughly 60–80 cases a year from thousands of petitions; it chooses its docket through the "rule of four" — four justices must agree to hear a case.

Since Barrett's confirmation (2020) the Court has had a 6–3 conservative majority: six justices appointed by Republican presidents, three of them by Trump.

Judicial review appears nowhere in the Constitution. It was claimed by the Court itself in Marbury v Madison (1803), when Chief Justice Marshall held that it is "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is", and that a law repugnant to the Constitution is void. The Court's most significant power therefore rests on its own assertion — accepted by the other branches for two centuries, and now as settled as anything in the constitutional order.

Why confirmations became so contested

  • Life tenure. A justice appointed in their fifties may serve thirty years or more, so one appointment fixes policy for a generation — stakes no election can reverse.
  • The Court decides divisive questions. Abortion, guns, campaign finance, voting rights and affirmative action are settled judicially rather than legislatively.
  • The filibuster's removal. A President whose party holds the Senate can confirm without any opposition support, removing the incentive to nominate a consensus candidate.
  • Norm erosion. The refusal to consider the Garland nomination in 2016 and the rapid confirmation of Barrett in 2020 established that the process is governed by power rather than settled convention.

Why rulings do not always change practice

The Court has neither an army nor a budget and depends on the executive, the states and lower courts to implement. Brown met massive resistance across the South, and its follow-up requirement of desegregation "with all deliberate speed" was exploited to delay for years. Desegregation advanced decisively only once Congress passed the Civil Rights Act 1964 and the executive enforced it — which indicates the Court can remove a legal obstacle but cannot supply the enforcement that makes a right real.

4. Civil liberties and rights

  • The Bill of Rights — the first ten amendments; incorporated against the states progressively through the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause.
  • The Fourteenth Amendment — due process and equal protection; the basis of most modern rights litigation.
  • Race and rights — from Dred Scott and Plessy through Brown, the Civil Rights Act 1964 and the Voting Rights Act 1965, to Shelby County (2013), after which covered states enacted restrictions the Act had prevented.
  • Affirmative action — Bakke (1978) prohibited quotas but allowed race as one factor; Grutter (2003) upheld this; Students for Fair Admissions (2023) effectively ended the practice.

Race and rights: campaigns and effectiveness

  • Methods — litigation (the NAACP's strategy culminating in Brown); direct action and protest (the 1960s marches and boycotts; Black Lives Matter from 2013 and on a mass scale in 2020); and lobbying Congress and the states.
  • Effectiveness — the civil rights movement secured the Civil Rights Act 1964 and Voting Rights Act 1965; BLM shifted public debate and some state and city policing policy, but federal police reform (the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act) did not pass. Court rulings since 2013 — Shelby County, SFFA and Louisiana v Callais — have narrowed earlier gains.
  • Representation — Congress has become more racially diverse with each recent election, with record numbers of Black and Hispanic members, though both groups remain under-represented relative to population, especially in the Senate.
  • Immigration reform — comprehensive reform has repeatedly failed in Congress (2007; the Senate-passed 2013 bill died in the House), so policy has been made by executive action, such as DACA (2012), and reversed between administrations — from Obama to Trump, Biden and Trump's second-term deportation drive.

The affirmative action debate

For: formal neutrality leaves intact the accumulated effects of generations of legal discrimination in education, housing and employment, so equal treatment from now on cannot produce equal outcomes. Against: classifying individuals by race to remedy racial discrimination is in tension with the equal protection principle the remedy invokes. The underlying disagreement is whether the Fourteenth Amendment requires colour-blindness or permits race-conscious remedies for race-based injustice — a question about what equality means.

Is it political or judicial?

Nominated for philosophy, confirmed on party lines, deciding questions where outcomes track composition — Dobbs overturned a fifty-year precedent after a change in personnel rather than in law. Against that: it must reason from text and precedent, give public reasons, and justices frequently vote against the expectations of the President who appointed them; many decisions are unanimous. The distinction may be false: interpreting contested constitutional provisions cannot avoid judgements about values, which makes the Court unavoidably political in its consequences without making its method arbitrary.

The activism/restraint distinction is worth handling carefully for AO3 marks. It describes a real difference in willingness to strike down law, but in practice the label is applied to rulings the speaker dislikes — the Warren Court's activism advanced civil rights, and later conservative majorities have been equally willing to overturn precedent. Saying so shows you can evaluate the vocabulary, not just use it.
