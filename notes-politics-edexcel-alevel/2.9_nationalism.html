2.9 Nationalism

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 2: Non-core Political Ideas, Section B

This is one of five non-core ideas. You sit one of Anarchism, Ecologism, Feminism, Multiculturalism or Nationalism, answering a single 24-mark "To what extent" question (AO1 8 / AO2 8 / AO3 8). Learn your chosen idea properly rather than all five thinly.

Nationalism holds that humanity divides naturally into nations, and that nations are entitled to govern themselves. It has been the most politically consequential idea of the modern era — and the most double-edged, because the same premise underwrote liberation from empire and the subordination of minorities within the states that liberation created.

Nation — a community bound by shared culture, history, language or identity, which may or may not have its own state.
State — a political and legal entity with defined territory, a permanent population, a government and sovereignty.
Nation-state — a state whose borders coincide with a national community.
Self-determination — the principle that a nation has the right to govern itself.
Civic nationalism — the nation defined by shared political values, institutions and citizenship.
Ethnic nationalism — the nation defined by common descent, culture and ancestry.
Cultural nationalism — emphasis on preserving a nation's language and customs, not necessarily on statehood.
Volksgeist — Herder's term for the distinctive spirit of a people, expressed in language and custom.
Integral nationalism — an authoritarian, organic conception in which the individual is wholly subordinate to the nation.
Chauvinism — belief in the superiority of one's own nation over others.
Imagined community — Anderson's description of the nation as a community members imagine, since they will never meet most of it.

Nation and state distinguished

A nation is a sociological and psychological entity; a state is a legal and political one. They frequently do not coincide: the Kurds are a nation without a state; the UK is a state containing several nations; many post-colonial states contain multiple nations within borders drawn by others. Nationalism is the doctrine that the two should coincide — which is why it has been the most destabilising principle in modern politics.

3. Key thinkers

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–78) — The Social Contract (1762). The general will: sovereignty belongs to the people as a collective body with a common interest, distinct from the sum of private wills. He gave nationalism its foundation of popular sovereignty — the idea that legitimate authority flows from the people rather than from a monarch or dynasty. He is not himself straightforwardly a nationalist, but no nationalism is possible without his premise.
Johann Gottfried Herder (1744–1803) — cultural nationalism. Each nation possesses a distinctive Volksgeist, a spirit expressed through its language, customs, folklore and history, which should be cherished and preserved. His nationalism is pluralist rather than hierarchical: every culture has its own value, and none is superior. It does not necessarily demand a state — the objective is the flourishing of the culture.
Giuseppe Mazzini (1805–72) — liberal nationalism. Nations should achieve unification and independence and then co-operate as free equals; a Europe of free nations would be a peaceful one. Nationhood carries duties as well as rights — to one's own nation and, through it, to humanity. Central to Italian unification, and the clearest case of nationalism as a liberating rather than an oppressive force.
Marcus Garvey (1887–1940) — black nationalism and pan-Africanism. Liberation requires racial pride, economic self-reliance and a common political project among people of African descent, including the possibility of return to Africa. Founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association and the Black Star Line. Significant because he extends national self-determination to a people dispersed by slavery rather than occupying a single territory, and because psychological liberation — pride, recovered history — is treated as a precondition of political liberation.
Charles Maurras (1868–1952) — integral nationalism. The nation is an organic whole to which the individual is wholly subordinate; its interests override individual rights entirely. Anti-democratic, monarchist and authoritarian, associated with Action Française. He is on the specification precisely to show nationalism's dark side: the same premise that liberated Italy underwrites the subordination of the individual to the nation.
The five thinkers are chosen to span the range — Rousseau supplies the foundation, Herder the cultural version, Mazzini the liberal, Garvey the anti-colonial, Maurras the authoritarian. A 24-mark answer should use that spread as evidence: the fact that one doctrine accommodates Mazzini and Maurras is the strongest single point about nationalism's nature.

Are nations natural or constructed?

Primordialists hold that national identities are ancient and organic, with demonstrable continuity of language and custom over long periods. Modernists — Anderson's "imagined communities", Gellner, Hobsbawm's "invented traditions" — hold that nations are products of modernity: print capitalism, standardised national languages, mass education and newspapers allowed large populations to conceive of themselves as a single community moving through history together.

Anderson's point is not that nations are false. Members will never meet most fellow nationals, so belonging must be imagined — as it must in any community beyond the face-to-face. An imagined community commands genuine loyalty and sacrifice, and its political effects are entirely real. The analysis explains nationalism's force rather than dismissing it, and it undercuts only the nationalist's own claim that the nation is primordial.

5. Evaluation

Is nationalism a coherent ideology?

NoYes
Has underwritten liberal republicanism, anti-colonial socialism and fascism alikeA consistent core: humanity divides into nations; national identity is a primary loyalty; nations should govern themselves
No canonical text, no agreed account of human nature, no theory of how the economy or society should be organisedThose are substantive claims, denied by liberals (the individual is primary), socialists (class is primary) and cosmopolitans (borders carry no moral weight)
Supplies no criterion for identifying which groups are nations — the one question it exists to answerIt answers a different question from other ideologies: what the political community is, not how it should be organised

The judgement: nationalism is coherent as a claim about the unit of politics and incoherent as a programme, and the second follows from the first. Like ecologism, it is cross-cutting rather than free-standing: it supplies premises that must fuse with a social analysis before they generate policy. Measuring it against liberalism or socialism is measuring it against ideologies answering a different question.

Why the consequences have been so varied

The deepest point, and the best way to end an essay: nationalism supplies no principle for determining which groups count as nations entitled to self-government, so every nationalism must at some point deny to others the claim it makes for itself. Anti-colonial movements invoked self-determination against empire; the states they created then faced the identical claim from minorities within their borders. That is not a contingent failing but a structural feature — which is why nationalism has been both the most liberating and the most destructive political force of the modern era, frequently in the same place within a generation.
