2.8 Multiculturalism

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 2: Non-core Political Ideas, Section B

This is one of five non-core ideas. You sit one of Anarchism, Ecologism, Feminism, Multiculturalism or Nationalism, answering a single 24-mark "To what extent" question (AO1 8 / AO2 8 / AO3 8). Learn your chosen idea properly rather than all five thinly.

Multiculturalism is concerned with how a society should respond to cultural and religious diversity. Its central claim is that formal equality is not enough, because institutions built around majority norms are not neutral in effect — and its central difficulty is that accommodating a group may mean failing the individuals within it.

Multiculturalism — positive endorsement of diversity, holding that minorities should maintain their identities while participating fully in public life.
Assimilation — the expectation that minorities abandon their distinctive culture and adopt the majority's.
Integration — participation in common institutions while retaining cultural identity.
Value pluralism — there are several genuine human goods that may conflict and cannot all be realised together.
Politics of recognition — identity is formed dialogically, so misrecognition is a form of harm.
Politics of difference — equal treatment may require recognising and accommodating group differences.
Minority rights — entitlements held by cultural groups or by individuals as members of them.
Community of communities — Parekh's conception of a society composed of interacting cultural groups.
Cosmopolitan multiculturalism — identity as fluid and chosen, drawing on several traditions.

1. Core ideas and principles

PrincipleWhat it means
Diversity as a goodCultural variety enriches a society rather than being a problem to be managed away
Identity and belongingCultural membership is constitutive of who people are, not an optional attachment
RecognitionEqual standing requires public acknowledgement of identity, not official indifference to it
Minority rightsExemptions, accommodations and support that correct an existing asymmetry rather than granting a privilege
Tolerance and beyondToleration merely permits; multiculturalism requires positive endorsement and engagement

Why formal equality is said to be insufficient

  • Neutral rules are not neutral in effect. The working week and public holidays are built around a Christian calendar; dress codes around majority norms; curricula around majority history. Applying identical rules imposes costs on minorities the majority does not bear.
  • Difference-blindness entrenches the status quo. Treating culture as irrelevant means existing arrangements go unexamined, and only departures from them appear as demands for special treatment.
  • Recognition is a requirement of standing. On Taylor's argument, official indifference is not neutrality but a form of misrecognition.

3. Key thinkers

Isaiah Berlin (1909–97) — value pluralism. There are several genuine human goods — liberty, equality, justice, mercy — which may conflict and cannot all be realised together, and there is no single correct ranking of them. This is not relativism: Berlin holds that judgement remains possible and that some ways of life are worse than others. It supports multiculturalism by implying that no one culture can realise every human good, so diversity reflects the structure of value rather than merely being a fact to manage.
Charles Taylor (1931– ) — The Politics of Recognition (1992). Identity is formed dialogically, through interaction with others, rather than generated internally. It follows that how others regard us partly constitutes who we are, so misrecognition or non-recognition is a form of harm capable of inflicting a damaged self-image on a group across generations. Equal citizenship may therefore require public recognition of distinct identities, which challenges the liberal assumption that the state should be neutral about culture.
Bhikhu Parekh (1935– ) — Rethinking Multiculturalism (2000). A multicultural society requires a dialogue between cultures in which none is treated as the unquestioned standard, including the majority's. Human beings are culturally embedded, but cultures are internally plural and change through interaction. His "community of communities" describes a society whose shared public culture emerges from dialogue rather than being assumed from the majority. Chaired the 2000 report on the future of multi-ethnic Britain.
Tariq Modood (1952– ) — focuses on religious identity, particularly Muslim identity. Argues that secular multiculturalism accommodates ethnic difference but not religious difference, recognising religious minorities only as ethnic groups and so missing what matters most to them. Calls for "moderate secularism" in which the state engages with religious communities rather than excluding religion from public life, and argues that British national identity can be remade inclusively rather than abandoned.
Will Kymlicka (1962– ) — Multicultural Citizenship (1995). A liberal defence beginning from individual autonomy rather than the value of cultures in themselves. Culture provides the context of choice: meaningful choice requires a range of options intelligible to the chooser, and cultural membership supplies this. Since majority culture is sustained automatically by state institutions — language of administration, public calendar, curriculum — minority rights correct an asymmetry. His crucial distinction: external protections (shielding a minority from the wider society) are justified; internal restrictions (allowing a group to constrain its own members) are not.
Kymlicka's external protections / internal restrictions distinction is the highest-value content in this topic. It is the most serious attempt to resolve the group-versus-individual tension, and noting its limitation earns AO3: because it protects culture only instrumentally, as a condition of autonomy, it cannot accommodate cultures whose own self-understanding is not liberal.

5. Evaluation

Criticisms

CriticismResponse
Relativism — leaves no ground from which to criticise any practice, including illiberal onesMost multiculturalists are liberals holding accommodation must stay within individual rights; Berlin's pluralism explicitly rejects relativism
Harm to individuals within groups — group rights may be used by leaders against members, especially women and dissentersKymlicka's internal restrictions limit is designed for exactly this — though it constrains multiculturalism considerably
Essentialism — treats communities as bounded units with authentic representatives, empowering their most conservative voicesParekh's cultures are internally plural and change through dialogue; cosmopolitan multiculturalism rejects fixed identity outright
Undermines cohesion — emphasising distinct identities weakens shared civic attachmentSeparation is better explained by housing, employment and discrimination; assimilation produces its own exclusion

Multiculturalism and nationalism

Where nationalism is ethnic — the nation defined by descent and culture — the two are genuinely incompatible, because they give opposite answers to who belongs. Where it is civic — defined by shared institutions and citizenship — they can be reconciled, and Parekh's community of communities is an attempt to do so. But civic nations still operate in particular languages and teach particular histories, so compatibility depends on a nation's willingness to treat its own inherited culture as one among several. That is a demanding requirement, and explains why the argument has been politically contested wherever it has been attempted.
