1.3 Electoral Systems

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 1: UK Politics, Section A

This topic is the most technical in Component 1 and the most rewarding, because almost every other question in UK Politics has an electoral-system component. Party systems, minor-party failure, the democratic deficit and the two-party debate all reduce to how votes are converted into seats. Learn the mechanics precisely: examiners can tell immediately whether a candidate understands how AMS or STV actually works.

First-past-the-post (FPTP) — single-member constituencies; the candidate with the most votes wins, with no majority required.
Additional Member System (AMS) — hybrid: single-member constituencies elected by FPTP plus a regional top-up list.
Single Transferable Vote (STV) — multi-member constituencies; voters rank candidates; surpluses and eliminations are transferred until a quota is met.
Supplementary Vote (SV) — voters express a first and second preference; used for mayors and PCCs until the Elections Act 2022, and restored for them from June 2026.
Safe seat — a constituency the same party wins consistently by a wide margin.
Marginal seat — a constituency where the gap between the leading candidates is narrow enough that it may change hands.
Minority government — a government without an overall Commons majority.
Coalition government — two or more parties agreeing to govern together and share ministerial office.
Wasted vote — one cast for a losing candidate, or surplus to the number the winner required.
Keep the dates straight. The Elections Act 2022 replaced SV with FPTP for elected mayors and police and crime commissioners from May 2023; the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026 restored SV for those elections from June 2026. The Senedd also changed: from May 2026 it is elected by closed-list PR (96 members, 16 six-member constituencies), not AMS.

The 1951 and February 1974 cases are worth knowing because they show the distortion is not merely a matter of degree: FPTP can reverse the order of the two largest parties entirely.

AMS in detail

Voters cast two votes. The constituency vote elects a member by FPTP; the regional vote is for a party list, and list seats are allocated so that each party's overall regional representation more closely reflects its regional vote share. In Scotland, 73 constituency members and 56 regional members across eight regions; in Wales, 40 constituency and 20 regional under the arrangements in force for most of the devolution period.

The correction is partial rather than complete, which is why AMS is called a hybrid. A party that wins most constituency seats receives few list seats, because the calculation offsets what it already holds.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Markedly more proportional than FPTP while keeping a constituency linkCreates two classes of member with different mandates and workloads
Voters can split the ticket — supporting a constituency candidate and a different party regionallyList members are chosen by the party, not the voter, under a closed list
Gives smaller parties realistic representationCoalition or minority government becomes the norm, so the governing programme is settled after the election

Scotland's record illustrates the range of outcomes: a Labour–Lib Dem coalition, SNP minority governments, an SNP overall majority in 2011 which the system was designed to make unlikely, and later an SNP–Green co-operation agreement.

STV in detail

Voters rank candidates in multi-member constituencies. A candidate is elected on reaching the Droop quota:

quota = ( total valid votes ÷ ( seats + 1 ) ) + 1

Surplus votes above the quota transfer at a reduced value to later preferences; the lowest-placed candidates are eliminated and their votes transferred, until all seats are filled. In a six-seat constituency the quota is therefore just over one-seventh of the vote — which is why a party with modest but real support wins representation.

In Northern Ireland the purpose is structural. Multi-member seats and transfers ensure both communities are represented in every area, which makes the Good Friday Agreement's power-sharing executive consistent with the electoral outcome. It also gives candidates an incentive to seek transfers beyond their own community — though in practice transfers have often stayed within blocs.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Highly proportional; very few wasted votesWeaker constituency link — several members serve one area, so responsibility is diffused
Voters choose between candidates of the same party, not just between partiesComplex count taking several days
Encourages candidates to appeal beyond their core supportIntra-party competition can create friction between colleagues
Almost always produces coalition, which requires broad agreementCoalition may deliver a programme no voter actually chose

SV in detail

Voters mark a first and a second preference. If a candidate wins more than 50% of first preferences they are elected. Otherwise all but the top two are eliminated, and the second preferences of eliminated candidates' voters are added where they name one of the remaining two. It produces a winner with broad rather than merely plurality support, but only among those whose second preference happened to fall on a survivor — many ballots are exhausted. The Elections Act 2022 replaced it with FPTP from May 2023, a change its critics argued would advantage the largest party; the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026 brought it back from June 2026.

Referendums have been used almost exclusively for constitutional questions — a convention rather than a rule, since nothing prevents one on any subject. The 2011 AV referendum repays detailed knowledge: it was conceded by the Conservatives as the price of coalition, AV is not itself a proportional system, turnout was low at 42.2%, and the decisive rejection has been treated as settling the wider reform question for a generation. A campaign framing reform as a preoccupation of politicians was central to the outcome.

Effects on party systems within the UK

The same country running different systems is an unusually clean natural experiment, and it is the strongest evidence available in this topic. Proportional rules in Scotland and Wales convert support that FPTP suppresses into seats, so smaller parties such as the Greens hold representation at Holyrood that Westminster arithmetic denies them. Coalition and minority government have been normalised there, which directly weakens a central argument for FPTP — that only it delivers stable government. Northern Ireland's STV sustains a wholly separate multi-party system.

Turnout and safe seats

Roughly half of Westminster seats change hands very rarely. Turnout is consistently lower in safe seats than in marginals, and campaign resources flow overwhelmingly to the few dozen genuinely competitive constituencies. The consequence extends beyond fairness to governing: policy attention follows the voters who can change the outcome, which is a distortion distinct from disproportionality and arguably more serious.

Why has Westminster not changed?

  • Self-interest. The parties able to legislate the change are the ones FPTP advantages.
  • The 2011 result. Rejected 67.9% to 32.1% on 42% turnout.
  • Genuine attachment to the status quo. Decisive government and the constituency link are real goods, and there is no sustained public demand for change.
  • No trigger. Reform tends to follow a hung parliament in which a smaller party extracts it as a condition — exactly how the AV referendum arose, and its failure removed the mechanism.
  • The Jenkins Commission (1998) recommended AV Plus after a Labour manifesto commitment to a referendum. With a majority of 179 (177 on the stricter count that treats the Speaker as Labour — both figures are in circulation), the government had no incentive to proceed, and it did not.

The circularity is worth stating explicitly: FPTP produces majorities that make reform unnecessary for the governing party, so the system protects itself.

For "Evaluate the case for reforming the Westminster electoral system" (30 marks, AO1/AO2/AO3 10 each), the judgement should turn on what elections are for. If they exist to choose and remove a government, FPTP performs well. If they exist to represent the distribution of opinion, it performs badly. The two purposes genuinely conflict, and saying so is the conclusion — not a failure to reach one. Use the devolved systems as evidence rather than speculation.
