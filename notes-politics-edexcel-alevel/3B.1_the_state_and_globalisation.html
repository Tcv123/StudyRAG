3B.1 The State and Globalisation

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3B: Global Politics

Component 3 has two routes. This is 3B (Global Politics); the alternative is 3A (Comparative Politics: USA). You sit one, never both. Section B is a compulsory 12-mark question on comparative theories — realism and liberalism — so 3B.6 is unavoidable.

This opens 3B and supplies the framework for everything after it: what a state is, what sovereignty means, and how far globalisation has changed both.

Sovereignty — supreme authority within a territory, free from external control.
Internal sovereignty — supreme authority over everything within the borders.
External sovereignty — independence from outside interference; formal equality with other states.
Nation state — a political community with defined territory, permanent population and sovereign authority.
Non-state actors — NGOs, multinational corporations, armed groups, international organisations.
Globalisation — the growing interconnectedness of societies across borders.
Homogenisation — the tendency of cultures to become more alike.
Interconnectedness — the density of links, so events in one place have effects elsewhere.
Global governance — managing shared problems through rules and institutions without a world government.
World government — a supranational authority with sovereign power over states; does not exist.
Pooled sovereignty — states exercising powers jointly through a shared institution.

1. The state and national sovereignty

The Westphalian settlement of 1648 is the conventional starting point: sovereign territorial states, formally equal, with non-intervention as the cardinal rule. Its four characteristics — defined territory, permanent population, a government, and the capacity to enter relations with other states — remain the working definition.

FeatureDetail
Territorial integrityBorders are inviolable; this is the principle Russia's actions in Ukraine directly challenged
Monopoly of legitimate forceThe defining internal capacity, and what no non-state actor possesses
Legal equalityAll states are formally equal in international law regardless of power — one state, one vote in the UN General Assembly
Non-interventionArticle 2(7) of the UN Charter; qualified in practice by human rights obligations and R2P

Failed and fragile states

A state that can no longer perform basic functions across its territory retains formal external sovereignty while losing effective internal control. The consequences cross borders — conflict, displacement, ungoverned space in which armed groups and organised crime operate — which is why state failure is treated as an international security concern rather than a purely internal matter.

ViewClaimEvidence cited

The transformationalist position is now the most widely held, because it accommodates evidence the other two treat selectively.

3. Debates about globalisation's impact

Has it reduced state autonomy?

Yes: capital mobility narrows tax and regulatory choices; treaty obligations bind; transnational problems cross borders; financial markets discipline fiscal policy directly.

But unevenly: large states with deep markets shape the rules others are constrained by. Globalisation has redistributed autonomy as much as reduced it — a point worth making, because it converts a general claim into an analytical one.

Inequality

Globalisation reduced povertyGlobalisation increased inequality
The largest reduction in absolute poverty in history occurred over the same period, principally in China and IndiaGains concentrated where capital, skills and institutions already existed; much of sub-Saharan Africa did not converge
Integration gave developing economies access to markets and investmentWithin developed states, low-skilled workers faced competition from far cheaper labour — deindustrialisation and regional decline
Technology and knowledge transfer accelerated developmentThe same capital mobility constrains the taxation that would fund compensation for the losers

Both claims are true, which is why assessments differ so sharply: globalisation reduced absolute poverty globally and increased relative inequality within many developed states.

Culture

Critics argue cultural globalisation spreads a dominant, largely Western consumer culture that displaces local practice — homogenisation, or "McDonaldisation". The counter-argument is glocalisation: global products are adapted locally, and cultural flows run in several directions rather than one.

4. Sovereignty under pressure

PressureEffect on sovereignty
International law and human rightsHow a state treats its own population became a matter of international concern — breaching the principle that internal conduct is nobody else's business
R2P and humanitarian interventionSovereignty reframed as conditional on protecting the population
International criminal lawThe ICC prosecutes individuals including heads of state, removing the immunity sovereignty conferred
Pooled sovereigntyStates exercise powers jointly — the EU is the developed case; whether this is exercising or surrendering sovereignty is contested
EconomicTreaty obligations, dispute mechanisms, market discipline

The realist objection to conditional sovereignty: enforcement runs through the Security Council, so a P5 interest blocks action regardless of severity. Conditionality is asserted universally and applied selectively — which describes a hierarchy of power rather than a rule of law.

5. Evaluation

Is the nation state obsolete?

No, but transformed. Autonomy over economic policy has narrowed, but states retain the monopoly on force, legal authority and citizenship, and they largely chose integration. The pandemic demonstrated the point under stress: when a genuine crisis arrived, responses were overwhelmingly national — borders closed, vaccines were procured by governments, international coordination was marginal. The return of great power competition has reasserted state centrality further.

Has globalisation undermined democracy?

The most serious version of the charge: decisions with domestic consequence shift to markets and to bodies electorates cannot remove, so the range of choice a national election can deliver narrows. That mismatch — problems operating globally, accountability remaining national — is the strongest structural explanation for the populist backlash of the last decade, and treats it as a response to a real loss of correspondence rather than to a misperception.

The most useful single analytical move in 3B is the scale mismatch: the problems that matter most now operate at a scale where no democratic authority exists, while democratic authority remains national. It works in questions on globalisation, on global governance, on climate and on regionalism — four of the six topics on this route.
