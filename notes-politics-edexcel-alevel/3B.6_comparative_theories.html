3B.6 Comparative Theories

Politics — Edexcel (9PL0) — Component 3B: Global Politics

This topic is tested directly by Section B: one compulsory 12-mark question on comparative theories (AO1 6 / AO2 6). It is the one topic on the route you cannot avoid, and the theories also supply the evaluative framework for every Section C essay.

Realism and liberalism are the two theories the specification names. They are not descriptions to be recited but lenses: the marks come from applying them to the developments covered elsewhere on the route.

Anarchy — the absence of any authority above states; not disorder, but the absence of a ruler.
Security dilemma — measures one state takes for its own security appear threatening to others, who respond in kind, leaving everyone less secure.
Balance of power — stability arising when no single state or bloc can dominate.
Self-help — under anarchy, states can rely only on themselves for security.
Relative gains — concern with how much one gains compared to others, which inhibits co-operation.
Absolute gains — concern with whether one is better off overall, which permits co-operation.
Complex interdependence — multiple channels linking societies, with military force a less usable instrument.
Democratic peace theory — established democracies very rarely go to war with one another.
Hegemonic stability — order is most secure when a dominant power underwrites the rules.
Constructivism — interests and identities are socially constructed rather than fixed.

1. Realism

AssumptionDetail

States are the primary actorsRational, unitary, pursuing their own interests
Survival is the fundamental goalSecurity comes first; other objectives are secondary
Self-helpStates can rely only on themselves, so they accumulate capability
Relative gainsWhat matters is position relative to others, which inhibits co-operation even where all would benefit
Order rests on the balance of powerNot on law or institutions

Variants

  • Classical realism — conflict originates in human nature: the drive for power is a constant of human psychology.
  • Structural realism (neorealism) — Waltz. Conflict originates in the structure of the system rather than in human nature: anarchy alone compels states to behave as they do, whatever their internal character.
  • Offensive realism — Mearsheimer. States maximise power because it is the surest route to security, so great power competition is inevitable regardless of intentions.

The security dilemma is realism's most useful mechanism: because no authority guarantees protection and intentions can change, states must respond to capabilities rather than to declarations. This explains how conflict can arise between states that would both prefer to avoid it — which is a more sophisticated claim than "states are aggressive".

Democratic peace theory

Established liberal democracies have very rarely if ever fought full-scale wars against one another, though they fight non-democracies readily. Proposed mechanisms: institutional — leaders accountable to electorates that bear the costs face constraints autocrats do not, and democratic decision-making is slower and more transparent; normative — democracies externalise habits of peaceful dispute resolution and recognise one another as legitimate.

It is a direct challenge to structural realism, which holds that the internal character of states is irrelevant. Qualifications for AO3: definitions of democracy and war affect the findings; the correlation may partly reflect shared Cold War alliances; and democratising states appear more conflict-prone than either stable democracies or autocracies, which weakens the policy conclusion considerably.

4. Constructivism

Not one of the two named theories, but worth knowing because it supplies what both lack. Realism and liberalism take interests as given and derive behaviour from them; constructivism asks where interests come from, arguing that they and identities are socially constructed through interaction, shared norms and ideas.

Wendt's formulation — "anarchy is what states make of it" — is the key line: identical material conditions can produce rivalry or community depending on how actors understand one another. British nuclear weapons and North Korean ones present very different threats to Washington despite comparable material facts.

It explains change the other theories handle poorly: the most significant developments of the last century — decolonisation, the delegitimation of conquest, the European settlement — were changes in what states wanted rather than in what they could do.

5. Evaluation

Which theory better explains contemporary global politics?

The case for realism: the last decade has vindicated it. Great power competition returned; institutions were paralysed by the states that built them; interdependence was weaponised rather than restraining conflict; states are deliberately decoupling in strategic sectors, accepting economic cost for relative security. Its strength is parsimony — substantial predictions from few assumptions.

The case for liberalism: it explains the overwhelming majority of international interaction, which is routine co-operation realism treats as epiphenomenal — the trade regime, the EU, the human rights framework, the sheer density of compliance with international law in ordinary conditions. It also accounts for the absence of great power war since 1945.

The strongest Section B judgement is that the two theories explain different domains rather than competing to explain the same one: realism fits the hard cases where vital interests conflict, liberalism the dense co-operation where they align. A system containing both nuclear deterrence and the European single market will not reduce to one logic. Theoretical pluralism is an accurate response to a system with several logics operating simultaneously, not a failure to reach a conclusion.

Quick recall

  • Realism: anarchy, states as primary actors, survival, self-help, relative gains, balance of power
  • Classical (human nature) / structural or neorealism, Waltz (system structure) / offensive, Mearsheimer
  • Security dilemma explains conflict between states that both want to avoid it
  • Liberalism: co-operation possible, multiple actors, institutions, interdependence, absolute gains, domestic character matters
  • Democratic peace: robust finding, disputed mechanism; democratising states are more conflict-prone
  • Constructivism: "anarchy is what states make of it" (Wendt) — explains change in what states want
  • Best evidence for realism: the WTO Appellate Body disabled by one state
  • Best evidence for liberalism: the EU, and the density of routine compliance
