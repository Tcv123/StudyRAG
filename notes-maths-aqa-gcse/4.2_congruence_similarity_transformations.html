4.2 Congruence, Similarity & Transformations

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Geometry and Measures — spec refs G5, G7, G8, G19 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

This topic is about how shapes can be moved, flipped, turned and resized, and how to tell when two shapes are identical (congruent) or the same shape but a different size (similar). Higher tier adds negative scale factors, combined transformations, invariance, and the link between length, area and volume in similar figures.

4.2.1 Congruent Triangles (G5)

Two shapes are congruent if they are exactly the same shape and size. One can be mapped onto the other by a translation, rotation or reflection (or a combination of these). All corresponding sides and all corresponding angles are equal.

You do not need to check all six measurements to prove two triangles congruent. Any one of these four conditions is enough:

ConditionWhat must be equalNotes
SSSAll three sidesSide, Side, Side
SASTwo sides and the angle between themThe angle must be the included angle
ASA (or AAS)Two angles and a corresponding sideIf two angles match, the third does too, so any corresponding side works
RHSRight angle, Hypotenuse and one other SideOnly for right-angled triangles
AAA is not a congruence condition. Three equal angles only prove the triangles are similar — one could be an enlargement of the other. Similarly, SSA (two sides and a non-included angle) is not enough, except in the special right-angled case RHS.

Writing a congruence proof

A congruence proof is a short chain of statements, each with a reason, ending with the condition used.

ABCD is a parallelogram. Prove that triangles ABD and CDB are congruent.

$AB = CD$ (opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal)
$AD = CB$ (opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal)
$BD$ is common to both triangles.
So triangles ABD and CDB are congruent (SSS).
ABC is isosceles with $AB = AC$, and M is the midpoint of BC. Prove that the line AM is perpendicular to BC.

$AB = AC$ (given), $BM = CM$ (M is the midpoint), $AM$ is common, so triangles ABM and ACM are congruent (SSS).
Hence angle $AMB$ = angle $AMC$. These two angles lie on a straight line, so each is $180° \div 2 = 90°$. So $AM \perp BC$.

4.2.2 The Four Transformations (G7, G8)

A transformation maps an object onto an image. In the exam you must be able to carry out each one on coordinate axes and to describe it fully. "Describe fully" questions usually carry 2–3 marks, one for the name and one for each piece of detail.

TransformationTo describe it fully, give…Image congruent?
Translationthe column vectorYes
Reflectionthe equation of the mirror lineYes
Rotationthe centre, the angle, the directionYes
Enlargementthe scale factor and the centreNo — similar (unless scale factor is 1 or −1)

Translation

A translation slides every point the same distance in the same direction. It is written as a column vector $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$: the top number is the movement right (negative = left), the bottom number is the movement up (negative = down).
Translate the triangle with vertices $(1, 1)$, $(3, 1)$, $(3, 2)$ by $\begin{pmatrix} -4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

Subtract 4 from each $x$ and add 3 to each $y$: $(-3, 4)$, $(-1, 4)$, $(-1, 5)$.

Reflection

Every point is the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line as its image, on the opposite side. Common mirror lines:

Reflection in the $x$-axis ($y = 0$): $(x, y) \to (x, -y)$
Reflection in the $y$-axis ($x = 0$): $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$
Reflection in $y = x$: $(x, y) \to (y, x)$
Reflection in $y = -x$: $(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$
Reflection in $x = a$: the $x$-coordinate becomes $2a - x$    e.g. $(5, 3)$ in $x = 2$ gives $(-1, 3)$

Rotation

A rotation turns a shape about a fixed centre of rotation. You need tracing paper in the exam — ask for it. Rotations of 90° and 180° about the origin follow simple rules:

90° clockwise about $O$: $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$
90° anticlockwise about $O$: $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$
180° about $O$: $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$   (direction does not matter for 180°)
Rotate $(5, 1)$ by 90° anticlockwise about $(2, 1)$.

Position relative to the centre: $(5 - 2,\ 1 - 1) = (3, 0)$.
Rotate 90° anticlockwise: $(3, 0) \to (0, 3)$.
Add the centre back: $(2 + 0,\ 1 + 3) = \mathbf{(2, 4)}$.
A rotation description needs three things: centre, angle and direction. Missing the direction on a 90° rotation loses a mark. Never describe a single transformation as a combination (e.g. "reflect then translate") — that scores zero.

4.2.3 Enlargement and Scale Factors (G7)

An enlargement changes the size of a shape. Every length is multiplied by the scale factor $k$, and every point moves along a line from the centre of enlargement so that its distance from the centre is multiplied by $k$. Angles do not change, so the image is similar to the object.
Scale factor $k$Effect on the image
$k > 1$Larger, further from the centre
$0 < k < 1$ (fractional)Smaller, closer to the centre — still called an enlargement
$k < 0$ HigherOn the opposite side of the centre and upside down
Image of point $P$ under enlargement, scale factor $k$, centre $C$:
$$P' = C + k\,(P - C)$$ Scale factor $= \dfrac{\text{image length}}{\text{object length}}$
Enlarge $(6, 4)$ and $(10, 8)$ by scale factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$, centre $(2, 2)$.

$(6, 4)$: vector from centre $= (4, 2)$; halve it: $(2, 1)$; image $= (2 + 2,\ 2 + 1) = \mathbf{(4, 3)}$.
$(10, 8)$: vector from centre $= (8, 6)$; halve it: $(4, 3)$; image $= \mathbf{(6, 5)}$.

Finding the centre of enlargement

Draw straight lines through each pair of corresponding vertices (object vertex to image vertex) and extend them. They all cross at the centre. Algebraically, rearrange $P' = C + k(P - C)$ to get $C = \dfrac{P' - kP}{1 - k}$.

Triangle A has vertices $(2, 1)$, $(3, 1)$, $(2, 3)$; triangle B has vertices $(3, 2)$, $(5, 2)$, $(3, 6)$. Describe the transformation.

Side $(2,1)$–$(3,1)$ has length 1; the matching side of B has length 2, so $k = 2$.
Centre: $C = \dfrac{(3, 2) - 2(2, 1)}{1 - 2} = \dfrac{(-1, 0)}{-1} = (1, 0)$.
Enlargement, scale factor 2, centre $(1, 0)$.

Negative scale factors Higher

With a negative scale factor, the image is on the opposite side of the centre. Measure from the centre to each object vertex, multiply by $|k|$, and go that distance in the opposite direction. The image appears rotated by 180°.

Enlarge the triangle $(2, 1)$, $(4, 1)$, $(2, 4)$ by scale factor $-\tfrac{1}{2}$, centre $O$.

Multiply each coordinate by $-\tfrac{1}{2}$: $\mathbf{(-1, -0.5)}$, $\mathbf{(-2, -0.5)}$, $\mathbf{(-1, -2)}$.
An enlargement with scale factor $-1$ is the same as a rotation of 180° about the centre of enlargement. If asked to describe a single transformation, either answer earns the marks provided the centre is correct.

4.2.4 Combined Transformations and Invariance Higher

When one transformation is followed by another, the result can often be described as a single transformation. Work through the coordinates of each vertex step by step, then compare the final image with the original.

Triangle T $(1, 2)$, $(3, 2)$, $(3, 3)$ is reflected in $y = x$, then the image is reflected in the $x$-axis. Describe the single transformation.

Reflect in $y = x$: $(x, y) \to (y, x)$ gives $(2, 1)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 3)$.
Reflect in $x$-axis: $(x, y) \to (x, -y)$ gives $(2, -1)$, $(2, -3)$, $(3, -3)$.
Overall $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$: rotation 90° clockwise about the origin.
An invariant point is a point that maps onto itself. An invariant line maps onto itself (its points may move along it).
  • Reflection: every point on the mirror line is invariant.
  • Rotation: only the centre of rotation is invariant.
  • Enlargement ($k \ne 1$): only the centre is invariant.
  • Translation (non-zero vector): no invariant points.

Useful results about combinations:

  • Two reflections in parallel lines give a translation (twice the distance between the lines).
  • Two reflections in lines that cross give a rotation about the crossing point, through twice the angle between the lines.
  • Two translations combine by adding the column vectors: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -6 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • Order can matter: "reflect then rotate" may not give the same image as "rotate then reflect".

4.2.5 Similar Shapes (G7, G19)

Two shapes are mathematically similar if one is an enlargement of the other. Corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are in the same ratio (the length scale factor $k$).

Two triangles are similar if any one of these is true:

  • all three pairs of angles are equal (two pairs is enough, as the third must then match);
  • all three pairs of sides are in the same ratio;
  • two pairs of sides are in the same ratio and the included angles are equal.

When a line inside a triangle is parallel to one side (DE parallel to BC above), it cuts off a smaller similar triangle: angle A is shared, and the other angles are equal as corresponding angles.

In the diagram, $AD = 4$ cm, $DB = 6$ cm and $DE = 5$ cm. Find $BC$.

$AB = 4 + 6 = 10$ cm, so the scale factor from ADE to ABC is $10 \div 4 = 2.5$.
$BC = 5 \times 2.5 = \mathbf{12.5}$ cm.
Use the whole side of the large triangle ($AB = AD + DB$), not just the extra piece $DB$. Using $6 \div 4$ is the most common mistake. Sketch the two triangles separately, matching equal angles, before finding the scale factor.
Lines AE and BD cross at C, with AB parallel to DE. $AB = 9$ cm, $DE = 6$ cm, $AC = 7.5$ cm, $CD = 4$ cm. Find $CE$ and $BC$.

Angle $ACB$ = angle $ECD$ (vertically opposite); angle $BAC$ = angle $DEC$ (alternate). So ABC and EDC are similar with $A \leftrightarrow E$, $B \leftrightarrow D$.
Scale factor (small to large) $= 9 \div 6 = 1.5$.
$CE = 7.5 \div 1.5 = \mathbf{5}$ cm;   $BC = 4 \times 1.5 = \mathbf{6}$ cm.

4.2.6 Lengths, Areas and Volumes of Similar Figures Higher

If the length scale factor between two similar shapes is $k$, areas are multiplied by $k^2$ and volumes by $k^3$. This applies to any area (surface area, cross-section, area of a label) and any volume (capacity, and mass if the material is the same).

Length scale factor $= k$
Area scale factor $= k^2$
Volume scale factor $= k^3$

Going backwards: $k = \sqrt{\text{area scale factor}} = \sqrt[3]{\text{volume scale factor}}$
Length ratioArea ratioVolume ratio
1 : 21 : 41 : 8
1 : 31 : 91 : 27
2 : 34 : 98 : 27
3 : 59 : 2527 : 125
Two similar bottles are 15 cm and 25 cm tall. The smaller holds 270 ml. How much does the larger hold?

$k = \dfrac{25}{15} = \dfrac{5}{3}$,   volume scale factor $= \left(\dfrac{5}{3}\right)^3 = \dfrac{125}{27}$.
Capacity $= 270 \times \dfrac{125}{27} = \mathbf{1250}$ ml.
Two similar pyramids have volumes 54 cm³ and 250 cm³. The smaller has surface area 36 cm². Find the surface area of the larger.

Volume scale factor $= \dfrac{250}{54} = \dfrac{125}{27}$, so $k = \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{125}{27}} = \dfrac{5}{3}$.
Area scale factor $= \left(\dfrac{5}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{25}{9}$.
Surface area $= 36 \times \dfrac{25}{9} = \mathbf{100}$ cm².
Always go through the length scale factor. If you are given areas and need a volume, square-root to get $k$, then cube it. Never multiply an area by a length scale factor directly.
