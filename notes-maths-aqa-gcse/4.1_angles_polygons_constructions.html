4.1 Angles, Polygons & Constructions

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Geometry & Measures — spec refs G1–G4, G6, G15 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

4.1.1 Geometric Terms and Notation

TermMeaning
Point, line, planeA point has position only; a line is straight and has length; a plane is a flat surface.
Vertex (plural vertices)A corner where edges or sides meet.
EdgeA line segment where two faces of a 3D shape meet (or a side of a 2D shape).
Parallel linesLines that are always the same distance apart and never meet; marked with matching arrows.
Perpendicular linesLines that meet at a right angle (90°), marked with a small square.
PolygonA closed 2D shape made of straight sides.
Regular polygonAll sides equal and all angles equal.
Line symmetryThe shape can be folded onto itself along a mirror line.
Rotational symmetryThe order is the number of times the shape looks the same in one full turn.

Labelling triangles and angles

  • Vertices are labelled with capital letters: triangle ABC.
  • The side opposite angle A is labelled with the lower-case letter $a$, and so on.
  • Angle ABC (or $\angle ABC$, or $A\hat{B}C$) is the angle at B, between BA and BC. The middle letter is the vertex.
  • A line segment between A and B is written AB.
When a question says “draw a diagram from this description”, sketch it, label every vertex and mark equal sides (dashes), parallel lines (arrows) and right angles (small square). A clear diagram makes the angle facts easier to spot.

Two angles on a straight line are $2x$ and $3x$. Find $x$.

$2x + 3x = 180 \Rightarrow 5x = 180 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{36°}$

4.1.3 Angles and Parallel Lines

A straight line that crosses two parallel lines is called a transversal. It creates three key pairs of angles.

Alternate angles are equal (on opposite sides of the transversal, between the parallel lines).
Corresponding angles are equal (in matching positions at each parallel line).
Co-interior angles add up to $180°$ (on the same side of the transversal, between the parallel lines).
AQA will not accept “Z angles”, “F angles” or “C angles” as reasons. You must write the full reason, e.g. “alternate angles are equal”, “corresponding angles are equal” or “co-interior angles add up to 180°”.
A transversal makes corresponding angles $(3x + 10)°$ and $(5x - 30)°$ with two parallel lines. Find $x$.

Corresponding angles are equal: $3x + 10 = 5x - 30$
$40 = 2x \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{20}$
PQ and RS are parallel, with T a point between them. Angle QPT = 35° and angle SRT = 50°. Find angle PTR.

Draw a line through T parallel to PQ and RS.
The angle above this line at T = 35° (alternate angles are equal).
The angle below this line at T = 50° (alternate angles are equal).
Angle PTR = $35 + 50 = \mathbf{85°}$

Proof: angles in a triangle add up to 180°

Draw a line through A parallel to BC.
The angle to the left of $a$ equals $b$ (alternate angles are equal).
The angle to the right of $a$ equals $c$ (alternate angles are equal).
These three angles lie on a straight line, so $a + b + c = 180°$.  ∎

Exterior angle of a triangle

An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.
Proof: $a + b + c = 180$ (angles in a triangle) and $c + e = 180$ (angles on a straight line), so $e = a + b$.
The angles of a triangle are $x$, $x + 20$ and $2x$. Find $x$.

$x + x + 20 + 2x = 180 \Rightarrow 4x = 160 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{40°}$  (angles 40°, 60°, 80°)

The angles of a quadrilateral are $2x + 10$, $3x$, $4x - 20$ and $x + 30$. Find the largest angle.

$10x + 20 = 360 \Rightarrow x = 34$

A square is a special rectangle and a special rhombus; a rhombus is a special parallelogram. When asked for a property that one shape has but another does not, choose one that really distinguishes them (e.g. a rhombus has diagonals at right angles, a general parallelogram does not).

Sum of interior angles of an $n$-sided polygon $= (n - 2) \times 180°$
Sum of exterior angles of any polygon $= 360°$
Regular polygon: exterior angle $= \dfrac{360°}{n}$,   interior angle $= 180° - \text{exterior angle}$
Each interior angle of a regular polygon is 156°. How many sides does it have?

Exterior angle $= 180 - 156 = 24°$
$n = 360 \div 24 = \mathbf{15}$ sides
The interior angles of a polygon add up to 1980°. How many sides does it have?

$(n - 2) \times 180 = 1980 \Rightarrow n - 2 = 11 \Rightarrow n = \mathbf{13}$
Explain why a regular polygon cannot have an interior angle of 100°.

Exterior angle = 80°, so $n = 360 \div 80 = 4.5$. The number of sides must be a whole number, so this is impossible.
Exterior angles are usually the quickest route. To find the number of sides of a regular polygon, find the exterior angle first and divide it into 360°.

4.1.7 Deriving Results and Simple Proofs

You must be able to use angle facts, properties of shapes and congruent triangles to derive results and write short proofs, giving a reason for every step.

Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal

Triangle ABC has AB = AC. Draw the bisector of angle A to meet BC at D.
AB = AC (given), angle BAD = angle CAD (AD bisects angle A), AD is common.
So triangles ABD and ACD are congruent (SAS), and therefore angle ABC = angle ACB.  ∎

Pythagoras' theorem can also be derived by fitting four congruent right-angled triangles inside a square and comparing areas.

Multi-step angle reasoning Higher

ABC is isosceles with AB = AC and angle BAC = 36°. D lies on AC with BD = BC. Prove that AD = BD.

Angle ABC = angle ACB = $(180 - 36) \div 2 = 72°$ (base angles of an isosceles triangle)
Triangle BDC is isosceles (BD = BC), so angle BDC = angle BCD = 72°
Angle DBC = $180 - 72 - 72 = 36°$ (angles in a triangle)
Angle ABD = $72 - 36 = 36°$ = angle BAD
Two equal angles, so triangle ABD is isosceles and AD = BD.  ∎
ABCDEFGH is a regular octagon. Find angle ACD.

Interior angle = 135°. Triangle ABC is isosceles (AB = BC), so angle BCA = $(180 - 135) \div 2 = 22.5°$.
Angle ACD = $135 - 22.5 = \mathbf{112.5°}$
In “give reasons” questions, each reason is usually worth a mark. Write the angle fact in words next to each calculation and say which triangle is isosceles and why.

4.1.8 Ruler and Compass Constructions

Use a sharp pencil, a ruler and compasses only (no protractor unless told). Leave all construction arcs showing — they are the evidence that earns the marks.

Perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB

  1. Open the compasses to more than half of AB.
  2. With the point on A, draw arcs above and below AB.
  3. Keep the same radius; repeat from B so the arcs cross twice.
  4. Join the two crossing points. This line cuts AB in half at 90°.

Perpendicular from a point P to a line

  1. With centre P, draw an arc that cuts the line at two points, X and Y.
  2. Construct the perpendicular bisector of XY (arcs from X and Y on the far side of the line).
  3. The line from P through the crossing point is perpendicular to the line.

Perpendicular at a point P on a line

  1. With centre P, draw arcs cutting the line at X and Y, equal distances either side of P.
  2. Open the compasses wider; draw arcs from X and from Y that cross above P.
  3. Join P to the crossing point.
The shortest distance from a point to a line is the perpendicular distance — measured along the line from the point that meets the given line at 90°.

Bisecting an angle

  1. With centre at the vertex O, draw an arc crossing both arms at X and Y.
  2. With the same radius, draw arcs from X and from Y that cross at Z.
  3. Join OZ. It splits the angle into two equal halves.

Constructing a 60° angle

  1. Draw a line and mark a point O on it.
  2. With centre O, draw an arc cutting the line at X.
  3. With the same radius and centre X, draw an arc cutting the first arc at Y.
  4. Join OY. Triangle OXY is equilateral, so angle XOY = 60°. Bisect it to get 30°; bisect a 90° perpendicular to get 45°.
If you rub out your arcs you will usually lose the marks even if the line is in the right place. Accuracy is normally expected to within 2 mm and 2°.

4.1.9 Loci

A locus (plural loci) is the set of all points that obey a given rule.
RuleLocus

Equidistant from two intersecting linesThe bisector of the angle between them

“Within 4 m” means the region inside the circle; “closer to A than to B” means the side of the perpendicular bisector that contains A. Combine conditions by shading the region that satisfies all of them.

4.1.10 Measuring, Scale Drawings and Bearings

Measuring and scale drawings

  • Measure lengths to the nearest millimetre and angles to the nearest degree with a protractor (use the scale that starts from 0° on the arm you are measuring from).
  • A scale such as 1 cm : 2 km or 1 : 25 000 links lengths on a drawing or map to real lengths.
  • Real length = drawing length × scale factor. Drawing length = real length ÷ scale factor. Convert units carefully.
A map has scale 1 : 25 000. Two churches are 6.4 cm apart on the map. Find the real distance.

$6.4 \times 25\,000 = 160\,000$ cm $= 1600$ m $= \mathbf{1.6}$ km

Bearings

A bearing is:
1. measured from North
2. measured clockwise
3. written with three figures (e.g. 065°, 270°)

The bearing of B from A is 065°. Find the bearing of A from B.

$065 + 180 = \mathbf{245°}$   (draw a North line at B: co-interior angles on the parallel North lines add to 180°)
A ship sails from P on a bearing of 050° to A, then on a bearing of 140° to B. Show that angle PAB = 90°.

Bearing of P from A = $050 + 180 = 230°$
Angle PAB = $230 - 140 = \mathbf{90°}$
“The bearing of B from A” means stand at A, face North, and turn clockwise until you face B. Always draw the North line at the point you are measuring from.
