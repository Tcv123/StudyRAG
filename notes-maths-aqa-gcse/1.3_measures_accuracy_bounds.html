1.3 Measures, Accuracy & Bounds

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Number — spec refs N13–N16 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

1.3.1 Standard Units and Metric Conversions

You must be able to use standard units of length, mass, time, money and capacity, and standard compound measures such as speed and density, working with decimal quantities where appropriate. You are expected to know the metric conversion factors. Any imperial/metric conversion (for example 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km or 1 kg ≈ 2.2 pounds) will be given in the question.

MeasureConversions you must know
Length10 mm = 1 cm    100 cm = 1 m    1000 m = 1 km
Mass1000 mg = 1 g    1000 g = 1 kg    1000 kg = 1 tonne
Capacity1000 ml = 1 litre    100 cl = 1 litre    1 ml = 1 cm³    1000 cm³ = 1 litre
Time60 s = 1 min    60 min = 1 hour    24 h = 1 day    365 days = 1 year (366 in a leap year)
Money100p = £1
Converting: going from a bigger unit to a smaller unit you multiply (there will be more of them); going from a smaller unit to a bigger unit you divide.
Convert 3.45 km to centimetres.

$3.45 \text{ km} = 3.45 \times 1000 = 3450 \text{ m}$
$3450 \text{ m} = 3450 \times 100 = \mathbf{345\,000 \text{ cm}}$

Area and Volume Conversions

Area is two-dimensional, so the length conversion factor is used twice. Volume is three-dimensional, so it is used three times.

Area
$1 \text{ cm}^2 = 10 \times 10 = 100 \text{ mm}^2$
$1 \text{ m}^2 = 100 \times 100 = 10\,000 \text{ cm}^2$

Volume
$1 \text{ cm}^3 = 10^3 = 1000 \text{ mm}^3$
$1 \text{ m}^3 = 100^3 = 1\,000\,000 \text{ cm}^3$
$1 \text{ m}^3 = 1000 \text{ litres}$
Convert 4.2 m² to cm².

$4.2 \times 10\,000 = \mathbf{42\,000 \text{ cm}^2}$
A fish tank holds 360 000 cm³ of water. How many litres is this?

$1000 \text{ cm}^3 = 1 \text{ litre}$, so $360\,000 \div 1000 = \mathbf{360 \text{ litres}}$
The most common mistake is to convert 1 m² as 100 cm². Sketch the square: each side is 100 cm, so the area is 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm².

Time and Compound Measures

Time is not decimal: 2.5 hours is 2 hours 30 minutes, not 2 hours 50 minutes. To change a decimal of an hour into minutes, multiply by 60.

$$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \qquad \text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} \qquad \text{pressure} = \frac{\text{force}}{\text{area}}$$
A car travels 135 km in 1 hour 30 minutes. Find its average speed.

$1 \text{ h } 30 \text{ min} = 1.5 \text{ h}$
$\text{speed} = 135 \div 1.5 = \mathbf{90 \text{ km/h}}$
Convert 1.35 hours into hours and minutes.

$0.35 \times 60 = 21$, so the time is 1 hour 21 minutes.

1.3.2 Rounding

You must round numbers and measures to an appropriate degree of accuracy, for example to a given number of decimal places (d.p.) or significant figures (s.f.), and choose sensible rounding for answers in context.

The rounding rule: find the last digit you are keeping, then look at the next digit.
  • 5 or more → round the kept digit up
  • 4 or less → leave the kept digit as it is

Significant Figures

The first significant figure is the first non-zero digit. Zeros between non-zero digits count; leading zeros do not. When rounding a large number, fill the places with zeros so the size of the number does not change.

Round 0.0009865 to 3 significant figures.

The first significant figure is 9, then 8, then 6. The next digit is 5, so round the 6 up.
Answer: 0.000987

Truncation

Truncating means cutting a number off at a certain place without rounding. For example, 8.379 truncated to 1 d.p. is 8.3 (rounded it would be 8.4).

Rounding in Context

  • Give money to the nearest penny: £14.3 should be written £14.30.
  • Decide whether to round up or down from the context: if each minibus holds 16 people, 50 people need $50 \div 16 = 3.125 \Rightarrow$ 4 minibuses; but with £50 and tickets at £16 you can buy only 3 tickets.
  • An answer should not be given to more accuracy than the data it came from — a sensible default is the accuracy of the least accurate value used.
Never round values during the intermediate steps of a calculation. Keep the full calculator value (or use the memory/ANS button) and only round the final answer. Early rounding can lose you the accuracy mark.

1.3.3 Estimation and Checking

You should be able to estimate answers and check calculations using approximation, including answers obtained on a calculator, and comment on whether a result is sensible.

Method: round every number to 1 significant figure (unless told otherwise), then do the simpler calculation. Write down the rounded values — that is where the method mark is.
Estimate $\dfrac{6.12 \times 19.6}{0.495}$.

$6.12 \approx 6$, $19.6 \approx 20$, $0.495 \approx 0.5$
$\dfrac{6 \times 20}{0.5} = \dfrac{120}{0.5} = \mathbf{240}$
Hannah works out $28.9 \times 0.512$ on her calculator and gets 147.968. Is she right?

Estimate: $30 \times 0.5 = 15$. Her answer is about 10 times too big, so she must have made an error (the correct value is 14.7968).

Over- or Underestimate?

Think about the effect of each rounding:

  • Rounding numbers you multiply or add up makes the estimate bigger.
  • Rounding a number you divide by down makes the estimate bigger (you are dividing by something smaller).
  • If roundings work in opposite directions, compare with the exact value or say it cannot be decided without more working.
Dividing by a decimal: $120 \div 0.5$ means "how many halves in 120?" — the answer is 240, which is bigger than 120. Multiply top and bottom by 10 if it helps: $1200 \div 5 = 240$.

Estimating with Powers and Roots

Estimate $\dfrac{\sqrt{48.7} \times 3.08^2}{0.48}$.

$\sqrt{48.7} \approx \sqrt{49} = 7$, $\;3.08^2 \approx 3^2 = 9$, $\;0.48 \approx 0.5$
$\dfrac{7 \times 9}{0.5} = \dfrac{63}{0.5} = \mathbf{126}$

1.3.4 Error Intervals

When a number has been rounded or truncated, the true value could lie anywhere within a range. This range is the error interval, written using inequality notation.

For a value rounded to a given unit, the true value lies within half a unit either side:
$$\text{value} - \tfrac{1}{2}\text{unit} \le x < \text{value} + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{unit}$$ The lower limit is included ($\le$); the upper limit is not included ($<$) because it would round up.
A length $L$ is 14 cm to the nearest centimetre. Write down the error interval.

Half of 1 cm is 0.5 cm, so $\mathbf{13.5 \le L < 14.5}$
$n = 3400$ correct to 2 significant figures. Write down the error interval.

2 s.f. here means "to the nearest 100", and half of 100 is 50, so $\mathbf{3350 \le n < 3450}$

Error Intervals for Truncation

If $x$ has been truncated, the true value can be anything from the stated value up to (but not including) the next value at that accuracy:
$x = 5.7$ truncated to 1 d.p. ⇒ $5.7 \le x < 5.8$
A number $n$ is 4.6 when rounded to 1 d.p. and also 4.6 when truncated to 1 d.p. Find the error interval.

Rounding: $4.55 \le n < 4.65$. Truncation: $4.6 \le n < 4.7$.
Both must be true, so $\mathbf{4.6 \le n < 4.65}$
Write the upper limit as 14.5, not 14.49 or 14.4999… The strict inequality sign $<$ already tells the examiner that 14.5 itself is not included.

1.3.5 Limits of Accuracy and Bounds

The lower bound (LB) and upper bound (UB) are the smallest and largest values a measurement could have been before it was rounded. They are the two ends of the error interval.

Bounds in Calculations Higher

When rounded values are combined in a calculation, choose the bound of each value that makes the result as large (for the upper bound) or as small (for the lower bound) as possible.

CalculationUpper boundLower bound
$a + b$$\text{UB}_a + \text{UB}_b$$\text{LB}_a + \text{LB}_b$
$a - b$$\text{UB}_a - \text{LB}_b$$\text{LB}_a - \text{UB}_b$
$a \times b$$\text{UB}_a \times \text{UB}_b$$\text{LB}_a \times \text{LB}_b$
$a \div b$$\text{UB}_a \div \text{LB}_b$$\text{LB}_a \div \text{UB}_b$
For subtraction and division, use the opposite bound for the second value.
A rectangle measures 8.6 cm by 4.2 cm, both to 1 d.p. Find the upper bound of its area.

$\text{UB} = 8.65 \times 4.25 = \mathbf{36.7625 \text{ cm}^2}$
$p = 7.2$ and $q = 3.4$, both to 1 d.p. Find the upper bound of $p - q$.

$\text{UB}_p - \text{LB}_q = 7.25 - 3.35 = \mathbf{3.9}$
A runner covers 200 m (to the nearest 5 m) in 24.6 s (to the nearest 0.1 s). Find the upper bound of her speed.

$\text{UB of speed} = \dfrac{\text{UB distance}}{\text{LB time}} = \dfrac{202.5}{24.55} = 8.248\ldots \approx \mathbf{8.25 \text{ m/s}}$

Giving an Answer to a Suitable Degree of Accuracy Higher

Work out both bounds, then round each one. The answer is the most accurate rounding on which the two bounds agree.

$p = 9.6$ and $q = 2.4$ (both 1 d.p.). Give $r = p \div q$ to a suitable degree of accuracy.

$\text{UB} = 9.65 \div 2.35 = 4.106\ldots$    $\text{LB} = 9.55 \div 2.45 = 3.897\ldots$
To 2 s.f.: 4.1 and 3.9 — different. To 1 s.f.: 4 and 4 — the same.
So $r = \mathbf{4}$ (1 s.f.), because both bounds round to 4.
The area of a square is 50 cm² to the nearest cm². Find the upper bound of its side length.

$\text{UB of area} = 50.5$, so $\text{UB of side} = \sqrt{50.5} = 7.106\ldots \approx \mathbf{7.11 \text{ cm}}$
Always write down the bounds you are using (e.g. "UB = 202.5, LB = 24.55") before calculating — there are usually method marks for the bounds even if the final answer goes wrong. Do not round the bounds before using them.
