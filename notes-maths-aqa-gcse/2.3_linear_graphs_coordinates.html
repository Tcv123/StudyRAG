2.3 Linear Graphs & Coordinates

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Algebra — spec refs A8–A10 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

This topic covers working with coordinates in all four quadrants (A8), plotting and using straight-line graphs including $y = mx + c$, parallel lines and the equation of a line through given points (A9), and identifying and interpreting gradients and intercepts (A10). The Higher-only content is using $y = mx + c$ to identify perpendicular lines.

2.3.1 Coordinates in Four Quadrants

A coordinate $(x, y)$ gives the position of a point. The x-coordinate tells you how far across (right is positive, left is negative); the y-coordinate tells you how far up (up is positive, down is negative). The axes cross at the origin $(0, 0)$ and divide the plane into four quadrants.
“Along the corridor, then up (or down) the stairs” — always read the $x$-coordinate first. Swapping the coordinates is the most common mistake when plotting or reading points.

Midpoint of a Line Segment

The midpoint of $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $$M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$ i.e. the mean of the $x$-coordinates and the mean of the $y$-coordinates.
Find the midpoint of $A(-3, 4)$ and $B(5, -2)$.

$x = \dfrac{-3 + 5}{2} = 1, \quad y = \dfrac{4 + (-2)}{2} = 1$
Midpoint $= \mathbf{(1, 1)}$
The midpoint of $AB$ is $(4, 1)$ and $A = (1, -3)$. Find $B$.

Going from $A$ to $M$ is $+3$ across and $+4$ up. Do the same again from $M$:
$B = (4 + 3,\ 1 + 4) = \mathbf{(7, 5)}$   (or use $B = (2 \times 4 - 1,\ 2 \times 1 - (-3))$)

Problems with Coordinates and Shapes

You may be asked to find a missing vertex of a shape. Use properties of the shape: for example, in a parallelogram $ABCD$ the “step” from $A$ to $B$ is the same as the step from $D$ to $C$.

$A(1, 1)$, $B(5, 2)$ and $C(6, 5)$ are three vertices of parallelogram $ABCD$. Find $D$.

Step from $B$ to $A$: $(-4, -1)$. Apply the same step from $C$: $D = (6 - 4,\ 5 - 1) = \mathbf{(2, 4)}$.

2.3.2 Plotting Straight-Line Graphs

An equation such as $y = 2x - 1$ is linear: it has no powers of $x$ above 1, and its graph is a straight line. To plot it, build a table of values, plot the points and join them with a ruler.

$x$$-1$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$y = 2x - 1$$-3$$-1$$1$$3$$5$
  • The $y$-values go up in equal steps (here $+2$ each time) — if they don't, check your arithmetic.
  • Plot at least three points; a third point acts as a check.
  • Draw the line across the whole range of $x$ asked for, not just between the plotted points.

2.3.3 Gradient and Intercept: $y = mx + c$

The equation of any non-vertical straight line can be written as $$y = mx + c$$ where $m$ is the gradient (steepness) and $c$ is the $y$-intercept — the line crosses the $y$-axis at $(0, c)$.
Gradient between two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$: $$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}$$
  • Positive gradient: line slopes up from left to right.
  • Negative gradient: line slopes down from left to right.
  • Zero gradient: horizontal line.
  • The bigger the size of $m$, the steeper the line.
Find the gradient of the line through $(-2, 1)$ and $(4, -2)$.

$m = \dfrac{-2 - 1}{4 - (-2)} = \dfrac{-3}{6} = \mathbf{-\tfrac{1}{2}}$

Reading $m$ and $c$ from an Equation

Always rearrange into the form $y = mx + c$ first.

In $y = 7 - 3x$ the gradient is $-3$, not $7$. The gradient is always the coefficient of $x$ once $y$ is on its own, whatever order the terms are written in.

Finding the $x$-intercept

A line crosses the $x$-axis where $y = 0$. For $y = 2x - 8$: $0 = 2x - 8$, so $x = 4$ and the line crosses at $(4, 0)$.

2.3.4 Finding the Equation of a Line

Through one point with a given gradient

  1. Write $y = mx + c$ with the known gradient.
  2. Substitute the coordinates of the point.
  3. Solve for $c$ and write the final equation.
Find the equation of the line with gradient 4 through $(2, 3)$.

$3 = 4 \times 2 + c \Rightarrow 3 = 8 + c \Rightarrow c = -5$
$\mathbf{y = 4x - 5}$

Through two points

  1. Find the gradient using $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$.
  2. Substitute either point into $y = mx + c$ to find $c$.
  3. Check using the other point.
Find the equation of the line through $(-2, 9)$ and $(4, -3)$.

$m = \dfrac{-3 - 9}{4 - (-2)} = \dfrac{-12}{6} = -2$
$9 = -2 \times (-2) + c \Rightarrow 9 = 4 + c \Rightarrow c = 5$
$\mathbf{y = -2x + 5}$   Check: $x = 4$ gives $y = -8 + 5 = -3$ ✓
Find the line through $(-3, 8)$ and $(5, -4)$ in the form $ax + by = c$ with integers.

$m = \dfrac{-4 - 8}{5 - (-3)} = \dfrac{-12}{8} = -\tfrac{3}{2}$
$8 = -\tfrac{3}{2}(-3) + c = 4.5 + c \Rightarrow c = 3.5$
$y = -1.5x + 3.5 \Rightarrow 2y = -3x + 7 \Rightarrow \mathbf{3x + 2y = 7}$

Parallel Lines

Lines are parallel if and only if they have the same gradient. For example $y = 3x + 2$ and $2y = 6x - 5$ (i.e. $y = 3x - 2.5$) are parallel.
Find the line parallel to $y = 4x - 3$ that passes through $(-1, 6)$.

Parallel, so $m = 4$.   $6 = 4 \times (-1) + c \Rightarrow c = 10$
$\mathbf{y = 4x + 10}$
Two lines with the same gradient and different intercepts never meet. If the intercepts are also equal, they are the same line.

2.3.5 Interpreting Gradients and Intercepts

In real-life linear models, the gradient is a rate of change and the intercept is a starting value (the value when the horizontal variable is zero).

A plumber charges a fixed fee plus an hourly rate. A 2-hour job costs £110 and a 5-hour job costs £215. Find the fee and the rate.

Gradient $= \dfrac{215 - 110}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{105}{3} = 35$, so the rate is £35 per hour.
$110 = 35 \times 2 + c \Rightarrow c = 40$, so the call-out fee is £40.   Model: $C = 35h + 40$.
When asked to “interpret” a gradient, give the units and the context, e.g. “the cost increases by £35 for each extra hour” — not just “35”.

2.3.6 Perpendicular Lines Higher

Two lines are perpendicular (meet at $90°$) if the product of their gradients is $-1$: $$m_1 \times m_2 = -1 \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1}$$ The perpendicular gradient is the negative reciprocal: flip the fraction and change the sign.

Find the equation of the line perpendicular to $y = 3x + 2$ through $(6, 1)$.

$m = -\tfrac{1}{3}$.   $1 = -\tfrac{1}{3} \times 6 + c \Rightarrow 1 = -2 + c \Rightarrow c = 3$
$\mathbf{y = -\tfrac{1}{3}x + 3}$   (or $x + 3y = 9$)
Show that $2y = x + 6$ and $y = 4 - 2x$ are perpendicular.

$2y = x + 6 \Rightarrow y = \tfrac{1}{2}x + 3$, gradient $\tfrac{1}{2}$.   $y = 4 - 2x$ has gradient $-2$.
$\tfrac{1}{2} \times (-2) = -1$, so the lines are perpendicular.

Perpendicular Bisectors

The perpendicular bisector of $AB$ passes through the midpoint of $AB$ and is perpendicular to $AB$.

Find the perpendicular bisector of $A(1, 3)$ and $B(7, 5)$.

Midpoint $= (4, 4)$.   Gradient $AB = \dfrac{5 - 3}{7 - 1} = \dfrac{1}{3}$, so the perpendicular gradient is $-3$.
$4 = -3 \times 4 + c \Rightarrow c = 16$   $\Rightarrow$   $\mathbf{y = -3x + 16}$

Proving Shapes with Gradients

$A(-2, 1)$, $B(4, 4)$, $C(6, 0)$, $D(0, -3)$. Show $ABCD$ is a rectangle.

$m_{AB} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $m_{DC} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $m_{BC} = \dfrac{-4}{2} = -2$, $m_{AD} = \dfrac{-4}{2} = -2$.
Opposite sides are parallel, and $\tfrac{1}{2} \times (-2) = -1$ so adjacent sides are perpendicular. Hence $ABCD$ is a rectangle.
A common error is to change only the sign (giving $-3$ instead of $-\tfrac{1}{3}$) or only flip the fraction. Check by multiplying: the two gradients must give $-1$.
