5.1 Probability

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Probability — spec refs P1–P9 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

5.1.1 The Probability Scale and Basic Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is, on a scale from $0$ (impossible) to $1$ (certain). Probabilities can be written as fractions, decimals or percentages — but never as ratios or “1 in 4” in an exam answer.

Theoretical Probability

When all outcomes are equally likely: $$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}}$$
A bag contains 3 red, 5 blue and 2 green counters. One is taken at random. Find $P(\text{blue})$ and $P(\text{not green})$.

Total $= 10$.   $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{5}{10} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.   $P(\text{not green}) = \dfrac{8}{10} = \dfrac{4}{5}$

Outcomes That Sum to 1 (P4)

The probabilities of an exhaustive set of mutually exclusive outcomes add up to $1$. In particular: $$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$$
A biased spinner lands on red, blue, green or yellow. $P(\text{red}) = 0.3$, $P(\text{blue}) = 0.25$, $P(\text{green}) = 0.1$. Find $P(\text{yellow})$.

$P(\text{yellow}) = 1 - (0.3 + 0.25 + 0.1) = 1 - 0.65 = \mathbf{0.35}$
A four-colour spinner has $P(\text{red}) = x$, $P(\text{blue}) = 2x$, $P(\text{yellow}) = 3x$ and $P(\text{green}) = 0.1$. Find $x$.

$x + 2x + 3x + 0.1 = 1 \Rightarrow 6x = 0.9 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{0.15}$
Check every probability answer lies between 0 and 1. If you get $1.2$ or $-0.3$, you have made an error. Fractions do not have to be simplified unless the question asks, but must be correct.

5.1.2 Experiments, Relative Frequency and Expected Outcomes (P1, P2, P3, P5)

Relative frequency (experimental probability) $= \dfrac{\text{number of times the outcome occurs}}{\text{total number of trials}}$. It is an estimate of the probability.

Results of experiments can be recorded in frequency tables and frequency trees. As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency tends towards the theoretical probability (P5). So a larger number of trials gives a more reliable estimate.

A coin is thrown 200 times and lands heads 116 times. Estimate $P(\text{heads})$. Is the coin biased?

Relative frequency $= \dfrac{116}{200} = 0.58$. This is above $0.5$, so the coin may be biased towards heads — but we cannot be certain. More throws would give a more reliable estimate.

Fairness and Randomness

An object is fair (unbiased) if every outcome is equally likely. Choosing “at random” means every item has the same chance of being picked. Even a fair dice will not give exactly equal frequencies in a small experiment.

Expected Outcomes (P2, P3)

$$\text{Expected number of outcomes} = P(\text{outcome}) \times \text{number of trials}$$
$P(\text{win}) = 0.15$ for a game that costs £1 and pays £5 to a winner. It is played 200 times. Find the expected profit for the organiser.

Expected wins $= 0.15 \times 200 = 30$. Takings = 200 × £1 = £200. Payouts = 30 × £5 = £150.
Expected profit = £200 − £150 = £50

Frequency Trees (P1)

A frequency tree splits a group into categories using numbers, not probabilities.

From the tree: $P(\text{a woman who exercises}) = \dfrac{50}{120} = \dfrac{5}{12}$, and $15 + 25 = 40$ people do not exercise.

5.1.3 Listing Outcomes and Sample Spaces (P6, P7)

A sample space (possibility space) is a list or table of every possible outcome. List outcomes systematically so none are missed — e.g. fix the first item and run through all choices of the second.
Ali chooses a starter (soup or salad) and a main (fish, pie or curry). List all combinations.

soup–fish, soup–pie, soup–curry, salad–fish, salad–pie, salad–curry   ($2 \times 3 = 6$ combinations)

5.1.4 Venn Diagrams (P6)

A Venn diagram shows how items are shared between sets. The rectangle contains everything being considered. Always fill in the overlap first, then work outwards.

30 students: 12 play football, 15 play tennis, 5 play both.

Overlap $= 5$. Football only $= 12 - 5 = 7$. Tennis only $= 15 - 5 = 10$.
Neither $= 30 - (7 + 5 + 10) = 8$.
$P(\text{tennis only}) = \dfrac{10}{30} = \dfrac{1}{3}$

5.1.5 Combined Events: When to Add and When to Multiply (P8)

Mutually exclusive events cannot happen at the same time. Independent events do not affect each other — the outcome of one does not change the probability of the other.
OR rule (mutually exclusive): $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$

AND rule (independent): $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)$

At least one: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$
$P(\text{Sam's bus is late}) = 0.3$ and $P(\text{his train is late}) = 0.2$, independently. Find the probability that (a) both are late (b) neither is late.

(a) $0.3 \times 0.2 = \mathbf{0.06}$    (b) $0.7 \times 0.8 = \mathbf{0.56}$
A fair dice is rolled twice. Find $P(\text{at least one six})$.

$P(\text{no six}) = \dfrac{5}{6} \times \dfrac{5}{6} = \dfrac{25}{36}$, so $P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - \dfrac{25}{36} = \mathbf{\dfrac{11}{36}}$

5.1.6 Tree Diagrams (P6, P8)

Tree diagrams show two or more events in sequence. Multiply along the branches to find the probability of a combined outcome, and add the end results for “or”. Each pair of branches from a point must add to $1$.

A spinner with $P(\text{red}) = 0.6$ is spun twice. $P(\text{exactly one red}) = 0.24 + 0.24 = 0.48$. The four end results add to $0.36 + 0.24 + 0.24 + 0.16 = 1$ ✓

With and Without Replacement (Dependent Events)

If an item is replaced, the probabilities stay the same on each pick (independent events). If it is not replaced, the second probabilities change: the total goes down by 1, and so does the count of the colour already taken. These are dependent events.

A bag has 5 red and 3 blue sweets. Two are taken without replacement. Find $P(\text{one of each colour})$.

$P(R, B) = \dfrac{5}{8} \times \dfrac{3}{7} = \dfrac{15}{56}$    $P(B, R) = \dfrac{3}{8} \times \dfrac{5}{7} = \dfrac{15}{56}$
$P(\text{one of each}) = \dfrac{30}{56} = \mathbf{\dfrac{15}{28}}$
A bag contains $n$ sweets, 6 of them orange. Two are taken without replacement and $P(\text{both orange}) = \dfrac{1}{3}$. Find $n$.

$\dfrac{6}{n} \times \dfrac{5}{n-1} = \dfrac{1}{3} \Rightarrow n(n-1) = 90 \Rightarrow n^2 - n - 90 = 0$
$(n - 10)(n + 9) = 0$, so $n = \mathbf{10}$ (reject $-9$).
The assumptions behind your calculation matter: multiplying probabilities assumes the events are independent. In “without replacement” questions, look out for the words “takes two”, “eats” or “does not replace” — the second-pick denominator must drop by 1.

5.1.7 Conditional Probability (P9) Higher

Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another event has happened, written $P(A \mid B)$. Knowing $B$ has happened restricts the outcomes to those in $B$. $$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

Restrict to the “owns a bike” column: 50 students, of whom 30 are in Year 11.

From Tree Diagrams

$P(\text{rain}) = 0.3$. If it rains, $P(\text{Mia late}) = 0.4$; if not, $P(\text{Mia late}) = 0.1$. Given Mia is late, find the probability it rained.

$P(\text{late}) = 0.3 \times 0.4 + 0.7 \times 0.1 = 0.12 + 0.07 = 0.19$
$P(\text{rain} \mid \text{late}) = \dfrac{0.12}{0.19} = \mathbf{\dfrac{12}{19}} \approx 0.632$

From Venn Diagrams

$P(A) = 0.45$, $P(B) = 0.3$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.1$. Find $P((A \cup B)')$ and $P(B \mid A)$.

$P(A \cup B) = 0.45 + 0.3 - 0.1 = 0.65$, so $P((A \cup B)') = \mathbf{0.35}$
$P(B \mid A) = \dfrac{0.1}{0.45} = \mathbf{\dfrac{2}{9}}$

Using Expected Frequencies

Converting probabilities to frequencies out of a convenient total (e.g. 100 or 1000 people) makes conditional questions easier: in the rain example, out of 100 days, 30 rainy days give 12 late days and 70 dry days give 7 late days, so $12$ of the $19$ late days were rainy.

Look for the words “given that”, “of those who” or “a student who… is chosen”. The denominator is then the size of the given group, not the overall total. Independent events satisfy $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$.
