2.4 Non-linear Graphs

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Algebra — spec refs A11–A16 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

This topic covers quadratic graphs and their roots, intercepts and turning points (A11), recognising and sketching linear, quadratic, cubic and reciprocal graphs (A12), real-life graphs including kinematics (A14). At Higher tier it adds turning points by completing the square, exponential and trigonometric graphs (A12), translations and reflections of graphs (A13), gradients and areas under graphs (A15) and circles centred at the origin with their tangents (A16).

2.4.1 Quadratic Graphs

A quadratic graph has equation $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ ($a \ne 0$). Its shape is a parabola:
  • $a > 0$: U-shaped, with a minimum turning point.
  • $a < 0$: ∩-shaped, with a maximum turning point.
Every parabola is symmetrical about a vertical line through its turning point.

Deducing Roots Algebraically

The roots of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ are the solutions of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$. Factorise (or use another method) and set each bracket to zero.

Find the roots and turning point of $y = x^2 + 2x - 8$.

$(x + 4)(x - 2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -4$ or $x = 2$
Line of symmetry: $x = \dfrac{-4 + 2}{2} = -1$,   $y = (-1)^2 + 2(-1) - 8 = -9$
Turning point $\mathbf{(-1, -9)}$
A ball's height is $h = 20t - 5t^2$ metres. When does it land?

$20t - 5t^2 = 0 \Rightarrow 5t(4 - t) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 0$ or $t = 4$. It lands after $\mathbf{4}$ seconds. By symmetry its greatest height is at $t = 2$: $h = 40 - 20 = 20$ m.

Solving Equations Graphically

To solve $f(x) = k$ using the graph of $y = f(x)$, draw the horizontal line $y = k$ and read off the $x$-coordinates where the line meets the curve. The roots (solutions of $f(x) = 0$) are where the curve meets $y = 0$.

Use the table above to solve $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 5$.

Draw $y = 5$. It meets the curve at $x = \mathbf{-2}$ and $x = \mathbf{4}$.

2.4.2 Turning Points by Completing the Square Higher

$$x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c$$ If $y = (x + p)^2 + q$, the turning point is $\mathbf{(-p,\ q)}$ and it is a minimum (since $(x + p)^2 \ge 0$).
Find the turning point of $y = x^2 - 8x + 20$.

$x^2 - 8x + 20 = (x - 4)^2 - 16 + 20 = (x - 4)^2 + 4$
Turning point $\mathbf{(4, 4)}$ (minimum). Since the minimum value 4 is above zero, the graph has no real roots.
Write $x^2 + 6x + 2$ in the form $(x + p)^2 + q$ and state the turning point.

$(x + 3)^2 - 9 + 2 = (x + 3)^2 - 7$, so the turning point is $\mathbf{(-3, -7)}$.
The sign of the $x$-coordinate flips: $(x - 4)^2$ gives $x = 4$; $(x + 3)^2$ gives $x = -3$. The $y$-coordinate keeps its sign.

2.4.3 Cubic and Reciprocal Graphs

TypeGeneral formShape and key features
Linear$y = mx + c$Straight line
Quadratic$y = ax^2 + bx + c$Parabola (U or ∩), one turning point, symmetrical
Cubic$y = ax^3 + \dots$S-shaped; $y = x^3$ passes through the origin; can have 1, 2 or 3 roots. If $a > 0$ it goes from bottom-left to top-right.
Reciprocal$y = \dfrac{k}{x}$, $x \ne 0$Two separate branches; the axes are asymptotes. For $k > 0$, branches in the 1st and 3rd quadrants; symmetrical about $y = x$ and $y = -x$.
An asymptote is a line that a graph gets closer and closer to but never meets. For $y = \frac{1}{x}$: as $x$ gets very large, $y$ gets closer to 0; $x = 0$ is not allowed because you cannot divide by zero.
Find the roots of $y = x^3 - 4x$.

$x(x^2 - 4) = x(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{-2,\ 0,\ 2}$. It is a cubic with three roots (see the graph above).

2.4.4 Exponential and Trigonometric Graphs Higher

Exponential Graphs $y = k^x$

  • Every graph $y = k^x$ ($k > 0$) passes through $(0, 1)$, because $k^0 = 1$.
  • $y$ is always positive: the $x$-axis is an asymptote.
  • $k > 1$: exponential growth (increasing ever more steeply). $0 < k < 1$: exponential decay.
  • Real-life models: compound interest $V = P \times 1.04^t$, population growth, depreciation. The vertical intercept is the starting value $P$.

How many solutions does $\sin x = 0.5$ have for $0° \le x \le 360°$?

The line $y = 0.5$ meets the sine curve twice: $x = 30°$ and, by symmetry about $x = 90°$, $x = 180° - 30° = 150°$. So 2 solutions.
The graphs repeat for angles of any size, including negative angles — use the period and symmetry to find further solutions rather than relying only on your calculator, which gives one answer.

Changes inside the bracket affect $x$ and work the “opposite way”: $f(x - 4)$ moves the graph 4 to the right. Changes outside affect $y$ and work as expected.

2.4.6 Real-Life and Kinematic Graphs

Distance–time graph: gradient = speed; horizontal line = stationary; steeper = faster; a line sloping back down = returning towards the start.
Velocity (speed)–time graph: gradient = acceleration; horizontal line = constant speed; negative gradient = deceleration; area under the graph = distance travelled.
A train travels 15 km in its first 20 minutes, stops for 10 minutes, then returns 15 km in 30 minutes. Find the speed in each moving section.

First section: $15 \div \tfrac{20}{60} = \mathbf{45}$ km/h.   Return: $15 \div \tfrac{30}{60} = \mathbf{30}$ km/h.
A car's speed increases uniformly from 0 to 24 m/s in 8 s. Acceleration $= \dfrac{24}{8} = \mathbf{3}$ m/s².

Filling Containers and Other Contexts

When a container is filled at a constant rate, the depth–time graph is a straight line for any part with a constant cross-section. Wider parts fill more slowly (shallower gradient); narrower parts fill faster (steeper gradient). A container with sloping sides gives a curve.

Reciprocal and Exponential Models

Inverse relationships give reciprocal graphs: if $n$ workers take $t = \frac{24}{n}$ hours, the graph is a reciprocal curve that never reaches the $n$-axis. At Higher, growth and decay ($V = 500 \times 1.04^t$) give exponential graphs; read approximate solutions from the graph, e.g. when the value first exceeds £600.

2.4.7 Gradients and Areas Under Graphs Higher

Gradient of a Curve

  • Instantaneous rate of change at a point: draw a tangent to the curve at that point and find its gradient.
  • Average rate of change between two points: find the gradient of the chord joining them.
  • On a distance–time curve the tangent gives the speed at that instant; on a velocity–time curve it gives the acceleration.

Area Under a Graph

Split the area into triangles, rectangles and trapezia. For a curve, use trapezia of equal width to estimate the area.

Area of a trapezium strip $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{width} \times (y_{\text{left}} + y_{\text{right}})$
A car accelerates from rest to 15 m/s in 6 s, travels at 15 m/s for 10 s, then decelerates to rest in 4 s. Find the total distance.

$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 15 + 10 \times 15 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 15 = 45 + 150 + 30 = \mathbf{225}$ m
A velocity–time curve passes through $(0, 0)$, $(2, 6)$, $(4, 10)$, $(6, 12)$. Estimate the distance in the first 6 s.

$\tfrac{1}{2}(2)(0 + 6) + \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(6 + 10) + \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(10 + 12) = 6 + 16 + 22 = \mathbf{44}$ m
The curve gets less steep, so it bends above the trapezium tops: this is an underestimate.
Always state units: the area under a velocity–time graph (m/s × s) is in metres; the gradient (m/s ÷ s) is in m/s². In financial graphs the gradient is a rate such as £ per year.

2.4.8 Equation of a Circle and Tangents Higher

The circle with centre the origin and radius $r$ has equation $$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$ e.g. $x^2 + y^2 = 49$ has centre $(0, 0)$ and radius 7. A point lies on the circle if its coordinates satisfy the equation.
Tangent at a point $P$: the tangent is perpendicular to the radius $OP$.
  1. Gradient of radius $OP = \dfrac{y_P}{x_P}$.
  2. Tangent gradient = negative reciprocal.
  3. Substitute $P$ into $y = mx + c$ to find $c$.
Find the tangent to $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at $(3, 4)$.

Radius gradient $= \tfrac{4}{3}$, so tangent gradient $= -\tfrac{3}{4}$.
$4 = -\tfrac{3}{4}(3) + c \Rightarrow c = 4 + \tfrac{9}{4} = \tfrac{25}{4}$
$y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + \tfrac{25}{4}$, i.e. $\mathbf{3x + 4y = 25}$
Find the tangent to $x^2 + y^2 = 20$ at $(2, -4)$.

Check: $4 + 16 = 20$ ✓. Radius gradient $= \tfrac{-4}{2} = -2$, so tangent gradient $= \tfrac{1}{2}$.
$-4 = \tfrac{1}{2}(2) + c \Rightarrow c = -5$   $\Rightarrow$   $\mathbf{y = \tfrac{1}{2}x - 5}$
The radius is $\sqrt{r^2}$: for $x^2 + y^2 = 50$ the radius is $\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}$, not 25. To find the equation from a point on the circle, use $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, e.g. through $(6, -8)$: $r^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$.
