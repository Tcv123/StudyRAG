4.3 Circles & Circle Theorems

AQA GCSE Mathematics 8300 — Geometry and Measures — spec refs G9, G10, G18 — Papers 1 (non-calculator), 2 and 3 (calculator)

This topic covers the vocabulary of circles, calculating circumference and area, arc lengths and sector areas, and (Higher tier) the standard circle theorems — including how to apply them with reasons and how to prove them.

4.3.1 Parts of a Circle (G9)

TermMeaning
CentreThe fixed point equidistant from every point on the circle
RadiusCentre to circumference
DiameterChord through the centre; $d = 2r$
CircumferenceThe perimeter of the circle
ChordLine joining two points on the circle
TangentLine touching the circle at one point
ArcPart of the circumference
SectorRegion bounded by two radii and an arc
SegmentRegion bounded by a chord and an arc
Do not mix up sector (two radii, "pizza slice") and segment (chord, "cut-off piece"). A chord splits a circle into a minor and a major segment; two radii split it into a minor and a major sector. A semicircle is a sector with angle 180°, and a quadrant is a sector with angle 90°.

4.3.2 Circumference and Area

Circumference: $C = \pi d = 2\pi r$
Area: $A = \pi r^2$

Reverse: $r = \dfrac{C}{2\pi}$    and    $r = \sqrt{\dfrac{A}{\pi}}$
A circle has diameter 7 cm. Find its circumference and area, to 1 decimal place.

$C = \pi \times 7 = 21.99\ldots \approx \mathbf{22.0}$ cm
$r = 3.5$, so $A = \pi \times 3.5^2 = 38.48\ldots \approx \mathbf{38.5}$ cm²
A circle has circumference $12\pi$ cm. Find its area in terms of $\pi$.

$2\pi r = 12\pi \Rightarrow r = 6$.   $A = \pi \times 6^2 = \mathbf{36\pi}$ cm².

On the non-calculator paper you are often asked to leave answers in terms of $\pi$: treat $\pi$ like a letter and do not round. For example, a ring between circles of radius 5 cm and 3 cm has area $25\pi - 9\pi = 16\pi$ cm².

Check whether you have been given the radius or the diameter. Using the diameter in $\pi r^2$ makes the area four times too big — a very common lost mark.

4.3.3 Arc Length and Sector Area (G18)

A sector with angle $\theta$ is the fraction $\dfrac{\theta}{360}$ of the whole circle, so its arc is that fraction of the circumference and its area is that fraction of the circle's area.

Arc length $= \dfrac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$

Sector area $= \dfrac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$

Perimeter of a sector $=$ arc length $+\ 2r$
A sector has radius 6 cm and angle 120°. Find (a) the arc length, (b) the area, (c) the perimeter.

(a) $\dfrac{120}{360} \times 2\pi \times 6 = \dfrac{1}{3} \times 12\pi = 4\pi \approx \mathbf{12.6}$ cm
(b) $\dfrac{120}{360} \times \pi \times 6^2 = \dfrac{1}{3} \times 36\pi = 12\pi \approx \mathbf{37.7}$ cm²
(c) $4\pi + 6 + 6 = 4\pi + 12 \approx \mathbf{24.6}$ cm

Working backwards to find the angle or radius

A sector has radius 5 cm and arc length 7 cm. Find the angle.

$\dfrac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi \times 5 = 7 \Rightarrow \theta = \dfrac{7 \times 360}{10\pi} = 80.21\ldots \approx \mathbf{80.2°}$
A sector has angle 40° and arc length $8\pi$ cm. Find the radius.

$\dfrac{40}{360} \times 2\pi r = 8\pi \Rightarrow \dfrac{2\pi r}{9} = 8\pi \Rightarrow 2r = 72 \Rightarrow r = \mathbf{36}$ cm

Area of a segment

Segment area $=$ sector area $-$ area of the triangle formed by the two radii and the chord.

Radius 10 cm, angle at centre 90°. Find the area of the minor segment.

Sector $= \dfrac{90}{360} \times \pi \times 10^2 = 25\pi$. Triangle $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 10 = 50$.
Segment $= 25\pi - 50 = 28.54\ldots \approx \mathbf{28.5}$ cm²
Higher Radius 8 cm, angle at centre 60°. Find the area of the minor segment.

Sector $= \dfrac{60}{360} \times \pi \times 64 = 33.51\ldots$. Triangle $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 8 \times \sin 60° = 27.71\ldots$
Segment $= 33.51 - 27.71 \approx \mathbf{5.80}$ cm²

Composite shapes

A window is a rectangle 1.2 m wide and 1.5 m tall with a semicircle on top. Find its perimeter.

Curved edge $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times \pi \times 1.2 = 0.6\pi = 1.885$ m. Straight edges $= 1.2 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 4.2$ m.
Perimeter $= 4.2 + 1.885 = \mathbf{6.09}$ m (the top of the rectangle is inside the shape, so it is not counted).
For a perimeter, trace round the outside edge with your finger and list every piece. The straight sides of a sector (two radii) and the diameter of a semicircle are easy to forget.

4.3.4 The Circle Theorems (G10) Higher

In AQA GCSE, the circle theorems are Higher-tier content. You must be able to apply them, giving the theorem as a reason, and prove them. Learn the exact wording — "circle theorem" on its own scores nothing.

#Theorem (wording to use as a reason)
1The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference (subtended by the same arc).
2The angle in a semicircle is 90°.
3Angles in the same segment are equal.
4Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°.
5The tangent at any point is perpendicular to the radius at that point.
6Tangents from an external point are equal in length.
7The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
8Alternate segment theorem: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.
A cyclic quadrilateral is a quadrilateral whose four vertices all lie on the circumference of a circle. The alternate segment is the segment on the other side of the chord from the angle between the tangent and the chord.

Applying the theorems

A and B lie on a circle centre O and angle $OAB = 28°$. C is on the major arc AB. Find angle $ACB$.

$OA = OB$ (radii), so triangle OAB is isosceles and angle $OBA = 28°$.
Angle $AOB = 180 - 28 - 28 = 124°$ (angles in a triangle).
Angle $ACB = 124 \div 2 = \mathbf{62°}$ (angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference).
TA and TB are tangents touching a circle centre O at A and B. Angle $ATB = 40°$. Find angle $AOB$ and angle $OAB$.

Angles $OAT = OBT = 90°$ (tangent is perpendicular to the radius).
Angle $AOB = 360 - 90 - 90 - 40 = \mathbf{140°}$ (angles in a quadrilateral).
Triangle OAB is isosceles ($OA = OB$), so angle $OAB = (180 - 140) \div 2 = \mathbf{20°}$.
ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral with angle $ABC = 3x$ and angle $ADC = x + 40°$. Find $x$.

$3x + x + 40 = 180$ (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°)
$4x = 140$, so $x = \mathbf{35}$, giving angle $ABC = 105°$ and angle $ADC = 75°$.
A chord of length 24 cm is in a circle of radius 13 cm. How far is the chord from the centre?

The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so the half-chord is 12 cm.
Distance $= \sqrt{13^2 - 12^2} = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5}$ cm.
The tangent at P makes an angle of 58° with chord PQ. R is in the alternate segment and angle $PQR = 71°$. Find angle $QPR$.

Angle $PRQ = 58°$ (alternate segment theorem).
Angle $QPR = 180 - 58 - 71 = \mathbf{51°}$ (angles in a triangle).
Look for hidden isosceles triangles: any triangle with two radii as sides is isosceles. Mark radii and tangents on the diagram first, then add every angle you can find, writing a reason beside each one.

4.3.5 Proving the Circle Theorems Higher

AQA expects you to prove the standard theorems and to use them to prove related results. Most proofs start by drawing in radii to create isosceles triangles.

Prove that the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference.

Join C to O and extend the line to a point D. $OA = OC$ (radii), so triangle OAC is isosceles: let angle $OAC$ = angle $OCA = x$.
The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the interior opposite angles, so angle $AOD = 2x$.
Similarly $OB = OC$: let angle $OBC$ = angle $OCB = y$, so angle $BOD = 2y$.
Angle $ACB = x + y$ and angle $AOB = 2x + 2y = 2(x + y)$. Hence the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference. $\blacksquare$
Prove that the angle in a semicircle is 90°.

AB is a diameter with centre O; C is on the circle. $OA = OB = OC$ (radii), so triangles OAC and OBC are isosceles.
Let angle $OAC$ = angle $OCA = x$ and angle $OBC$ = angle $OCB = y$.
Angles in triangle ABC: $x + y + (x + y) = 180$, so $2(x + y) = 180$ and $x + y = 90$.
Angle $ACB = x + y = 90°$. $\blacksquare$  (Alternatively: angle $AOB = 180°$, so by the centre theorem angle $ACB = 90°$.)
Prove that opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°.

Let ABCD be cyclic with centre O, angle $BAD = x$ and angle $BCD = y$. Join OB and OD.
The angle at O on the side of C is $2x$, and the reflex angle at O on the side of A is $2y$ (centre theorem).
Together they make a full turn: $2x + 2y = 360$, so $x + y = 180°$. $\blacksquare$
Prove that the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.

Let M be the foot of the perpendicular from O to chord AB. In triangles OMA and OMB: angle $OMA$ = angle $OMB = 90°$; $OA = OB$ (radii, the hypotenuses); $OM$ is common.
So the triangles are congruent (RHS) and $AM = MB$. $\blacksquare$
Prove that tangents from an external point are equal.

Tangents from T touch the circle at A and B. Angle $OAT$ = angle $OBT = 90°$ (tangent perpendicular to radius); $OA = OB$ (radii); $OT$ is common.
So triangles OAT and OBT are congruent (RHS), and $TA = TB$. $\blacksquare$
Outline proof of the alternate segment theorem.

Let the tangent at A make angle $z$ with chord AB. Draw the diameter AD; angle $DAT = 90°$ (tangent perpendicular to radius), so angle $DAB = 90 - z$.
Angle $ABD = 90°$ (angle in a semicircle), so angle $ADB = 180 - 90 - (90 - z) = z$.
Any angle $ACB$ in the same segment as D equals angle $ADB$ (angles in the same segment), so angle $ACB = z$. $\blacksquare$
In a "prove" question, every step needs a reason, and you must reach the required statement exactly. Use letters for unknown angles ($x$, $y$) rather than specific numbers — a numerical example is not a proof.
