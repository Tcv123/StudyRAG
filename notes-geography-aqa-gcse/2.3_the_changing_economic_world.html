2.3 The Changing Economic World

1. Defining Development

Development refers to improvements in the standard of living and quality of life for people within a country. It is measured across two broad dimensions:

  • Economic development — growth in wealth, industrialisation, trade, and GDP per capita.
  • Human development — improvements in quality of life: health, education, wellbeing, and political freedom.

Development is best understood as a continuum — a sliding scale from the least to most developed — rather than a binary division of "developed" and "developing" worlds. Countries sit at different points on this continuum and move along it over time.

Classification of Countries

HICs (High Income Countries) — GNI per capita above ~$13,900 (World Bank threshold, 2025). Examples: USA, UK, Germany, Japan, Australia.
LICs (Low Income Countries) — GNI per capita below ~$1,135 (2025). Examples: Niger, South Sudan, DR Congo, Afghanistan.
NEEs (Newly Emerging Economies) — Countries rapidly industrialising and growing, often moving from LIC/MIC status. Examples: China, India, Brazil, Nigeria, Mexico, Indonesia, Turkey, South Africa.
Exam Tip: Avoid using the terms "first world / third world" or "developed / developing" in exam answers — they imply a binary and are outdated. Use HIC, LIC, NEE, or "more/less economically developed country" (MEDC/LEDC). Examiners reward precise terminology.

3. Patterns of Development

Wealth and development are unevenly distributed globally. A broad pattern is visible in the North-South divide, summarised by the Brandt Line.

Key patterns of global wealth distribution:

  • HICs are concentrated in North America, Western Europe, Japan, and Australia/NZ.
  • LICs are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South/Southeast Asia.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest concentration of the world's LICs.
  • NEEs — the BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) and MINT (Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey) — are rapidly changing the global economic landscape.
  • The Brandt Line is a simplification: Australia and New Zealand are geographically south but economically "north".
Exam Tip: The Brandt Line is a useful concept but always note its limitations — it oversimplifies a complex spectrum. Australia and NZ are exceptions. Many NEEs are in the "south" but are growing rapidly.

5. Causes of Uneven Development

Uneven development has multiple, interrelated causes — both physical and human.

Physical Causes

  • Landlocked countries — no access to sea trade routes, making trade expensive (Mali, Chad, Zambia). 16 of the world's 32 landlocked developing countries are in Africa.
  • Tropical climate — high temperatures and humidity increase prevalence of diseases (malaria, dengue) which reduce workforce productivity. Seasonal droughts reduce agricultural output.
  • Natural hazards — frequent earthquakes, floods, cyclones destroy infrastructure and divert development funds to disaster relief (Haiti, Bangladesh).
  • Lack of natural resources — limited mineral or energy wealth restricts income generation. However, the "resource curse" shows that over-reliance on a single resource (e.g. oil in Nigeria) can actually worsen inequality if wealth is poorly distributed.

Human Causes

  • Colonialism — European powers extracted resources, cash crops, and labour from colonies for centuries. Infrastructure built to serve colonial export needs, not local development. Borders drawn arbitrarily, ignoring ethnic groups, causing post-independence conflict.
  • Trade inequality — "Terms of trade" work against LICs. LICs export primary commodities (cocoa, coffee, copper) at volatile, low prices; import manufactured goods at higher prices. "Commodity trap" — prices set by global markets, not producers.
  • Debt — Many LICs borrowed heavily in the 1970s-80s for development; interest repayments now exceed incoming aid. World Bank and IMF Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs) required cuts to health/education spending — worsening development.
  • Political instability — corruption diverts funds; civil war destroys infrastructure and displaces populations. South Sudan — world's youngest nation — suffered civil war from 2013 to 2018 and continuing instability since, preventing development. Conflict deters foreign investment.
  • Poor governance — weak institutions, lack of rule of law, and corruption reduce effectiveness of development spending.
Exam Tip: "Explain why development is uneven" questions require you to state, explain, and link multiple causes. Don't just list them — explain the mechanism (e.g. "landlocked countries face higher transport costs, which makes imports more expensive and reduces trade, slowing economic growth").

7. Trade and Development

International trade is a major driver of development — but access to trade and its terms are unequal.

Fairtrade

Fairtrade — a trading partnership that ensures producers in LICs receive a guaranteed minimum price for their goods, plus a "Fairtrade Premium" (extra money for community projects). Managed by the Fairtrade Foundation. Nearly 2 million farmers and workers in around 70 countries. Products include cocoa, coffee, bananas, cotton, gold.
  • Benefits: stable income, social premium for schools/wells/healthcare, empowers smallholder farmers, encourages environmental standards.
  • Criticisms: only a small fraction of the Fairtrade price reaches farmers; consumers pay more but benefit may be limited; not all large-scale producers can access Fairtrade certification.

Trade Blocs and the WTO

  • Trade blocs (e.g. EU single market, NAFTA/USMCA) reduce tariffs between member countries — benefitting members but excluding LICs.
  • WTO (World Trade Organisation) — sets global trade rules. Accused of favouring HIC agendas; agricultural subsidies in the EU/USA undercut LIC farmers.

Commodity Dependence

  • Ghana — heavily dependent on cocoa and gold exports. When cocoa prices fall, government revenue falls sharply.
  • Nigeria — oil accounts for ~90% of export revenue and ~70% of government income. The "Dutch Disease": oil wealth causes the currency to rise, making other exports uncompetitive and damaging manufacturing.

9. Other Strategies to Reduce Inequality

Microfinance / Microcredit

Microfinance — small loans (micro-loans) given to individuals (often women) in LICs to start or grow small businesses, without requiring collateral. Developed by the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh (founded 1983 by Muhammad Yunus). Over 9 million borrowers, 97% women. Average loan ~$200. Success rate high: ~98% repayment.

Benefits: empowers women, breaks poverty cycle, stimulates local economy, does not create national debt.
Criticisms: high interest rates in some schemes, small scale — cannot address systemic inequality.

Debt Relief

The HIPC (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries) initiative — IMF/World Bank programme that cancelled around $76 billion of debt for over 35 qualifying countries. Freed up government funds for health and education spending (Mozambique, Uganda, Bolivia). Conditions applied — some argue these conditions still favour creditor countries.

Intermediate/Appropriate Technology

Appropriate technology — technology that is suited to the local environment, skills, culture, and economic circumstances of the community. Often simple, low-cost, repairable locally. Championed by NGO Practical Action (formerly ITDG).
  • Wind pumps for irrigation (Kenya) — no fuel needed, maintained locally.
  • Solar cookers (India) — reduce firewood use, improve health (less indoor smoke).
  • Biosand filters (Cambodia) — clean water at low cost.
  • Treadle pumps (Bangladesh) — foot-powered irrigation for small farmers.

Tourism

Tourism brings foreign exchange to LICs and NEEs. Kenya — safari and beach tourism earns billions of dollars a year, creating jobs in rural areas. Maldives — ~28% of GDP from tourism. However: leakage (profits leaving to TNC-owned hotels in HICs), environmental damage (reef degradation), seasonality of income, vulnerability to global events (COVID-19 devastated Maldives tourism).

Export Processing Zones (EPZs)

EPZs are designated areas where companies pay reduced taxes and have relaxed regulations to attract foreign investment and manufacturing. China used EPZs (Shenzhen Special Economic Zone from 1980) as a key driver of rapid industrialisation and export growth.

10. Case Study: Tourism in Jamaica (Reducing the Development Gap)

Tourism in Jamaica, an upper-middle-income Caribbean country
  • Jamaica is an island in the Caribbean with a tropical climate, beaches, coral reefs, the Blue Mountains, waterfalls (Dunn's River Falls) and a rich musical culture (reggae, Bob Marley).
  • It receives over 4 million visitors a year (stay-over and cruise visitors combined) — mainly from the USA, Canada and the UK.
  • Tourism contributes around 30% of GDP (direct and indirect) and employs over 200,000 people, roughly a fifth of all jobs.

Benefits:

  • Foreign exchange income and taxes help pay for infrastructure — new roads (e.g. the Highway 2000 network), airport upgrades and water supply.
  • Jobs in hotels, restaurants, transport and tour guiding; the multiplier effect supports farmers, fishermen and craft producers.
  • Raises the international profile of Jamaica and encourages investment.

Problems:

  • Many jobs are seasonal and low-paid; many resorts are foreign-owned, so profits leak overseas (economic leakage). "All-inclusive" resorts mean tourists spend little money in local businesses.
  • Environmental damage — coral reef damage, beach erosion, waste and water shortages near resorts.
  • A gap between wealthy tourist enclaves and nearby poor communities; vulnerability to hurricanes and global recessions (tourism collapsed during the 2020 pandemic).

Evaluation: Tourism has helped Jamaica's economy grow and reduced the development gap to some extent, but it depends on keeping leakage low and spreading benefits to local people — e.g. through community-based ecotourism in the Blue Mountains and Cockpit Country.

11. Case Study: Nigeria — A Newly Emerging Economy

Nigeria — location and importance
  • Nigeria is in West Africa, on the Gulf of Guinea, bordered by Benin, Niger, Chad and Cameroon.
  • It has the largest population in Africa — over 220 million — and one of its largest economies.
  • It is a major oil producer and a member of OPEC; its film (Nollywood) and music industries are influential across Africa and beyond.
  • Nigeria contributes peacekeeping troops to the UN and African Union and is a leading member of ECOWAS (the Economic Community of West African States).

Wider political, social, cultural and environmental context

  • Political: a former British colony (independent in 1960); civil war (1967–70) and long periods of military rule, with democratic civilian government since 1999. Insurgency by Boko Haram in the north-east has displaced millions.
  • Social and cultural: over 250 ethnic groups (largest: Hausa-Fulani, Yoruba and Igbo); the north is mainly Muslim and the south mainly Christian.
  • Environmental: a range of environments, from semi-arid savanna in the north (prone to desertification) through tropical forest to the Niger Delta's mangroves and swamps in the south.

Changing industrial structure

SectorChange
Primary (farming, oil)Agriculture still employs about a third of workers, but its share of GDP has fallen to around 20–25%. Oil makes up around 90% of exports but under 10% of GDP.
Secondary (manufacturing)Growing slowly — food processing, cement (Dangote Cement), vehicle assembly, textiles. Manufacturing creates more reliable jobs and multiplier effects.
Tertiary and quaternary (services)Now over half of GDP — banking, telecommunications (MTN, Airtel), retail, film and IT. Lagos is a growing tech hub.

The role of transnational corporations (TNCs)

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Shell and other oil companies have invested billions of dollars and pay large sums in taxes and royalties.Oil spills in the Niger Delta have polluted farmland, creeks and drinking water. Gas flaring releases CO₂ and toxic gases.

Changing political and trading relationships

  • Historically, trade was mainly with the UK and Europe. Today Nigeria's main trading partners include China (its largest source of imports), India, the USA and the Netherlands.
  • China has invested heavily in infrastructure — railways (Lagos–Ibadan, Abuja–Kaduna), the Lekki Deep Sea Port and roads — often in return for access to oil and other resources.
  • Nigeria joined the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), aiming to increase trade within Africa.

International aid

  • Nigeria receives aid for health, education and humanitarian needs — e.g. UK and US funding for malaria nets and vaccination; Gavi and the WHO's role in eradicating wild polio (Africa was declared free of wild polio in 2020).
  • Aid has improved health outcomes, but corruption has sometimes meant that money does not reach those it was intended for.

Environmental impacts of economic development

  • Oil spills in the Niger Delta — thousands of spills since the 1970s have destroyed fisheries and mangroves.
  • Air and water pollution from industry, traffic and waste in Lagos; illegal dumping of toxic waste.
  • Deforestation for farming, logging and fuelwood — Nigeria has lost a large share of its original forest.
  • Desertification in the north, worsened by overgrazing.

Effects of economic development on quality of life

  • Improvements: life expectancy has risen (from about 46 in 2000 to around 54 today); more access to safe water, mobile phones and the internet; a growing middle class.
  • Limits: wealth is very unequal — tens of millions of Nigerians live in extreme poverty, more than in almost any other country; unreliable electricity; high youth unemployment.
Exam Tip: The NEE case study is often tested with 9-mark "Evaluate the role of TNCs" or "To what extent has economic development improved quality of life?" questions. Always balance positives and negatives with Nigerian evidence and finish with a clear judgement.

12. Globalisation and Development

Globalisation — the increasing interconnectedness of the world through trade, investment, migration, and communications.

Transnational Corporations (TNCs)

TNCs (Transnational Corporations) — companies that operate in multiple countries. Examples: Nike, Apple, Shell, Unilever, Samsung. They locate headquarters in HICs but manufacturing in LICs/NEEs to exploit lower labour costs, tax advantages, and weaker regulations.
Positive impacts on LICs/NEEsNegative impacts on LICs/NEEs
Creates employment (often thousands of jobs)Low wages — often close to minimum; poor working conditions
Technology transfer to local workers and firmsProfits repatriated back to HIC headquarters — "leakage"
Brings Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)Environmental damage — weaker regulations exploited
Develops infrastructure (roads, ports)Worker exploitation — long hours, unsafe conditions (Rana Plaza, Bangladesh 2013)
Increases exports and government tax revenueUndermines local industries through competition

The Global Shift

Since the 1970s–80s, manufacturing has shifted from HICs (USA, UK, Germany) to NEEs and LICs (China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Mexico). This "global shift" has brought rapid industrialisation to Asia in particular, reducing absolute poverty for hundreds of millions of people. However, it has also led to deindustrialisation and job losses in traditional manufacturing regions of HICs (e.g. Rust Belt, USA; steel towns in Wales/NE England).

Exam Tip: For TNC evaluation questions, structure your answer around: economic impacts (jobs, FDI, tax), social impacts (working conditions, urbanisation), and environmental impacts. Use a named TNC with specific details — Nike in Vietnam, or Apple's Foxconn factories in China — to access top marks.

13. Economic Futures in the UK — Causes of Change

CauseExplanation
DeindustrialisationThe decline of traditional industries such as coal mining, steel, shipbuilding and textiles from the 1960s onwards. Coal mining employed around a million people in the 1920s; by 2015 the last deep coal mine (Kellingley) had closed. Causes: cheaper imports, exhaustion of resources, mechanisation.
GlobalisationManufacturing moved to countries with lower labour costs (the global shift); the UK specialised in services, finance and high-tech industries instead.
Government policiesPrivatisation and the closure of state-owned industries in the 1980s; later investment in regeneration, enterprise zones, the Northern Powerhouse and "levelling up". Membership and then departure from the EU (2020) changed trade relationships.

14. The UK's Post-Industrial Economy

Post-industrial economy — an economy in which most people work in the tertiary (services) and quaternary (knowledge, research and IT) sectors rather than in manufacturing. Over 80% of UK jobs are now in services.
  • Information technology: software, digital and tech companies — London's "Tech City" (Shoreditch), Manchester, Cambridge.
  • Service industries: retail, tourism, health, education.
  • Finance: the City of London and Canary Wharf, Edinburgh and Leeds — financial services contribute around 8–9% of UK economic output.
  • Research: universities and research institutes, pharmaceutical and biotechnology firms.
Science and business parks — Cambridge Science Park
  • Founded by Trinity College in 1970 — the oldest science park in the UK. It is home to over 100 companies employing about 7,000 people, mainly in biotechnology, IT and research.
  • Location factors: links with Cambridge University research and graduates; access to the M11 and A14 and to Stansted airport; an attractive environment.
  • It is part of "Silicon Fen", the cluster of high-tech firms around Cambridge.

Environmental impact of industry — Torr Quarry, Somerset

  • Torr Quarry, near Frome, is one of the UK's largest limestone quarries, supplying crushed rock for roads and construction.
  • Impacts: visual scarring, noise, dust and heavy lorry traffic; loss of habitats.
  • Making it more sustainable: about 75% of the stone leaves by rail rather than road; screening banks and tree planting hide the quarry; the quarry floor is being restored as lakes and wildlife habitats; blasting is limited to set times.

16. Improvements to Transport Infrastructure

  • Roads: "smart motorways" using the hard shoulder as a running lane (now paused for new schemes over safety concerns); upgrades to routes such as the A14 Cambridge–Huntingdon.
  • Rail: the Elizabeth line (Crossrail) across London opened in 2022. HS2 Phase 1 (London–Birmingham) is under construction, though the northern legs were cancelled in 2023. Northern rail electrification and TransPennine upgrades aim to improve links between northern cities.
  • Ports: Liverpool2 deep-water container terminal; London Gateway port on the Thames estuary.
  • Airports: Heathrow is close to capacity; a third runway has long been proposed and remains controversial because of noise, air pollution and climate concerns.

17. The North–South Divide

  • The UK has an economic divide between the more prosperous South East (especially London) and parts of the North, Midlands, Wales and Northern Ireland, which were hit harder by deindustrialisation.
  • Differences include wages, house prices, life expectancy, educational results and unemployment. London's output per head is around 1.7 times the UK average.

Strategies to resolve regional differences

  • Northern Powerhouse (from 2014) — investment in transport, science and innovation in northern cities.
  • Devolution — powers and budgets given to elected mayors (e.g. Greater Manchester, West Midlands) and to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • Enterprise zones and freeports — tax breaks and simpler planning to attract businesses to struggling areas.
  • Levelling up funds — money for town centres, transport and regeneration in poorer areas; moving some government jobs out of London (e.g. Treasury campus in Darlington).

18. The UK's Place in the Wider World

  • Trade: major trading partners include the EU (still the largest overall), the USA and China. Since leaving the EU, the UK has signed new trade deals (e.g. with Australia and New Zealand) and joined the CPTPP trade bloc (2024).
  • Culture: a global influence through the English language, music, film, sport (the Premier League is watched worldwide) and the BBC.
  • Transport: Heathrow is a global hub; the Channel Tunnel links the UK to mainland Europe.
  • Electronic communication: undersea cables connect the UK to North America and Europe, making London a key centre for global data and finance.
  • The Commonwealth and international organisations: the UK is a member of the Commonwealth (56 countries), the G7, G20, NATO and a permanent member of the UN Security Council.
Exam Tip: Questions on the UK economy often ask you to "use a named example" — Cambridge Science Park, Torr Quarry, South Cambridgeshire and the Outer Hebrides give you specific, memorable evidence to quote.
