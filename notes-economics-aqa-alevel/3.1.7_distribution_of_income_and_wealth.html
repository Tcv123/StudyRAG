3.1.7 The Distribution of Income and Wealth: Poverty and Inequality

Income and Wealth: The Essential Distinction

Income is a flow of money received by an individual or household over a period of time — a week, a month or a year. It includes wages and salaries, rent, interest, dividends, profit and transfer payments (state benefits and pensions).

Wealth is a stock of assets owned at a single point in time, valued net of any debt secured against them. It includes property, private pension entitlements, financial assets (savings, shares, bonds) and physical possessions.

The flow/stock distinction is the single most examinable idea in this section. A footballer on £100,000 a week has a very large income; a retired person living on the state pension in a mortgage-free house has a low income but substantial wealth. The two are related but they are not the same variable.

The Relationship Between Income and Wealth

The two reinforce one another, which is why inequality tends to persist across generations:

  • Wealth generates income. Property produces rent, shares produce dividends, savings produce interest. Someone with a large stock of assets receives an unearned flow of income without working.
  • Income accumulates into wealth. Income not consumed is saved. Higher-income households have a higher marginal propensity to save, so they convert more of each extra pound into assets.
  • Asset prices magnify the gap. When house prices or share prices rise, the gain accrues only to those who already own the asset. Non-owners gain nothing and face higher rents.
  • Inheritance transmits the stock. Wealth passes between generations largely intact, whereas earnings ability does not. This is the main reason wealth is more unequally distributed than income.

Economists also distinguish marketable wealth (assets that can be sold, such as shares or a second home) from non-marketable wealth (entitlements that cannot be sold, such as accrued occupational pension rights). Because private pension wealth is the second-largest component of UK household wealth, this distinction matters when judging how much of measured wealth is actually available to spend.

UK Data: The Distribution of Income (DWP, Households Below Average Income, 2024/25)
The Gini coefficient for UK household income was 33% before housing costs (BHC) and 37% after housing costs (AHC) in 2024/25. A couple with no children needed a disposable income below £366 a week BHC to fall in the lowest-income tenth of the population, while the highest-income tenth needed at least £1,358 a week — 3.7 times as much. In 2023/24 the top fifth of individuals received 35% of total disposable household income and the bottom fifth received 8%. OECD comparisons place UK income inequality among the highest in Europe, although below that of the United States.
UK Data: The Distribution of Wealth (ONS Wealth and Assets Survey, April 2020 – March 2022, Great Britain)
The Gini coefficient for total household wealth was 0.59 — far higher than the figure for income. The wealthiest tenth of households held around 43% of all wealth, while the least wealthy half of households held about 9%. To be in the wealthiest decile a household needed total wealth of £1,200,500 or more. Exam Tip: Examiners repeatedly report candidates using "income" and "wealth" interchangeably. Signal the distinction explicitly in the first line of any answer on this topic — "income is a flow, wealth is a stock" — and then quote the contrast between the UK income Gini (33% BHC in 2024/25) and the wealth Gini (0.59 in 2020–22). One sentence of accurate data will lift an answer into the top level for knowledge.

Causes of Inequality in the Distribution of Income and Wealth

CauseHow it creates inequality
Wage differentialsWages are set in individual labour markets by the demand for and supply of each type of labour. Occupations with high marginal revenue product and scarce, hard-to-acquire skills (surgeons, barristers, software engineers) command far higher wages than occupations with elastic labour supply (cleaning, retail, hospitality). Trade union power, monopsony employers and compensating differentials widen the gaps further.
UnemploymentThe unemployed lose earned income entirely and rely on benefits, which replace only a fraction of previous earnings. Long-term and structural unemployment causes skills to depreciate, so the income loss persists. Worklessness is the single strongest predictor of household poverty.
Age and the life cycleEarnings typically follow a hump shape: low in early adulthood, peaking in middle age, then falling in retirement. Wealth follows the opposite pattern, accumulating through working life as mortgages are repaid and pensions build. Some measured inequality is therefore simply people at different stages of the same life path, not permanently different groups.
InheritanceWealth transferred at death or as lifetime gifts allows advantage to pass between generations without being earned. Inherited deposits also determine who can buy property, which is the largest single component of UK wealth.
Ownership of assetsThose who own property, shares and pension funds receive rent, dividends and capital gains. Because asset ownership is far more concentrated than earnings, rising asset prices increase wealth inequality directly.
Differences in human capitalEducation, training, qualifications and health determine productivity and therefore earnings. Access to high-quality education is itself correlated with parental income, creating a self-reinforcing cycle.
DiscriminationWhere employers pay different wages, or restrict access to occupations, on grounds of sex, ethnicity, age or disability, groups earn less than their productivity justifies. Discrimination shifts the demand curve for the affected group's labour to the left and can trap workers in low-paid occupational segments.
Household compositionHousehold income depends on how many earners and how many dependants a household contains. A dual-earner couple with no children has a far higher equivalised income than a lone parent with three children, even at identical hourly wages. This is why official statistics equivalise incomes for household size before comparing them.
Exam Tip: Causes of inequality and causes of poverty overlap but are not identical. Inequality is about the whole distribution; poverty is about the bottom of it. Wage differentials at the very top (bankers, chief executives) raise inequality but have no effect on poverty. Make the distinction explicit rather than recycling one list for both questions.

Measuring Inequality: The Lorenz Curve

A Lorenz curve plots the cumulative percentage of the population (ranked from poorest to richest) on the horizontal axis against the cumulative percentage of total income (or wealth) received by them on the vertical axis.

The 45° line is the line of perfect equality: on it, the poorest 20% receive exactly 20% of income, the poorest 50% receive 50%, and so on. The further the Lorenz curve bows away from the 45° line, the greater the inequality.
Figure 1: The Lorenz curve. Area A is the gap between the line of perfect equality and the Lorenz curve; area B is the area beneath the Lorenz curve. The Gini coefficient is A divided by (A + B). The plotted curve uses UK disposable income shares: the bottom fifth receives 8% of income and the top fifth 35%, so the bottom 80% cumulatively receive 65%.

The Gini Coefficient

The Gini coefficient converts the Lorenz curve into a single number. It is the ratio of the area between the line of perfect equality and the Lorenz curve (A) to the total area beneath the line of perfect equality (A + B).
Gini coefficient = A / (A + B)
  • Gini = 0 — perfect equality. The Lorenz curve lies exactly on the 45° line, so area A is zero.
  • Gini = 1 — perfect inequality. One person receives all the income, so area B is zero.
  • It is often quoted as a percentage between 0 and 100 rather than a decimal between 0 and 1. The UK income figure of 33% BHC and the wealth figure of 0.59 are the same measure expressed in the two different conventions.
  • A rising Gini means inequality is increasing; a falling Gini means the distribution is becoming more equal.
StrengthsLimitations
Lorenz curveShows the whole distribution visually; reveals where in the distribution inequality lies; allows two distributions or two years to be compared directly on one diagramOnly a picture — no single number to rank countries; if two curves cross, neither distribution is unambiguously more unequal; relies on grouped data that conceals variation within each group

Quintile and Decile Shares

A simpler alternative is to rank households by income and split them into five equal groups (quintiles, 20% each) or ten equal groups (deciles, 10% each), then report the share of total income each group receives. The quintile ratio (S80/S20) divides the income share of the top fifth by that of the bottom fifth.

Worked Example: Constructing a Lorenz Curve from Quintile Data

Data questions in this topic usually supply the share of income received by each fifth of the population and ask you to plot or interpret the curve. The Lorenz curve is plotted from the cumulative column, never from the raw shares — this is the most common error in exam scripts.

Plotting the last two columns as coordinates — (20, 8), (40, 20), (60, 36), (80, 65), (100, 100) — and joining them from the origin produces the curve in Figure 1. Note the properties that must always hold:

  • The curve starts at (0, 0) — nought per cent of the population receive nought per cent of income — and ends at (100, 100), because 100% of the population necessarily receive 100% of income.
  • It is never above the 45° line, because the poorest x% of the population cannot receive more than x% of income once they are ranked from poorest upwards.
  • It gets steeper as it moves right, because each successive group is richer than the one before it. A convex-upward curve would mean the ranking had been done incorrectly.
Exam Tip — Calculation Questions: If you are given two years of quintile data and asked what happened to inequality, compare the bottom and top shares and state the direction of change in the Gini. If the bottom quintile's share falls while the top quintile's share rises, the Lorenz curve has bowed further from the diagonal and the Gini has risen. You will not be asked to compute a Gini coefficient from areas by hand at A-level, but you must be able to say what a change in its value means.

Before and After Housing Costs

UK income statistics are published on two bases. Before housing costs (BHC) measures disposable income including the money later spent on rent or mortgage interest. After housing costs (AHC) deducts rent, mortgage interest, water charges and buildings insurance first.

Housing costs absorb a much larger share of low incomes than of high incomes, so the AHC distribution is always more unequal. In 2024/25 the Gini was 33% BHC but 37% AHC — a gap created entirely by housing. AHC figures are generally regarded as the better guide to living standards for low-income households, particularly private renters, because their housing costs are effectively unavoidable. BHC figures can overstate the living standards of someone whose entire income gain is absorbed by a rent increase.

The Effect of Taxes and Benefits on the Distribution

The distribution produced by the market alone is called original income — earnings, self-employment income, investment income and private pensions, before any government action. The state then modifies it in three stages: it adds cash benefits to give gross income, deducts direct taxes to give disposable income, and adds benefits in kind such as state education and NHS treatment to give final income. Each stage pulls the Lorenz curve closer to the line of perfect equality.

(2) Draw the 45° line from the origin to the top right corner and call it the line of perfect equality. (3) Make the Lorenz curve start at (0, 0) and finish at (100, 100) and bow below the diagonal — a curve above it is an automatic loss of marks. Poverty: Absolute and Relative

Absolute poverty exists when income is insufficient to afford the basic necessities of life — food, clean water, shelter, clothing and warmth. It is measured against a fixed standard that does not rise with average living standards. The World Bank uses an international line; the UK's official "absolute low income" measure uses 60% of median income in a fixed base year, uprated only for inflation.

Relative poverty exists when income is substantially below the typical income in that society, so a household cannot participate in normal life. The UK's official relative low-income threshold is 60% of contemporary median household income, equivalised for household size and published both before and after housing costs.

Because the relative line moves with the median, economic growth that lifts all incomes proportionately reduces absolute poverty but leaves relative poverty unchanged. Conversely, a recession that cuts median income can reduce measured relative poverty even though everybody is worse off — a genuine weakness of the measure that examiners reward candidates for spotting.

Equivalisation adjusts household income for the size and composition of the household before it is compared with the threshold. A single adult on £20,000 is better off than a couple with three children on the same £20,000, because the same money has to stretch further. Official UK statistics therefore convert every household's income into the equivalent income of a childless couple before ranking households or applying the 60% line. Without equivalisation, large families would appear far better off than they are and the measured poverty rate would be meaningless.

UK Data: Joseph Rowntree Foundation, UK Poverty 2026 (data for 2023/24)
The JRF found 14.2 million people — 21% of the population — in poverty, including 4.5 million children (about three in ten) and 7.9 million working-age adults; around three in twenty pensioners were in poverty. 6.8 million people were in "very deep" poverty, defined as an income of at most two-thirds of the poverty line — the highest number and share on record since 1994/95. Around 3.8 million people, including about one million children, experienced destitution in 2022. The JRF's findings show that the depth of poverty has worsened even while the headcount rate has been broadly flat, which the headline 60%-of-median measure alone would not reveal.

Causes of Poverty

  • Unemployment and worklessness — the loss of earned income is only partly replaced by benefits. The 58% relative poverty rate among children in workless families shows how strong this link is.
  • Low pay and underemployment — most people in poverty in the UK are in working households. Part-time hours, insecure contracts and low hourly wages can leave a working household below the threshold.
  • Lack of human capital — poor qualifications and skills restrict workers to occupations with elastic labour supply and low marginal revenue product, and therefore low wages.
  • Sickness, disability and caring responsibilities — these reduce the hours a household can work and raise its costs at the same time.
  • Household composition — lone-parent households and households with several children have more dependants per earner. Poverty rates rise sharply with the number of children.
  • High housing costs — the difference between the 33% BHC and 37% AHC Gini, and the higher AHC poverty rates, are driven by rent and mortgage interest absorbing a large share of low incomes.
  • Inadequate or poorly taken-up benefits — means-tested support that is set too low, or not claimed, leaves households below the line.
  • The cycle of poverty — low income leads to poorer health, housing and education, which lowers future earning power, transmitting poverty to the next generation.

Social Mobility and the Cycle of Poverty

Social mobility is the extent to which an individual's economic position differs from that of their parents. Intergenerational mobility is low when a child's adult income can be predicted accurately from their parents' income — the sign that inequality is being inherited rather than earned.

The cycle of poverty explains why low income persists across generations even when the labour market rewards skill:

  1. A low household income restricts spending on housing quality, nutrition, books, tutoring, travel and extra-curricular activities.
  2. The child's health and educational attainment suffer, so human capital accumulation is lower.
  3. Lower qualifications restrict the child to occupations with elastic labour supply and low marginal revenue product.
  4. The child therefore earns a low income as an adult, and the cycle repeats with their own children.

The cycle is the reason why education and early-years policy dominate discussions of long-run poverty reduction: cash transfers relieve poverty in the current period, but only human capital policies break the transmission mechanism. It also shows why the stock of wealth matters so much — an inherited deposit determines who can buy property, and the 45% relative poverty rate among children in families with three or more children shows how household composition compounds the effect.

The Poverty Trap and the Unemployment Trap

The poverty trap (the earnings trap) occurs when a low-income household that increases its gross earnings gains very little net income, because it simultaneously pays income tax and National Insurance contributions and loses means-tested benefits as its earnings rise. The household faces a very high effective marginal tax rate.

The unemployment trap occurs when the net income available from taking a job is little higher than — or lower than — the income available out of work from benefits, so there is no financial incentive to accept employment. It is measured by the replacement ratio.
Effective marginal tax rate = (income tax + NICs + benefits withdrawn) ÷ extra gross earnings
Replacement ratio = disposable income out of work ÷ disposable income in work

The higher the replacement ratio, the weaker the incentive to work. Both traps are examples of government failure: policies designed to relieve poverty can create disincentives that keep households poor. Universal Credit was introduced partly to address them by merging six separate means-tested benefits into one payment with a single withdrawal rate, so that the overlapping tapers of the old system — which could leave a household keeping only a few pence in the pound — could not stack on top of one another. The Universal Credit taper rate has been 55% since it was reduced from 63% in November 2021, meaning that above any applicable work allowance a household keeps 45p of each extra pound earned before income tax and NICs are also deducted.

Worked Example: Why the Effective Marginal Tax Rate Matters

Suppose a household in receipt of a means-tested benefit earns an extra £100 a week above its work allowance. It pays basic-rate income tax and National Insurance contributions on those earnings, and it also loses benefit at the taper rate. The three deductions stack:

Deduction from the extra £100Amount
Income tax at the basic rate£20
National Insurance contributions at the employee rate£8
Means-tested benefit withdrawn on the remaining £72 at a 55% taper£39.60
Total deducted£67.60
Net gain to the household£32.40

The effective marginal tax rate here is roughly 68% — higher than the marginal rate faced by an additional-rate taxpayer. The figures are illustrative, because tax rates and taper rates change, but the mechanism is the examinable point: the poverty trap is created by taxes and benefit withdrawal acting together, not by either alone. It also shows why a government that wants to strengthen work incentives can act on three separate levers — raise the personal allowance, cut the taper rate, or raise the work allowance at which withdrawal begins.

Means-Tested Versus Universal Benefits

Means-tested benefits are paid only to those whose income and savings fall below a defined level, and are withdrawn as income rises. Examples include Universal Credit and Pension Credit.

Universal benefits are paid to everyone in a defined category regardless of income. Examples include the state pension (contribution-based rather than income-based) and free state education and NHS treatment.

Government Policies to Influence the Distribution

Progressive, Proportional and Regressive Taxes

The classification depends on what happens to the average rate of tax (tax paid ÷ income) as income rises — not to the amount of tax paid.

Progressive: the average rate rises as income rises, so the rich pay a larger proportion of their income.
Proportional: the average rate is constant at all income levels.
Regressive: the average rate falls as income rises, so the poor pay a larger proportion of their income.
TypeAverage rate as income risesUK examplesEffect on inequality
ProgressiveRisesIncome tax with a personal allowance and rising marginal bands; inheritance tax; capital gains taxReduces inequality — pulls the Lorenz curve towards the 45° line
ProportionalConstantA flat-rate income tax; corporation tax approximates this on profitsLeaves relative shares unchanged, so the Lorenz curve does not move
RegressiveFallsVAT and excise duties on alcohol, tobacco and fuel; the TV licence; council tax in practiceIncreases inequality — a flat cash amount is a larger share of a small income

Indirect taxes are regressive because a fixed amount per unit, or a fixed percentage of the price, represents a larger share of a low income. Duty on a packet of cigarettes is the same whether the smoker earns £15,000 or £150,000. This is a standard evaluation point when discussing sin taxes or fuel duty.

The Tax and Benefit System as a Whole

  • Progressive direct taxation raises revenue disproportionately from high earners, compressing the top of the distribution.
  • Cash transfers — Universal Credit, Pension Credit, disability benefits, the state pension — raise the incomes of the bottom decile by far more, proportionately, than any tax change can.
  • Empirically, benefits do more redistribution than taxes: adding cash benefits to original income moves the Lorenz curve inwards more than deducting direct taxes does, because transfers are concentrated in the lowest deciles while income tax is levied across a wide range.
  • Benefits in kind — state schooling and NHS treatment — are worth roughly the same in cash terms to every household, so they are worth a much larger share of a low income. Their inclusion makes "final income" substantially more equal than disposable income.

The National Living Wage

A statutory wage floor raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers directly. From April 2026 the National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour for workers aged 21 and over. Because it operates in the labour market rather than through the benefit system, it reduces in-work poverty without creating a withdrawal taper — but it only helps those in work, and classical analysis predicts that a wage floor above the market-clearing wage creates excess supply of labour. In practice the employment effects of UK increases have been smaller than that theory predicts, which is usually explained by monopsony power in low-wage labour markets, by firms absorbing costs in lower profits, and by offsetting productivity gains.

Policy Context: The National Living Wage and the Cost of Living, 2026
The National Living Wage rose to £12.71 an hour in April 2026 for workers aged 21 and over. Its effect on real living standards depends on inflation: with CPI inflation of about 3% in August 2026, a nominal increase only raises real pay if it exceeds that rate. The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 17 September 2026. Interest rates matter for distribution too: higher rates raise the mortgage costs of indebted, typically younger households while raising the interest income of asset-rich savers — an example of monetary policy having distributional consequences that are not part of its remit.

State Provision of Education and Healthcare

  • Immediate effect: free provision is a benefit in kind that raises the real living standards of low-income households, who could not afford to buy the service privately.
  • Long-run effect: education raises human capital, productivity and future earnings, attacking a cause of inequality rather than treating the symptom. This is the main policy lever for improving social mobility and breaking the cycle of poverty.
  • Supply-side link: higher human capital shifts LRAS to the right, so the policy can improve equity and efficiency at the same time — which is why education spending is often described as escaping the equity–efficiency trade-off.
  • Limitations: the returns take a generation to appear; the quality of provision varies geographically; and better-off families may capture a disproportionate share of high-quality state schooling through house prices in desirable catchment areas.

Inheritance Tax and Wealth Taxes

Because wealth is far more unequally distributed than income (a Gini of 0.59 against 0.33), taxes on the stock rather than the flow have a larger potential effect on overall inequality.

  • Inheritance tax taxes the transfer of wealth at death, directly attacking the mechanism by which advantage passes between generations. In practice it raises a small share of total UK tax revenue and is widely avoided through lifetime gifts, trusts and reliefs, so its distributional impact is much smaller than its political prominence suggests.
  • Capital gains tax taxes the increase in an asset's value when it is sold, capturing some of the gains from rising property and share prices.
  • An annual net wealth tax would levy a percentage of total assets each year. Supporters argue it reaches wealth that escapes income tax entirely; critics point to severe valuation problems (private businesses, art, pension rights), liquidity problems for the asset-rich but cash-poor, high administrative costs, and the risk of capital flight and emigration reducing the tax base.
  • Council tax and stamp duty are existing UK property taxes, but council tax bands are based on 1991 valuations in England, which weakens its progressivity considerably.

Evaluation: The Equity–Efficiency Trade-Off

Equity means fairness, which is a normative concept, and must be distinguished from equality, which is a factual description of the distribution. Horizontal equity means treating people in identical circumstances identically; vertical equity means treating people in different circumstances differently, which is the justification for progressive taxation.

The central evaluative argument in this topic is that redistribution which improves equity may reduce economic efficiency. Arthur Okun described this as carrying money from rich to poor in a leaky bucket: some of the transfer is lost on the way through administrative costs and through the behaviour the transfer itself changes.

Incentive Effects and the Laffer Curve

High marginal tax rates may reduce the incentive to work, to acquire skills, to take entrepreneurial risk or to remain in the country. The Laffer curve formalises the argument: as the tax rate rises from zero, revenue at first rises, but beyond some rate t* the disincentive, avoidance and evasion effects dominate and revenue falls. At a 100% rate nobody has any reason to earn taxable income, so revenue returns to zero.

Figure 3: The Laffer curve. Revenue is zero at a tax rate of 0% and again at 100%, peaking at Rmax when the rate is t*. The diagram is qualitative — economists disagree strongly about where t* actually lies for any given tax.

The Laffer argument should be handled carefully in evaluation. It shows only that some rate maximises revenue, not that current rates are above it. If the economy is on the upward-sloping section, a tax rise still raises revenue and the disincentive argument is weaker than it is usually claimed to be.

The Argument That Some Inequality Is Necessary

  • Incentives: wage differentials are the signal that allocates labour to where it is most productive, and the reward for acquiring scarce skills. Removing them removes the incentive to train, to work harder and to move to where labour is needed.
  • Enterprise: the prospect of large profit compensates entrepreneurs for bearing risk. Taxing that reward heavily may reduce risk-taking, innovation and dynamic efficiency.
  • Trickle-down: the claim that gains at the top eventually raise incomes throughout the distribution through investment and job creation. The empirical evidence for this is contested, and it has weakened as a policy argument.
  • The counter-case: many economists argue that excessive inequality itself harms growth, by leaving human capital undeveloped among those who cannot afford education, by concentrating income among high savers and weakening aggregate demand, and by damaging social cohesion and trust.
Evaluation framework for any question on poverty, inequality or redistribution:
  1. Equity versus efficiency: does the policy improve fairness at the cost of output, or can it do both? Education and health spending raise human capital and LRAS, so they may escape the trade-off; a high top rate of income tax probably does not.
  2. Incentive effects: what happens to the effective marginal tax rate and the replacement ratio? Does the policy create or relieve the poverty trap and the unemployment trap?
  3. The Laffer argument: would a higher rate actually raise revenue, or is the economy already near t*? Be sceptical of the claim in both directions.
  4. Targeting: means-testing targets scarce resources but reduces take-up and creates tapers; universal provision achieves full take-up but is expensive and poorly targeted. Which failure matters more here?
  5. Poverty or inequality? Identify which the question is actually about. A policy can cut poverty without touching inequality (raising the bottom) or cut inequality without touching poverty (capping the top).
  6. Absolute or relative? Growth can eliminate absolute poverty but never eliminates relative poverty, which is defined against a moving median.
  7. Stock or flow? Taxes on income do nothing about the far greater inequality in wealth. Does the policy address the stock, and if so, can wealth be valued and can it flee?
  8. Government failure: administrative costs, the leaky bucket, information failure about who is genuinely in need, benefit fraud and error, and unintended behavioural responses.
  9. Time lags: benefits and the minimum wage change incomes within months; education and health policy change the distribution only over a generation.
  10. Measurement caveats: is the evidence BHC or AHC? Does the Gini conceal where the change occurred? Do household surveys under-record incomes at the very top?
Exam Tip — 25-Mark Essays: Essays on this topic usually take the form "Evaluate the view that the government should do more to reduce inequality in the UK." A strong structure is: define income and wealth and the measure you are using; give one piece of dated UK evidence (the 33% BHC Gini in 2024/25, the 0.59 wealth Gini in 2020–22, or the 21% JRF poverty rate for 2023/24); analyse two policies in depth with a Lorenz curve diagram; evaluate each using the equity–efficiency trade-off, incentive effects and government failure; then reach a supported judgement that distinguishes short-run from long-run effects and says which policy you would prioritise and why. Do not simply list every policy you know.
