1.5 Coordinate Geometry

Mathematics B (MEI) — OCR B — A-Level (H640) — Pure Mathematics — spec refs c1–c3

What this topic covers. Straight lines and their gradients, the equation of a circle together with the circle properties MEI expects you to use, and parametric equations — including converting a parametric description into Cartesian form.

1. Straight lines

QuantityFormula
Gradient through \((x_1,y_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2)\)\(m = \dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\)
Midpoint\(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\)
Distance\(\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}\)
Point-gradient form\(y - y_1 = m(x-x_1)\)
Worked example. Find the gradient, midpoint and distance for \(A(1,2)\) and \(B(3,8)\). $$m = \frac{8-2}{3-1} = 3, \qquad \text{midpoint } (2,5), \qquad |AB| = \sqrt{2^2+6^2} = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$$

Parallel and perpendicular

  • Parallel lines have equal gradients.
  • Perpendicular lines have gradients whose product is \(-1\): if one has gradient \(m\), the other has \(-\dfrac1m\).
Worked example. Find the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining \((1,2)\) and \((5,6)\).

Midpoint \((3,4)\). Gradient of the segment \(= \dfrac{6-2}{5-1} = 1\), so the perpendicular gradient is \(-1\).

$$y - 4 = -1(x-3) \Longrightarrow y = -x + 7$$
Vertical and horizontal lines are the exceptions: \(y=5\) has gradient 0, and \(x=3\) has undefined gradient. The product rule for perpendicularity does not apply to this pair — they are perpendicular but you cannot multiply their gradients.

Collinearity

Three points are collinear if the gradient between successive pairs is the same.

2. The circle

The circle with centre \((a,b)\) and radius \(r\) has equation $$(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$$

Note the signs: centre \((2,-3)\) gives \((x-2)^2 + (y+3)^2 = r^2\).

The expanded form

Circles often appear expanded, as \(x^2+y^2+2gx+2fy+c=0\). Recover the centre and radius by completing the square in both variables.

Worked example. Find the centre and radius of \(x^2+y^2-6x+4y-12=0\). $$(x^2-6x) + (y^2+4y) = 12$$ $$(x-3)^2 - 9 + (y+2)^2 - 4 = 12$$ $$(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 25$$

Centre \((3,-2)\), radius 5.

Worked example. Find the centre and radius of \(x^2+y^2+2x-6y+1=0\). $$(x+1)^2 - 1 + (y-3)^2 - 9 + 1 = 0 \Longrightarrow (x+1)^2+(y-3)^2 = 9$$

Centre \((-1,3)\), radius 3.

The right-hand side is \(r^2\), not \(r\). Reading a radius of 25 off \((x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=25\) instead of 5 is one of the most frequent slips in this topic.

Circle from a diameter

Worked example. A circle has diameter from \((1,2)\) to \((7,10)\). Find its equation.

The centre is the midpoint, \((4,6)\). The radius is half the diameter: \(|AB| = \sqrt{6^2+8^2} = 10\), so \(r=5\).

$$(x-4)^2+(y-6)^2 = 25$$

Circle properties

PropertyUse
The angle in a semicircle is \(90^\circ\)Recognising a right angle when a diameter is involved
The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects itFinding chord lengths and centres
A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contactFinding tangent equations
Worked example. Find the tangent to \(x^2+y^2=25\) at \((3,4)\).

The radius to \((3,4)\) has gradient \(\tfrac43\), so the tangent has gradient \(-\tfrac34\):

$$y - 4 = -\tfrac34(x-3) \Longrightarrow 3x + 4y = 25$$

Line and circle: how many intersections?

Substitute the line into the circle and look at the discriminant of the resulting quadratic — two roots means two intersections, one means tangency, none means the line misses.

Worked example. For what \(c\) is \(y=x+c\) tangent to \(x^2+y^2=8\)?

The perpendicular distance from the centre \((0,0)\) to the line \(x-y+c=0\) must equal the radius \(2\sqrt2\):

$$\frac{|c|}{\sqrt{1^2+(-1)^2}} = 2\sqrt2 \Longrightarrow \frac{|c|}{\sqrt2} = 2\sqrt2 \Longrightarrow |c| = 4$$

So \(c = \pm 4\).

Worked example. A point \((5,5)\) and the circle \(x^2+y^2=25\): inside, on, or outside?

Compare \(5^2+5^2 = 50\) with \(r^2 = 25\). Since \(50 > 25\), the point lies outside.

3. Parametric equations

In a parametric description, \(x\) and \(y\) are each given as functions of a third variable, the parameter (often \(t\) or \(\theta\)). Each value of the parameter gives one point on the curve.

Parametric form handles curves that are awkward or impossible to write as \(y=f(x)\) — for instance a full circle, which fails the one-output-per-input test.

Converting to Cartesian form

Eliminate the parameter. For polynomial parameters, make \(t\) the subject of the simpler equation and substitute. For trigonometric parameters, use \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta = 1\).

Worked example. \(x = 2t\), \(y = t^2-1\).

From the first, \(t = \tfrac{x}{2}\). Substituting:

$$y = \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^2 - 1 = \frac{x^2}{4} - 1$$

a parabola.

Worked example. \(x = \cos\theta\), \(y = \sin\theta\). $$x^2+y^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$$

the unit circle.

Worked example. \(x = 2 + 3\cos\theta\), \(y = -1 + 3\sin\theta\).

Rearrange to isolate the trigonometric functions:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{x-2}{3}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{y+1}{3}$$

Then apply the identity:

$$\left(\frac{x-2}{3}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y+1}{3}\right)^2 = 1 \Longrightarrow (x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 9$$

a circle, centre \((2,-1)\), radius 3.

Worked example. \(x = 3\cos t\), \(y = 2\sin t\). $$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$$

an ellipse.

Worked example. \(x = t^2\), \(y = 2t\).

From the second, \(t = \tfrac{y}{2}\), so \(x = \tfrac{y^2}{4}\), i.e. \(y^2 = 4x\) — a parabola opening to the right.

Choose the equation that is easier to rearrange for the parameter — usually the linear one. Making \(t\) the subject of \(y=t^2-1\) forces a \(\pm\) square root and needless case-splitting.

Gradient from parametric equations

Using the chain rule,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t}$$
Worked example. For \(x = 2t\), \(y = t^2\), find \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\). $$\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{2t}{2} = t$$

4. Perpendicular distance from a point to a line

The distance from \((x_0,y_0)\) to the line \(ax+by+c=0\) is

$$d = \frac{|ax_0+by_0+c|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}$$
Worked example. Find the distance from \((1,1)\) to \(3x+4y-10=0\). $$d = \frac{|3+4-10|}{\sqrt{9+16}} = \frac{3}{5}$$
Rearrange the line into the form \(ax+by+c=0\) before using this — applying it to \(y = mx+c\) directly gives nonsense.

5. Common pitfalls

  • Reading the radius as \(r^2\) instead of \(r\) from the circle equation.
  • Getting the sign of the centre wrong: \((x+1)^2\) means the centre has \(x=-1\).
  • Forgetting the \(-9\) and \(-4\) correction terms when completing the square in both variables.
  • Using the gradient product rule on a vertical line, whose gradient is undefined.
  • Eliminating the parameter from the harder equation and introducing a spurious \(\pm\).
  • Writing \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}t}\) — the fraction is the other way up.
  • Using the perpendicular-distance formula without first rearranging the line to \(ax+by+c=0\).
