3.3 Kinematics in 2 Dimensions

Mathematics B (MEI) — OCR B — A-Level (H640) — Mechanics — spec refs k9–k11

What this topic covers. Extending kinematics to two dimensions using vectors — position, velocity and acceleration as vector functions of time, the vector forms of the constant acceleration formulae, relative position and velocity, and finding the Cartesian equation of a path.

1. Vector kinematics

In two dimensions each quantity becomes a vector with \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) components (spec ref k9).

QuantitySymbolRelationship
Position\(\mathbf{r}\)Measured from a fixed origin
Velocity\(\mathbf{v}\)\(\mathbf{v} = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t}\)
Acceleration\(\mathbf{a}\)\(\mathbf{a} = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}\)

Going the other way, \(\mathbf{v} = \int \mathbf{a}\,\mathrm{d}t\) and \(\mathbf{r} = \int \mathbf{v}\,\mathrm{d}t\), each with a vector constant of integration fixed by the initial conditions.

Differentiation and integration act component by component, which is what makes two-dimensional motion tractable: the \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) directions behave independently.

Worked example. A particle has \(\mathbf{r} = 3t\,\mathbf{i} + t^2\,\mathbf{j}\). Find its velocity, speed at \(t=2\), and acceleration. $$\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{i} + 2t\,\mathbf{j}$$

At \(t=2\), \(\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\), so the speed is \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{9+16} = 5\).

$$\mathbf{a} = 2\mathbf{j}$$
Worked example. A particle has \(\mathbf{a} = 2\mathbf{i} + 6t\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i}\) at \(t=0\). Find \(\mathbf{v}\) at time \(t\). $$\mathbf{v} = \int (2\mathbf{i}+6t\,\mathbf{j})\,\mathrm{d}t = 2t\,\mathbf{i} + 3t^2\,\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{c}$$

At \(t=0\), \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{i}\), so \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{i}\) and

$$\mathbf{v} = (1+2t)\mathbf{i} + 3t^2\,\mathbf{j}$$
Speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector, so it is a scalar: \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2}\). Quoting a "speed" as a vector loses the mark.

2. Distance and displacement in two dimensions

  • Distance from the origin at time \(t\) is \(|\mathbf{r}|\).
  • Displacement between two times is the difference of the position vectors.
  • Distance travelled is the length of the path, generally greater than the magnitude of the displacement.
Worked example. For \(\mathbf{r} = 2t\,\mathbf{i} + t^2\,\mathbf{j}\), find the average velocity between \(t=0\) and \(t=2\).

Displacement \(= (4\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}) - \mathbf{0} = 4\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\), over 2 seconds:

$$\text{average velocity} = 2\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$$

3. Constant acceleration in vector form

The suvat formulae carry over directly, with vectors in place of scalars (k10):

$$\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{a}t$$ $$\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{u}t + \tfrac12\mathbf{a}t^2$$ $$\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{v}t - \tfrac12\mathbf{a}t^2$$ $$\mathbf{s} = \tfrac12(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})t$$
Worked example. A particle has \(\mathbf{u} = 2\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{j}\). Find its displacement after 4 s. $$\mathbf{s} = 2\mathbf{i}(4) + \tfrac12\mathbf{j}(16) = 8\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}$$
Worked example. A particle starts at \(2\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\) with constant velocity \(3\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}\). Find its position at \(t=4\). $$\mathbf{r} = (2\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}) + 4(3\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}) = 14\mathbf{i} - 3\mathbf{j}$$
Because \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{a}t\), the change in velocity is always parallel to the acceleration. With zero acceleration the velocity is constant and the path is a straight line.

4. Relative position and relative velocity

$$\text{position of } B \text{ relative to } A = \mathbf{r}_B - \mathbf{r}_A$$ $$\text{velocity of } A \text{ relative to } B = \mathbf{v}_A - \mathbf{v}_B$$
Worked example. \(A\) is at \(3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\) and \(B\) at \(7\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\).

Position of \(B\) relative to \(A\) is \(4\mathbf{i}-3\mathbf{j}\), and the distance between them is \(\sqrt{16+9} = 5\).

Worked example. \(A\) has velocity \(5\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\) and \(B\) has \(3\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}\). The velocity of \(A\) relative to \(B\) is $$(5-3)\mathbf{i} + (2-(-1))\mathbf{j} = 2\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}$$

Collisions

Two particles collide when their position vectors are equal at the same instant — that is, both components agree for a single common value of \(t\).
Worked example. \(A\) has \(\mathbf{r}_A = (t+1)\mathbf{i}+2t\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(B\) has \(\mathbf{r}_B = (3t-1)\mathbf{i}+(t+1)\mathbf{j}\). Do they collide?

Equating \(\mathbf{i}\) components: \(t+1 = 3t-1\), so \(t=1\).

Equating \(\mathbf{j}\) components: \(2t = t+1\), so \(t=1\).

Both give the same time, so they collide at \(t=1\), at the point \(2\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\).

You must check both components and confirm they give the same \(t\). If the two components give different times, the paths cross but the particles are never there together — no collision.

The distance between two particles is least when the magnitude of the relative position vector is minimised, which is found by writing that magnitude as a function of \(t\) and minimising it.

5. The Cartesian equation of the path

Eliminate \(t\) between the component equations (k11).

Worked example. \(\mathbf{r} = 3t\,\mathbf{i}+t^2\,\mathbf{j}\).

Here \(x = 3t\) and \(y = t^2\), so \(t = \tfrac{x}{3}\) and

$$y = \frac{x^2}{9}$$
Worked example. \(\mathbf{r} = 4t\,\mathbf{i} + (3t-5t^2)\mathbf{j}\).

\(x = 4t\) gives \(t = \tfrac{x}{4}\), so

$$y = \frac{3x}{4} - \frac{5x^2}{16}$$

a parabola — the signature of motion under constant acceleration perpendicular to the initial velocity.

Worked example. For \(\mathbf{r} = (t-2)\mathbf{i}+(t^2-4)\mathbf{j}\), when does the particle pass through the origin?

The \(\mathbf{i}\) component vanishes at \(t=2\); the \(\mathbf{j}\) component vanishes at \(t = \pm 2\). Both are zero at \(t=2\), so it passes through the origin then.

6. Common pitfalls

  • Reporting a speed as a vector rather than its magnitude.
  • Omitting the vector constant of integration.
  • Subtracting relative position or velocity the wrong way round.
  • Concluding a collision from the \(\mathbf{i}\) components alone.
  • Confusing distance from the origin with distance travelled.
  • Applying constant-acceleration formulae when the acceleration is a function of \(t\).
  • Eliminating \(t\) from the harder component and introducing a spurious square root.
