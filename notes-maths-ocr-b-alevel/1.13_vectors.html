1.13 Vectors

Mathematics B (MEI) — OCR B — A-Level (H640) — Pure Mathematics — spec refs v1–v7

What this topic covers. Vectors in two and three dimensions — notation, addition and scalar multiplication, magnitude and direction, position vectors and distances — and their use in geometric problems and in resolving forces.
Not in this specification: the scalar (dot) product, and the vector equation of a line. Both appear in other A-Level Mathematics specifications but are not part of H640, so questions here will not require them. Angles between vectors are handled by trigonometry, not by a dot product.

1. Notation and language

TermMeaning
ScalarA quantity with magnitude only — mass, time, speed.
VectorA quantity with magnitude and direction — displacement, velocity, force.
Modulus / magnitudeThe size of a vector, written \(|\mathbf{a}|\) or \(a\).
Unit vectorA vector of magnitude 1.
Position vectorThe vector from the origin to a point.
Equal vectorsSame magnitude and same direction — position is irrelevant.
Parallel vectorsOne is a scalar multiple of the other.
Collinear pointsPoints lying on the same straight line.

Vectors are printed in bold and handwritten underlined. In component form, in two dimensions:

$$\mathbf{a} = a_1\mathbf{i} + a_2\mathbf{j} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2\end{pmatrix}$$

and in three dimensions the unit vector \(\mathbf{k}\) is added along the \(z\)-axis (spec ref v7):

$$\mathbf{a} = a_1\mathbf{i} + a_2\mathbf{j} + a_3\mathbf{k}$$

2. Arithmetic with vectors

Addition and subtraction work component by component, and a scalar multiple scales the magnitude while preserving direction (reversing it if the scalar is negative).

Worked example. With \(\mathbf{a} = 4\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}\): $$\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b} = 5\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}, \qquad \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} = 3\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}, \qquad 3\mathbf{a} = 12\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}$$

Geometrically, addition is the triangle (or parallelogram) rule: place the vectors nose to tail, and the resultant runs from the start of the first to the end of the last.

3. Magnitude and direction

$$|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2} \qquad \text{(2D)}, \qquad |\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2} \qquad \text{(3D)}$$
Worked example. \(|3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}| = \sqrt{9+16} = 5\); \(\ |2\mathbf{i}-3\mathbf{j}+6\mathbf{k}| = \sqrt{4+9+36} = 7\).

Unit vectors

To find a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{a}\), divide by its magnitude:

$$\hat{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|}$$
Worked example. A unit vector in the direction of \(6\mathbf{i}-8\mathbf{j}\): the magnitude is 10, so \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} = 0.6\mathbf{i}-0.8\mathbf{j}\).

Magnitude-direction form

In two dimensions a vector may be given by its magnitude \(r\) and the angle \(\theta\) it makes with the positive \(x\)-axis (v3):

$$a_1 = r\cos\theta, \qquad a_2 = r\sin\theta, \qquad r = \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{a_2}{a_1}$$
Worked example. Express \(3\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}\) in magnitude-direction form.

\(r = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2\) and \(\tan\theta = 1\), so \(\theta = 45^\circ\).

\(\arctan\) always returns an angle in the first or fourth quadrant. For a vector such as \((-3,-4)\), which points into the third quadrant, the calculator gives \(53.1^\circ\) and you must add \(180^\circ\) to get \(233.1^\circ\). Always sketch the vector before quoting a direction.

4. Position vectors and distance

If \(A\) and \(B\) have position vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), then

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$$

and the distance between them is \(|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}|\) (v4, v5).

Worked example. \(A(2,1)\) and \(B(6,4)\). Then \(\overrightarrow{AB} = 4\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}\) and \(|AB| = \sqrt{16+9} = 5\).
Worked example. \(A(2,-1,4)\) and \(B(5,3,0)\). Then \(\overrightarrow{AB} = 3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}-4\mathbf{k}\), so \(|AB| = \sqrt{9+16+16} = \sqrt{41}\).
The order matters: \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\), "destination minus start". Reversing it gives a vector of the same magnitude in the opposite direction — harmless for a distance, fatal for a direction.

Midpoints and division of a line

The midpoint of \(AB\) has position vector \(\tfrac12(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\). More generally, the point dividing \(AB\) in the ratio \(m:n\) has position vector

$$\frac{n\mathbf{a}+m\mathbf{b}}{m+n}$$
Worked example. \(P\) divides \(AB\) in the ratio \(2:1\). Then \(\overrightarrow{OP} = \mathbf{a} + \tfrac23(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}) = \tfrac{\mathbf{a}+2\mathbf{b}}{3}\).

5. Geometric problems

Proving points collinear

Worked example. \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) have position vectors \(\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\), \(3\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j}\), \(5\mathbf{i}+9\mathbf{j}\). Show they are collinear. $$\overrightarrow{AB} = 2\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}, \qquad \overrightarrow{BC} = 2\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}$$

\(\overrightarrow{BC}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) (here equal to it), so the two are parallel; since they share the point \(B\), the three points are collinear.

Parallel alone is not enough — two parallel vectors can lie on different lines. You must also state the common point. That sentence is what completes the proof.

Parallel vectors in three dimensions

Worked example. Find \(\lambda\) so that \(2\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}+\lambda\mathbf{k}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}+3\mathbf{k}\).

The first must be a scalar multiple of the second. Comparing \(\mathbf{i}\) components gives the factor 2, so \(\lambda = 2\times 3 = 6\).

Shapes

In a parallelogram \(OACB\) with \(\overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf{a}\) and \(\overrightarrow{OB}=\mathbf{b}\), the diagonal is \(\overrightarrow{OC} = \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\). The centroid of a triangle with vertices \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\), \(\mathbf{c}\) has position vector \(\tfrac13(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c})\).

6. Vectors and forces

The specification explicitly includes using vectors in context, "including problems involving forces", and interpreting the sum of force vectors as the resultant force (v6). This is the bridge into Mechanics.

Worked example. Forces \(3\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}\) and \(-\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}\) act on a particle. Find the resultant. $$(3-1)\mathbf{i} + (2+4)\mathbf{j} = 2\mathbf{i}+6\mathbf{j}$$
Worked example. Forces \(\mathbf{F}_1 = 2\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}\), \(\mathbf{F}_2 = -5\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_3\) hold a particle in equilibrium. Find \(\mathbf{F}_3\).

Equilibrium means the vector sum is zero, so

$$\mathbf{F}_3 = -(\mathbf{F}_1+\mathbf{F}_2) = -(-3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}) = 3\mathbf{i}-4\mathbf{j}$$

7. A useful inequality

For any two vectors, the magnitude of the sum depends on their directions:

$$\big||\mathbf{a}|-|\mathbf{b}|\big| \;\le\; |\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}| \;\le\; |\mathbf{a}|+|\mathbf{b}|$$

So if \(|\mathbf{a}|=5\) and \(|\mathbf{b}|=12\), then \(|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}|\) lies between 7 and 17 — reaching 17 only when they point the same way, and 7 only when they oppose.

Magnitudes do not simply add: \(|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}| \neq |\mathbf{a}|+|\mathbf{b}|\) in general. Adding the magnitudes is one of the most frequent vector errors.

8. Common pitfalls

  • Writing \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\) instead of \(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\).
  • Adding magnitudes instead of adding vectors and then taking the magnitude.
  • Quoting an \(\arctan\) angle without checking the quadrant.
  • Proving two vectors parallel and stopping, without stating the common point, in a collinearity proof.
  • Forgetting the third component when computing a magnitude in three dimensions.
  • Treating a scalar multiple as changing direction when the scalar is positive — it does not.
  • Reaching for a dot product, which is not part of this specification.
