1.11 Differential Equations

Mathematics B (MEI) — OCR B — A-Level (H640) — Pure Mathematics — spec refs c36–c39 (Calculus)

What this topic covers. Forming differential equations from a described rate of change, solving them by separating the variables, applying initial conditions, and interpreting and criticising the resulting models.

1. What a differential equation is

A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives. A first order equation involves only the first derivative.

Solving one produces a general solution containing an arbitrary constant. Applying an initial or boundary condition pins that constant down and gives the particular solution.

2. Forming a differential equation

Translate the wording of the rate directly.

StatementEquation
The rate of growth is proportional to the amount present\(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = kP\)
The rate of decay is proportional to the amount present\(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = -kN\)
The rate is proportional to the square of the population\(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = kP^2\)
The rate of cooling is proportional to the excess temperature\(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k(\theta-\theta_0)\)
The quantity approaches a limit \(A\) at a rate proportional to the shortfall\(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = k(A-y)\)
"Proportional to" introduces a constant \(k\); a decreasing quantity takes a minus sign, with \(k\) itself kept positive. Writing \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = kN\) for decay and then finding \(k\) negative is acceptable but muddles later interpretation.

3. Separating the variables

If the equation can be written as \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = f(x)\,g(y)\), gather each variable on its own side and integrate:

$$\int \frac{1}{g(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y = \int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$
Worked example. Solve \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = xy\), given \(y=1\) when \(x=0\). $$\int \frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y = \int x\,\mathrm{d}x \Longrightarrow \ln y = \frac{x^2}{2}+c$$

At \(x=0,\ y=1\): \(\ln 1 = 0\), so \(c=0\). Hence

$$y = \mathrm{e}^{x^2/2}$$
Worked example. Solve \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y}{x}\) for \(x>0\). $$\int\frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y = \int\frac{1}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x \Longrightarrow \ln y = \ln x + c$$

Writing \(c = \ln A\) gives \(\ln y = \ln(Ax)\), so \(y = Ax\).

Replacing the constant by \(\ln A\) turns an additive constant into a multiplicative one and keeps the final answer tidy. Similarly, \(y = \mathrm{e}^{x+c}\) is better written \(y = A\mathrm{e}^{x}\) with \(A = \mathrm{e}^c\).
Worked example. Solve \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = 2xy^2\). $$\int y^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}y = \int 2x\,\mathrm{d}x \Longrightarrow -\frac{1}{y} = x^2 + c$$
Worked example. Solve \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = y\tan x\). $$\int\frac{1}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y = \int \tan x\,\mathrm{d}x \Longrightarrow \ln y = \ln|\sec x| + c \Longrightarrow y = A\sec x$$

4. The exponential model

The single most important case:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = kP \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = P_0\mathrm{e}^{kt}$$

with \(P_0\) the value at \(t=0\). Decay is the same with \(-k\).

Worked example. Solve \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = 3y\) with \(y=5\) at \(t=0\).

\(y = A\mathrm{e}^{3t}\), and \(y(0)=5\) gives \(A=5\), so \(y = 5\mathrm{e}^{3t}\).

5. Newton's law of cooling

Worked example. A body cools according to \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k(\theta-20)\), with \(\theta = 100\) at \(t=0\). Solve, and state the long-term temperature. $$\int\frac{1}{\theta-20}\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \int -k\,\mathrm{d}t \Longrightarrow \ln(\theta-20) = -kt + c$$ $$\theta - 20 = A\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$$

At \(t=0\), \(\theta=100\), so \(A = 80\):

$$\theta = 20 + 80\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$$

As \(t\to\infty\) the exponential tends to zero, so \(\theta \to 20^\circ\mathrm{C}\) — the surrounding temperature, as physical sense demands.

Checking the long-term behaviour is a free sanity check on your answer. A cooling model that tends to anything other than the ambient temperature contains an algebraic error.

6. Approach to a limit

The equation \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = k(A-y)\) describes a quantity rising towards a ceiling \(A\), with the rate falling as the gap closes. Its solution has the form \(y = A - B\mathrm{e}^{-kt}\), which approaches but never reaches \(A\).

The same structure appears in the mechanics of a falling body with resistance, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = g - kv\), whose limit \(v = \dfrac{g}{k}\) is the terminal velocity — the value at which the acceleration becomes zero.

7. The logistic model

Unlimited exponential growth is unrealistic for a population with finite resources. The logistic equation builds in a ceiling:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = ky(1-y)$$

Separating requires partial fractions, since

$$\frac{1}{y(1-y)} = \frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{1-y}$$
Recognising that a logistic-type equation needs partial fractions before it can be integrated is the step that unlocks the whole question. If separation leaves you with a product in the denominator, split it first.

8. Criticising a model

MEI asks for evaluation, not just solution. Points that reliably earn credit:
  • Exponential growth predicts unbounded increase, ignoring limited food, space or resources.
  • A decay model tending to zero may be unrealistic if a residual background level remains.
  • The constant \(k\) is assumed fixed, but may drift with temperature, season or other conditions.
  • Populations are discrete, whereas the model treats them as continuous.
  • Extrapolating far beyond the range of the data that fitted the model is not justified.

9. Common pitfalls

  • Forgetting the constant of integration, so no initial condition can be applied.
  • Applying the initial condition before integrating.
  • Leaving the answer as \(\ln y = \dots\) when the question asks for \(y\).
  • Sign errors on a decay constant, giving growth instead of decay.
  • Attempting to separate an equation that is not separable.
  • Missing that a product in the denominator needs partial fractions.
  • Not stating the long-term behaviour when the question asks for it.
