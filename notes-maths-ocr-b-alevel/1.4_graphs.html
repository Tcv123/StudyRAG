1.4 Graphs

Mathematics B (MEI) — OCR B — A-Level (H640) — Pure Mathematics — spec refs g1–g2

What this topic covers. Sketching standard curves and identifying their key features — intercepts, turning points, asymptotes — and the four graph transformations, including the vector notation MEI uses for translations.

1. What a sketch must show

A sketch is not a plot. It need not be to scale, but it must be correct in every labelled feature. Examiners look for:

  • Intercepts — set \(x=0\) for the \(y\)-intercept, \(y=0\) for the \(x\)-intercepts.
  • Asymptotes — drawn as dashed lines and labelled with their equations.
  • Turning points — where relevant.
  • Behaviour at the extremes — what happens as \(x \to \pm\infty\).
  • Correct shape at repeated roots.

2. Standard curves

CurveShape and key features
\(y = x^2\)Parabola, minimum at the origin, symmetric about the \(y\)-axis.
\(y = x^3\)Increasing everywhere, point of inflection at the origin, symmetric about the origin.
\(y = x^4\)Like a parabola but flatter near the origin; minimum at the origin.
\(y = \dfrac1x\)Hyperbola in two opposite quadrants; asymptotes \(x=0\) and \(y=0\).
\(y = \dfrac{1}{x^2}\)Always positive; asymptotes \(x=0\) and \(y=0\).
\(y = \sqrt{x}\)Starts at the origin, defined only for \(x \ge 0\), increasing and flattening.
\(y = \mathrm{e}^x\)Always positive, through \((0,1)\), asymptote \(y=0\) to the left.
\(y = \ln x\)Defined for \(x>0\), through \((1,0)\), asymptote \(x=0\).
\(y = |x|\)V-shape with vertex at the origin.

3. Polynomial curves and repeated roots

Factorised form tells you almost everything. A polynomial of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) real roots and at most \(n-1\) turning points.

FactorBehaviour at that root
\((x-a)\)Crosses the axis straight through.
\((x-a)^2\)Touches the axis and turns back — a minimum or maximum sitting on the axis.
\((x-a)^3\)Crosses, but flattens at the crossing (point of inflection).
Worked example. Sketch \(y = x^2(x-2)\).

Roots at \(x=0\) (repeated, so the curve touches) and \(x=2\) (single, so it crosses). It is a cubic with positive leading coefficient, so \(y \to -\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\) and \(y \to +\infty\) as \(x\to+\infty\). The curve comes up from the bottom left, touches the axis at the origin, dips, then crosses at \(x=2\).

Worked example. Find where \(y=(x+1)(x-2)(x-4)\) crosses the axes.

\(x\)-intercepts at \(x=-1,\,2,\,4\). For the \(y\)-intercept set \(x=0\): \(y = (1)(-2)(-4) = 8\), so \((0,8)\).

4. Asymptotes

An asymptote is a line the curve approaches arbitrarily closely without meeting (at least in the limit).
  • Vertical: where the denominator is zero and the numerator is not. For \(y = \dfrac{1}{x-3}\), the asymptote is \(x=3\).
  • Horizontal: found from the behaviour as \(x \to \pm\infty\). For \(y = \dfrac{x}{x-1}\), dividing top and bottom by \(x\) gives \(\dfrac{1}{1-1/x} \to 1\), so \(y=1\).
  • Oblique (slant): arises when the numerator's degree is one more than the denominator's. For \(y = \dfrac{x^2+1}{x} = x + \dfrac1x\), the term \(\tfrac1x \to 0\), so the curve approaches \(y=x\).
Worked example. State the asymptotes of \(y = \dfrac{2}{x+1} - 3\).

Vertical: \(x = -1\). As \(x \to \pm\infty\), the fraction tends to 0, so the horizontal asymptote is \(y = -3\).

A curve may cross a horizontal or oblique asymptote — the asymptote only describes behaviour far out. It can never cross a vertical asymptote, because the function is undefined there.
Watch for a cancelling factor. In \(y = \dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1} = x+1\) there is no asymptote at \(x=1\) — instead there is a hole in the line, because the function is undefined at that single point.

5. Transformations

Four transformations, and the crucial split between those acting on the output (outside the function) and those acting on the input (inside).

TransformationEffect on \(y=f(x)\)Acts on
\(y = f(x) + a\)Translation \(a\) units upOutput
\(y = f(x + a)\)Translation \(a\) units leftInput
\(y = af(x)\)Stretch, factor \(a\), parallel to the \(y\)-axisOutput
\(y = f(ax)\)Stretch, factor \(\tfrac1a\), parallel to the \(x\)-axisInput
\(y = -f(x)\)Reflection in the \(x\)-axisOutput
\(y = f(-x)\)Reflection in the \(y\)-axisInput
Everything inside the bracket behaves in the way you do not expect: \(f(x+2)\) moves the graph left, and \(f(2x)\) halves the width. Everything outside behaves as expected. This single rule resolves most transformation errors.

Vector notation for translations

MEI writes a translation as a column vector. The graph \(y = f(x-2) + 5\) is the graph of \(y=f(x)\) translated by

$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$$

that is, 2 units in the positive \(x\)-direction and 5 in the positive \(y\)-direction. Note the sign: \(f(x-2)\) shifts right by 2.

Tracking a point

Worked example. \(y=f(x)\) has a maximum at \((2,5)\). Find the maximum of \(y = 3f(x+1) - 2\).

Work through the transformations in order:

  • \(f(x+1)\): translate 1 left, so \(x\) becomes \(2-1 = 1\).
  • \(3f(\cdot)\): stretch factor 3 vertically, so \(y\) becomes \(5 \times 3 = 15\).
  • \(-2\): translate 2 down, so \(y\) becomes \(13\).

The maximum is at \((1, 13)\).

Order of combined transformations

Worked example. Describe \(y = 2f(3x-6)\) as a sequence of transformations.

First rewrite the inside in factorised form: \(3x-6 = 3(x-2)\), so \(y = 2f\big(3(x-2)\big)\).

  • Translate 2 units in the positive \(x\)-direction.
  • Stretch parallel to the \(x\)-axis, factor \(\tfrac13\).
  • Stretch parallel to the \(y\)-axis, factor 2.
Always factorise the inside first. Reading \(f(3x-6)\) as "translate 6 then stretch \(\tfrac13\)" gives the wrong graph, because the stretch would also scale the translation. Writing \(3(x-2)\) makes the correct order unambiguous.

6. Intersections and roots

The number of real solutions of \(f(x) = g(x)\) is the number of intersections of \(y=f(x)\) and \(y=g(x)\). Sketching both curves often answers "how many solutions" far faster than algebra.

Worked example. How many real solutions does \(x^3-3x = k\) have when \(-2 < k < 2\)?

The curve \(y=x^3-3x\) has turning points where \(3x^2-3=0\), i.e. \(x=\pm1\), giving a local maximum at \((-1,2)\) and a local minimum at \((1,-2)\). A horizontal line \(y=k\) strictly between \(-2\) and \(2\) cuts the curve three times.

Worked example. How many times do \(y=\mathrm{e}^x\) and \(y=x+1\) meet?

Exactly once, at \(x=0\). The line is the tangent to the exponential curve at that point, and \(\mathrm{e}^x \ge x+1\) everywhere.

8. Common pitfalls

  • Translating the wrong way for \(f(x+a)\) — it moves left, not right.
  • Using stretch factor \(a\) instead of \(\tfrac1a\) for \(f(ax)\).
  • Failing to factorise the inside before describing a combined transformation, so the order comes out wrong.
  • Drawing a curve crossing a vertical asymptote.
  • Missing that a repeated factor makes the curve touch rather than cross the axis.
  • Missing a cancelling factor and inventing an asymptote where there is only a hole.
  • Leaving asymptotes unlabelled, or omitting intercepts, on a sketch that asks for key features.
