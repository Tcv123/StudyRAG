1.6 Sequences & Series

Mathematics B (MEI) — OCR B — A-Level (H640) — Pure Mathematics — spec refs s1, binomial expansion

What this topic covers. Arithmetic and geometric sequences and series, sigma notation, recurrence relations and limits, and the binomial expansion — both for a positive integer index and for negative or fractional indices, where a validity condition applies.

1. Sequences and notation

A sequence is an ordered list of terms; a series is what you get when you add them. The \(n\)th term is written \(u_n\).

Sigma notation

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} u_k = u_1 + u_2 + \dots + u_n$$

The letter below is the starting index, the number above is the last. Two standard results:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} (2k-1) = n^2$$
Worked example. Write \(3+6+9+\dots+30\) in sigma notation. $$\sum_{k=1}^{10} 3k$$

Recurrence relations

A recurrence relation defines each term from the previous one, together with a starting value — for example \(u_{n+1} = u_n + 3\) with \(u_1 = 2\).

If the sequence converges to a limit \(L\), then replacing both \(u_{n+1}\) and \(u_n\) by \(L\) gives an equation for \(L\).

Worked example. Find the limit of \(u_{n+1} = \sqrt{2+u_n}\). $$L = \sqrt{2+L} \Longrightarrow L^2 = 2 + L \Longrightarrow L^2 - L - 2 = 0 \Longrightarrow (L-2)(L+1)=0$$

The terms are positive, so \(L=2\) (rejecting \(L=-1\)).

Always check the rejected root against the context. A limit of a sequence of square roots cannot be negative, and saying so explicitly is worth a mark.

2. Arithmetic sequences and series

An arithmetic sequence has a constant common difference \(d\) between consecutive terms.
$$u_n = a + (n-1)d, \qquad S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a + (n-1)d\big) = \frac{n}{2}(a + l)$$

where \(a\) is the first term and \(l\) the last.

Worked example. Find the sum of the first 20 terms with \(a=3\), \(d=5\). $$S_{20} = \frac{20}{2}\big(2(3) + 19(5)\big) = 10(6+95) = 1010$$
Worked example. How many terms are in \(5, 9, 13, \dots, 101\)? $$a + (n-1)d = 101 \Longrightarrow 5 + 4(n-1) = 101 \Longrightarrow n-1 = 24 \Longrightarrow n = 25$$
Worked example. An arithmetic sequence has 5th term 17 and 10th term 32. Find \(a\) and \(d\).

The gap between the 5th and 10th terms is \(5d = 32-17 = 15\), so \(d=3\). Then \(a = 17 - 4(3) = 5\).

Worked example. An arithmetic series has \(a=20\), \(d=-3\). For which \(n\) is \(S_n\) greatest?

The sum grows while the terms are positive. \(u_n = 20 - 3(n-1) > 0\) gives \(23 > 3n\), so \(n < 7.67\). At \(n=7\) the term is \(20-18 = 2\) (still positive); at \(n=8\) it is \(-1\). So \(S_n\) is greatest at \(n=7\).

"\(n\)th term" and "sum to \(n\) terms" are constantly mixed up under time pressure. Read the question twice: \(u_n\) is a single term, \(S_n\) is a running total.

3. Geometric sequences and series

A geometric sequence has a constant common ratio \(r\): each term is the previous one multiplied by \(r\).
$$u_n = ar^{\,n-1}, \qquad S_n = \frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r} \quad (r \neq 1)$$
Worked example. For \(a=2\), \(r=3\), find \(S_5\). $$S_5 = \frac{2(1-3^5)}{1-3} = \frac{2(1-243)}{-2} = 242$$

Sum to infinity

If \(|r| < 1\) the series converges and $$S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}$$ If \(|r| \ge 1\) it diverges and no sum to infinity exists.
Worked example. Find the sum to infinity of \(8 + 4 + 2 + \dots\).

Here \(a=8\) and \(r=\tfrac12\), and \(|r|<1\), so

$$S_\infty = \frac{8}{1-\tfrac12} = 16$$
Worked example. A geometric series has \(S_\infty = 3a\). Find \(r\). $$\frac{a}{1-r} = 3a \Longrightarrow \frac{1}{1-r} = 3 \Longrightarrow 1-r = \tfrac13 \Longrightarrow r = \tfrac23$$
State the condition \(|r|<1\) whenever you use \(S_\infty\). MEI mark schemes award a mark for it, and using the formula on a divergent series produces a plausible-looking but meaningless number.

4. Binomial expansion: positive integer index

$$(1+x)^n = 1 + nx + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!}x^2 + \frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{3!}x^3 + \dots$$

Equivalently, using binomial coefficients,

$$(a+b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} \binom{n}{r} a^{\,n-r} b^{\,r}$$

For a positive integer \(n\) the expansion terminates, giving exactly \(n+1\) terms. The coefficients are the rows of Pascal's triangle; \(\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1\).

Worked example. Find the coefficient of \(x^3\) in \((1+2x)^5\).

The required term is \(\binom{5}{3}(2x)^3 = 10 \times 8x^3 = 80x^3\), so the coefficient is 80.

Worked example. Find the coefficient of \(x^2\) in \((2+x)^6\). $$\binom{6}{2}\,2^{4}\,x^2 = 15 \times 16 x^2 = 240x^2$$
When the bracket is \((1+2x)\) or \((2+x)\), the whole term must be raised to the power — \((2x)^3 = 8x^3\), not \(2x^3\). Forgetting to cube the 2 is the standard error.
Worked example. Find the coefficient of \(x^3\) in \((1+x)(1+2x)^4\).

First expand \((1+2x)^4 = 1 + 8x + 24x^2 + 32x^3 + 16x^4\).

Terms in \(x^3\) come from \(1 \times 32x^3\) and \(x \times 24x^2\):

$$32 + 24 = 56$$

5. Binomial expansion: negative and fractional index

For any real \(n\), the same series formula holds, but the expansion is infinite and valid only when \(|x| < 1\): $$(1+x)^n = 1 + nx + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!}x^2 + \frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{3!}x^3 + \dots$$
Worked example. Expand \((1+x)^{1/2}\) as far as the term in \(x^2\). $$1 + \tfrac12 x + \frac{\tfrac12\left(-\tfrac12\right)}{2}x^2 = 1 + \frac{x}{2} - \frac{x^2}{8}$$
Worked example. Expand \((1+3x)^{-2}\) as far as the term in \(x^2\). $$1 + (-2)(3x) + \frac{(-2)(-3)}{2}(3x)^2 = 1 - 6x + 3(9x^2) = 1 - 6x + 27x^2$$

The validity condition

The expansion of \((1+ax)^n\) for non-integer \(n\) is valid when \(|ax| < 1\), that is \(|x| < \dfrac{1}{|a|}\).

Worked example. State the validity of the expansion of \((1+2x)^{1/2}\).

Valid for \(|2x| < 1\), i.e. \(|x| < \tfrac12\).

The validity condition is worth its own mark and is very often omitted. It is \(|x| < \tfrac{1}{|a|}\), not \(|x|<1\), whenever the bracket contains a coefficient on \(x\).

Approximations

Worked example. Use the expansion of \((1+x)^{1/2}\) to approximate \(\sqrt{1.02}\).

Take \(x = 0.02\):

$$\sqrt{1.02} \approx 1 + \frac{0.02}{2} - \frac{(0.02)^2}{8} = 1 + 0.01 - 0.00005 = 1.00995$$
For a bracket not starting with 1, factorise first: \(\sqrt{4+x} = 2\left(1+\tfrac{x}{4}\right)^{1/2}\). Applying the series directly to \((4+x)^{1/2}\) is invalid.

6. Common pitfalls

  • Confusing \(u_n\) with \(S_n\).
  • Using \(S_\infty\) without checking, or without stating, that \(|r|<1\).
  • Forgetting to raise the coefficient as well as \(x\): \((2x)^3 = 8x^3\).
  • Omitting the validity condition for a non-integer index, or giving \(|x|<1\) when it should be \(|x|<\tfrac{1}{|a|}\).
  • Applying the infinite series to a bracket not of the form \((1+\dots)\) without factorising first.
  • Off-by-one errors in the number of terms: \(u_n = a+(n-1)d\), not \(a+nd\).
  • Missing one of the contributing terms when a binomial is multiplied by another bracket.
