C2.1 How Have Our Ideas About Atoms Developed Over Time?

The modern model of the atom was built up step by step. Each model was rejected, modified or extended when new evidence became available that it could not explain.

Scientist / dateModelEvidence / reason for change
Ancient Greeks (c. 400 BC)Everything made of four "elements": earth, air, fire and water. (Democritus also suggested matter was made of tiny indivisible particles — atomos.)Ideas based on reasoning, not experiment.
John Dalton (1803)First particle model: elements are made of tiny, solid, indivisible atoms; atoms of the same element are identical; atoms combine in fixed ratios to form compounds.Explained his measurements of the masses of elements that combine in reactions.
J.J. Thomson (1897)"Plum pudding" model: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.He discovered the electron (cathode-ray experiments) — so atoms are not indivisible and contain smaller charged particles.
Ernest Rutherford (1911)Nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus containing most of the mass, with electrons around it; most of the atom is empty space.Geiger and Marsden's gold foil (alpha scattering) experiment: most alpha particles passed straight through, but a few were deflected through large angles — impossible with a plum pudding.
Niels Bohr (1913)Electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed shells (energy levels) at set distances.Explained why atoms emit light only at particular wavelengths (line spectra), and why atoms are stable. His calculations agreed with experimental observations.

Later, the proton (Rutherford, about 1919) and the neutron (James Chadwick, 1932) were discovered inside the nucleus.

Ideas about Science (IaS3) — how models develop. Scientists suggested explanations, made predictions from them, checked those predictions by experiment, and built on each other's work (Rutherford was Thomson's student; Bohr worked with Rutherford). A model is kept until new evidence it cannot explain appears; then it is modified or replaced. Creative thinking is needed to propose a new model — it does not "emerge" automatically from the data.

The Structure of the Atom

An atom has a small, positively charged nucleus (protons and neutrons) surrounded by negatively charged electrons in shells. The radius of the nucleus is much smaller than the radius of the atom, but almost all of the mass is in the nucleus. Atoms are neutral overall: number of electrons = number of protons.

Size and Scale

  • Atoms are about 10⁻¹⁰ m across (0.1 nm).
  • The nucleus is about one hundred-thousandth (1/100 000) of the diameter of the atom: about 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Small molecules (e.g. H₂O, CO₂, CH₄) are about 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁹ m; molecules contain from two to hundreds of atoms (large molecules such as proteins and polymers are much bigger).
  • Anything you can see with the naked eye contains many millions of atoms.
A human hair is about 1 × 10⁻⁴ m wide. Roughly how many atoms (diameter 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) would fit side by side across it?
number = (1 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (1 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 1 × 10⁻⁴⁺¹⁰ = 1 × 10⁶ — about a million atoms.
If an atom were scaled up to the size of a football stadium (about 100 m across), how big would the nucleus be?
nucleus = atom ÷ 100 000 = 100 m ÷ 10⁵ = 10⁻³ m = 1 mm — about the size of a grain of sand in the centre circle. The atom is mostly empty space.

Atomic Number, Mass Number and Isotopes

Atomic number (Z) = number of protons (the order of the element in the Periodic Table).
Mass number (A) = number of protons + number of neutrons.
Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers.
protons = atomic number   ·   electrons = protons (in an atom)   ·   neutrons = mass number − atomic number

The Periodic Table gives the relative atomic mass (e.g. 35.5 for chlorine), which is an average over the isotopes. When asked about a specific atom, use its mass number; if you are told to use the Periodic Table, round the relative atomic mass to the nearest whole number (e.g. Na 23.0 → 12 neutrons).

C2.2 What Does the Periodic Table Tell Us About the Elements?

Structure of the Modern Periodic Table

  • Elements are arranged in order of atomic number (number of protons).
  • Groups are the vertical columns. Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell (Group 1 = 1, Group 7 = 7, Group 0 = full outer shell) and so similar chemical properties.
  • Periods are the horizontal rows. The period number = number of occupied electron shells. The number of shells increases down a group and stays the same across a period.
Silicon has atomic number 14. Where is it in the Periodic Table?
14 electrons: arrangement 2.8.4 → 3 shells = Period 3; 4 outer electrons = Group 4. The position follows from the electron arrangement, which follows from the atomic number.

Mendeleev's Periodic Table

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements in order of relative atomic mass, and put elements with similar properties (e.g. the formulae of their oxides and chlorides) in the same columns. He did not know about protons or electrons.

  • He left gaps for elements not yet discovered, and predicted their properties from the elements around the gaps.
  • He swapped the order of some elements (e.g. tellurium and iodine) so that they fitted with elements of similar properties, even though this broke the atomic mass order.
PropertyMendeleev's prediction for "eka-silicon" (1871)Germanium (discovered 1886)
Relative atomic mass7272.6
Density / g cm⁻³5.55.3
Formula of oxideEsO₂GeO₂
Appearancegrey metalgrey-white metalloid

Why his ideas were accepted: when new elements (gallium, scandium, germanium) were discovered, they fitted his gaps and had the properties he predicted. Later, the discovery of protons showed that ordering by atomic number (not mass) puts tellurium (Z = 52) before iodine (Z = 53) — exactly as Mendeleev had done. Atomic structure explained the order he proposed: the reversed pairs occur because of isotopes (tellurium's common isotopes are heavier).

Ideas about Science (IaS3 and IaS1). Mendeleev's table is an example of an explanation that needed a leap of imagination. It was accepted because it made predictions that were tested and found to be correct, and because a later model (atomic structure) supported it. You should also be able to make and test predictions about trends and patterns in the Periodic Table (IaS1).

Metals and Non-Metals

Metals are on the left and in the centre of the Periodic Table; non-metals are on the right (above the staircase line). Metal atoms have few outer electrons (1, 2 or 3) which they lose in reactions; non-metal atoms have more outer electrons (4 to 7) and tend to gain or share electrons.

Group 1 — the Alkali Metals

Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium. All have 1 electron in the outer shell. They are soft metals that can be cut with a knife, have low densities (Li, Na and K float on water) and low melting points for metals. They are stored under oil because they react with air and water.

Reactivity increases down Group 1 because the atoms get larger: the single outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner shells, so it is attracted less strongly and is lost more easily.

Experiments: Group 1 Reactivity (Demonstration)

Group 1 reactions with water are carried out by the teacher as a demonstration: cut a rice-grain-sized piece of the metal with a scalpel on a tile, dry off the oil, drop it into a large trough of water behind a safety screen; wear goggles. Compare how vigorously Li, Na and K react (speed of fizzing, movement, flame), and test the solution with universal indicator. Keep pieces the same size so it is a fair comparison.

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine. All have 7 electrons in the outer shell and exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂). They are non-metals, toxic, and their vapours are coloured.

Displacement Reactions (PAG1 — Reactivity Trend)

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its metal halide. Add a few drops of halogen solution (chlorine water, bromine water, iodine solution) to potassium chloride, bromide and iodide solutions in a spotting tile or test tubes and look for colour changes.

Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)

This shows the order of reactivity: chlorine > bromine > iodine. Reactivity decreases down Group 7 because the atoms get larger: the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so an incoming electron is attracted less strongly and is gained less easily.

Group 1 and Group 7 trends go in opposite directions. Group 1: reactivity increases down (outer electron lost more easily). Group 7: reactivity decreases down (electron gained less easily). In both, the reason is the distance of the outer shell from the nucleus and shielding.

Predicting Reactions from the Periodic Table

  • Elements in the same group react in similar ways and form compounds with similar formulae (e.g. LiCl, NaCl, KCl; NaF, NaCl, NaBr).
  • Use the trend to predict reactivity: rubidium and caesium will react with water even more violently than potassium; fluorine is the most reactive halogen and astatine the least.
  • Predict physical properties by extending the trend: e.g. astatine is expected to be a dark (black) solid at room temperature, with a higher melting point than iodine.
Predict the products and write the equation for rubidium reacting with water.
Rubidium is in Group 1 like sodium, so it reacts the same way but more vigorously: 2Rb(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2RbOH(aq) + H₂(g). The solution is alkaline (rubidium hydroxide).

C2.3 How Do Metals and Non-Metals Combine to Form Compounds?

Group 0 — the Noble Gases

Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. Their atoms have a full outer shell of electrons (2 for helium, 8 for the others). This stable arrangement means they have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons, so they are unreactive (inert).

  • They exist as single atoms (monatomic) with very weak forces between them, so they have very low melting and boiling points and are all colourless gases at room temperature and pressure.
  • Boiling points increase down the group (He −269 °C, Ne −246 °C, Ar −186 °C, Kr −153 °C, Xe −108 °C) as the atoms get larger.
  • Uses come from their lack of reactivity: argon in light bulbs and welding, helium in balloons (low density, non-flammable).

Outer Electrons Decide the Properties

  • Elements react to get a full outer shell (like a noble gas). Metals with 1–3 outer electrons lose them; non-metals with 5–7 outer electrons gain electrons.
  • So the number of outer electrons (and hence the group) determines how an element reacts, and the number of shells (the period) affects how easily this happens.
  • Since the electron arrangement is fixed by the atomic number, the atomic number determines both the position in the Periodic Table and the chemical reactions.
  • Metals are on the left because their atoms have few outer electrons; non-metals on the right because they have nearly full outer shells.

Ionic Bonding

An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) that has lost or gained electrons and so has an overall charge. Ionic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It forms when a metal reacts with a non-metal: electrons are transferred from the metal atoms to the non-metal atoms.
  • Metal atoms lose their outer electrons to form positive ions with complete outer shells, e.g. Na (2.8.1) → Na⁺ (2.8) + e⁻.
  • Non-metal atoms gain electrons to fill their outer shell, forming negative ions, e.g. Cl (2.8.7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2.8.8).
  • The number of electrons lost or gained = the charge on the ion: Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, Group 6 → 2−, Group 7 → 1−.

Protons, Neutrons and Electrons in Ions

Ions have the same number of protons and neutrons as the atom; only the number of electrons changes. Positive ion: electrons = protons − charge. Negative ion: electrons = protons + charge.

Giant Ionic Lattices

In an ionic compound such as sodium chloride, huge numbers of positive and negative ions are arranged in a regular, repeating three-dimensional giant ionic lattice. Each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge and the electrostatic forces act in all directions.

In the 2-D layer above there are 16 ions: 8 Cl⁻ and 8 Na⁺. What does this tell you about the formula?
ratio Na⁺ : Cl⁻ = 8 : 8 = 1 : 1, so the formula is NaCl. The charges balance: (+1) + (−1) = 0. In the 3-D drawing, each Na⁺ touches 6 Cl⁻ and each Cl⁻ touches 6 Na⁺.

Ideas about Science (IaS3) — representational models. Dot and cross diagrams and 2-D/3-D lattice drawings are representational models: they help us visualise and explain bonding and make predictions (e.g. formulae), but each simplifies reality. In an exam, be ready to say what a given model shows well and what it leaves out, and to translate between a 3-D picture, a 2-D drawing and a formula or ratio.

C2.4 How Are Equations Used to Represent Chemical Reactions?

Formulae of Elements

  • Metals and Group 0: single symbol — Na, Fe, Cu, He, Ar.
  • Diatomic non-metals: H₂, N₂, O₂ and the halogens F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.
  • Simple covalent compounds: H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, HCl, SO₂.

Working Out Ionic Formulae

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge (the compound is neutral). Use brackets for more than one compound ion.

Write the formulae of potassium bromide, sodium oxide, magnesium chloride and calcium hydroxide.

Potassium reacts with water to form potassium hydroxide solution and hydrogen. Write a balanced symbol equation with state symbols.
Word equation: potassium + water → potassium hydroxide + hydrogen

C2.5 What Are the Properties of the Transition Metals?

The transition metals are the block of metals in the middle of the Periodic Table (between Groups 2 and 3). They do not show clear group trends like Groups 1 and 7; instead they form a family with similar general properties that make them very useful.

Practical: Colours and Catalysis

  • Colours: make solutions of transition metal compounds (e.g. copper(II) sulfate, iron(II) sulfate, iron(III) chloride, chromium(III) salts) and compare them with Group 1 compounds (e.g. sodium chloride — colourless).
  • Catalysts: add equal small masses of different metal oxides (e.g. copper(II) oxide, iron(III) oxide, manganese(IV) oxide) to equal volumes of hydrogen peroxide solution; compare the volume of oxygen collected in a gas syringe in a set time (relights a glowing splint). The best catalyst gives oxygen fastest. Filter, dry and reweigh the solid afterwards to show the catalyst is not used up.
Ideas about Science (IaS4). Transition metals and their compounds are used widely to make consumer goods (wiring, steel, jewellery, electronics) and as industrial catalysts (e.g. iron in the Haber process), which save energy and resources — beneficial applications of science.
Exam questions often give a table of data (melting point, density, colour of compounds) and ask which element is a transition metal. Look for: high melting point, high density, coloured compounds, more than one ion charge. Learn the five named examples — copper, iron, chromium, silver and gold.

Summary & Key Equations

  • Atomic models: Greeks → Dalton (solid spheres) → Thomson (plum pudding) → Rutherford (nucleus) → Bohr (shells)
  • Proton +1, mass 1; neutron 0, mass 1; electron −1, mass ≈ 0. Atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m; nucleus ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m
  • neutrons = mass number − atomic number; electrons = protons − charge on the ion
  • Group number = outer electrons; period number = number of shells
  • Mendeleev: ordered by atomic mass, left gaps, predicted properties, swapped Te/I — confirmed by discoveries and atomic numbers
  • Group 1: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂; 2M + Cl₂ → 2MCl; reactivity increases down
  • Group 7: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂; reactivity decreases down
  • Group 0: full outer shell, monatomic, unreactive, low boiling points
  • Ionic bonding = electron transfer + electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice
  • Ionic compounds: high mp; conduct when molten or aqueous, not solid; often soluble
  • State symbols (s) (l) (g) (aq); formulae from balancing ion charges
  • Transition metals: high mp and density, less reactive, coloured ions with different charges, catalysts
