C1.1 How Has the Earth's Atmosphere Changed Over Time, and Why?

The Particle Model

The particle model says all matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are constantly moving and that attract one another. The arrangement, movement and strength of attraction between the particles explain the three states of matter.
StateArrangementMovementBulk properties explained
SolidClose together, regular patternVibrate about fixed positionsFixed shape and volume; cannot be compressed
LiquidClose together (touching), randomSlide past each otherFlows, takes the shape of the container; fixed volume; hardly compressible
GasFar apart, randomFast, random, in all directionsFills the container; easily compressed; particles hitting the walls cause gas pressure

Physical and Chemical Changes

  • A physical change (e.g. melting, boiling, dissolving) does not make a new substance. The particles themselves are unchanged — only their arrangement and movement change — so it is easily reversed and mass is conserved.
  • A chemical change (a reaction, e.g. burning) makes new substances: atoms are rearranged and bonds are broken and made. It is usually hard to reverse. Mass is still conserved.
The particles do not have the properties of the bulk substance. A single copper atom is not orange and does not conduct; a sulfur atom is not yellow; a single water molecule is not "wet" and does not "melt". Particles do not expand when heated — the spaces between them get bigger. Writing "the particles melt" or "the particles get bigger" loses marks.

Limitations of the Particle Model (HT)

Diagrams of particles as small solid spheres are a useful model, but they have limitations (HT):

  • They do not show the scale correctly — real particles are far smaller, and in a gas the spaces between particles are much larger than can be drawn (about 10 particle diameters apart in air).
  • They treat particles as inelastic (hard, solid) spheres. Real atoms and molecules are not hard balls: they have a nucleus and electrons, can have different shapes, and can deform or vibrate internally.
  • They do not fully show the forces between particles — the different strengths of attraction in different substances, and the fact that particles attract over a distance, not only when touching.
  • They do not show particle motion (speeds vary and change continually) in a still picture.

Why Substances Change State at Different Temperatures

To melt or boil a substance, energy must be transferred to it (by heating) to overcome the forces of attraction between its particles. When a liquid freezes or a gas condenses, attractions form and energy is transferred to the surroundings.

  • Weak forces between particles (e.g. between small molecules such as O₂, N₂, CH₄) → little energy needed → low melting and boiling points (gases at room temperature).
  • Strong forces (e.g. between ions in sodium chloride or atoms in a metal) → lots of energy needed → high melting and boiling points.
  • During a change of state the temperature stays constant: the energy transferred is used to break (or is released by forming) attractions between particles, not to change the speed of the particles.

Practical: Cooling Curve for Stearic Acid (or Heating Curve for Ice)

Melt stearic acid in a boiling tube in a hot water bath, put a thermometer in it, remove it from the water and record the temperature every 30 seconds as it cools. Plot temperature (y-axis) against time (x-axis). The flat section shows the freezing (melting) point.

How the Atmosphere Formed and Changed

Scientists' explanations are based on evidence, including the types and chemical composition of ancient rocks (e.g. iron-rich rock layers that could only form once oxygen was present) and fossils of early life (e.g. stromatolites made by photosynthesising bacteria).

  1. Early volcanic activity released gases — mainly carbon dioxide and water vapour, with nitrogen and small amounts of other gases (e.g. methane, ammonia). There was little or no oxygen.
  2. The Earth cooled; water vapour condensed (a change of state: gas → liquid) and formed the oceans.
  3. Carbon dioxide dissolved in the oceans and was locked up in carbonate sedimentary rocks (e.g. limestone, from shells and skeletons of marine organisms).
  4. Photosynthesising organisms (algae, then plants) evolved: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. They removed CO₂ and slowly built up oxygen.
  5. Carbon from dead organisms was trapped as fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas). The rising oxygen level allowed animals to evolve.

Today's dry air: about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon, 0.04% carbon dioxide, plus traces of other gases. The amount of water vapour varies.

Ideas about Science (IaS3) — data vs explanations. Data are the observations (e.g. "rocks older than 2.4 billion years contain unoxidised iron minerals"). An explanation is the idea proposed to account for them (e.g. "the early atmosphere contained almost no oxygen"). Nobody observed the early atmosphere, so explanations are developed creatively from evidence and can change as new evidence (e.g. new rock samples or fossils) is found. The particle model is also used here to explain state changes such as the formation of the oceans and the water cycle (evaporation, condensation).

Formulae and Balanced Equations

Many non-metal elements exist as diatomic molecules: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. Metals and the noble gases are written as single symbols (Fe, Na, He). Simple covalent compounds include H₂O, CO₂, CO, CH₄, NH₃, SO₂, NO, NO₂ and HCl.

Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction (conservation of mass), so a symbol equation must have the same number of each type of atom on both sides. Balance by changing the numbers in front of formulae — never the small subscript numbers inside a formula.

Balance the combustion of propane: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
1. Carbon: 3 on the left, so 3CO₂.
2. Hydrogen: 8 on the left, so 4H₂O.
3. Oxygen on the right: (3 × 2) + (4 × 1) = 10 atoms = 5 O₂.
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O   Check: C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 10 ✓
Incomplete combustion of methane making carbon monoxide: CH₄ + O₂ → CO + H₂O
Use ratios: 1 CH₄ gives 1 CO and 2 H₂O, which need 3 O atoms = 1½ O₂. Double everything to remove the fraction:
2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O

Combustion is Oxidation

Oxidation is the gain of oxygen by a substance. When a fuel burns, its carbon and hydrogen combine with oxygen (C → CO₂, H → H₂O), so combustion is an oxidation reaction. Forming SO₂ from sulfur and NO/NO₂ from nitrogen are also oxidations.

Temperature of surroundingsRises (the mixture/thermometer gets warmer)Falls (gets colder)

Reaction Profiles and Activation Energy

The activation energy is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur — the energy needed to start breaking bonds in the reactants. This is why a fuel needs a spark or flame before it burns.
  • Activation energy = height from the reactants to the top of the hump.
  • Overall energy change = difference between reactants and products. Exothermic: products lower than reactants. Endothermic: products higher.

Bond Breaking and Bond Making

  • Breaking bonds needs energy — it is endothermic.
  • Making bonds releases energy — it is exothermic.
  • If more energy is released making new bonds than is needed to break the old ones, the reaction is exothermic overall. If less, it is endothermic.

(HT) Calculate the energy change for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.

Bonds made: 2 × H–Cl = 2 × 432 = 864 kJ
Energy change = 679 − 864 = −185 kJ/mol → exothermic.
(HT) Calculate the energy change for burning methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
Broken: 4 × C–H + 2 × O=O = (4 × 413) + (2 × 498) = 1652 + 996 = 2648 kJ
Made: 2 × C=O + 4 × O–H = (2 × 805) + (4 × 464) = 1610 + 1856 = 3466 kJ
Energy change = 2648 − 3466 = −818 kJ/mol → exothermic.
Draw out every molecule's bonds before counting — CO₂ has two C=O bonds and each H₂O has two O–H bonds. Multiply by the balancing numbers. Always state "exothermic" or "endothermic" from the sign.

Practical: Is a Reaction Exothermic or Endothermic?

  1. Measure a fixed volume (e.g. 25 cm³) of one reactant solution into a polystyrene cup standing in a beaker (for stability).
  2. Record the starting temperature with a thermometer (or temperature probe).
  3. Add the second reactant (a measured mass or volume), put on the lid, and stir.
  4. Record the highest (or lowest) temperature reached.
  5. Temperature rise → exothermic; temperature fall → endothermic. Repeat to check the results are repeatable.

The polystyrene cup and lid are insulators: they reduce energy transfer to or from the surroundings so the temperature change measured is closer to the true value.

Chemical Cells and Fuel Cells

A chemical cell (e.g. a battery) uses a chemical reaction to produce a potential difference. It keeps producing a potential difference until the reactants are used up; then it is "flat". Non-rechargeable cells must then be replaced.

A fuel cell is supplied continuously with a fuel and oxygen, so it keeps producing a potential difference as long as the fuel is supplied. In a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell the overall reaction is the same as burning hydrogen, and the only product is water — but the energy is transferred electrically to drive an electric motor instead of heating gases in an internal combustion engine.

hydrogen + oxygen → water     2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
Advantages of hydrogen fuel cells (for vehicles)Disadvantages
  • Only product is water — no CO₂, CO, NOₓ or particulates at the point of use, improving air quality in cities
  • More efficient than internal combustion engines; fewer moving parts
  • Refuel quickly; longer range than many battery cars
  • Hydrogen is usually made by electrolysis (or from natural gas); if the electricity comes from fossil fuels, pollutants are produced elsewhere
  • Hydrogen is a gas: hard to store (high-pressure tanks), flammable/explosive
  • Few refuelling stations; fuel cells are expensive (they use platinum catalysts)

Other fuel cells (e.g. methanol fuel cells) are easier to refuel because the fuel is a liquid, but they produce CO₂. Rechargeable batteries are an alternative for cars and phones but take longer to recharge and contain toxic metal compounds that must be disposed of carefully.

Ideas about Science (IaS4). Fuel cells are a positive application of science that can reduce emissions where people live. When evaluating a technology, consider the whole picture: where the hydrogen comes from, the cost, the risks of storage, and who benefits.

C1.3 What Is the Evidence for Climate Change, and Why Is It Occurring?

The Greenhouse Effect

  1. Electromagnetic radiation from the Sun (mostly visible light, shorter wavelength) passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, warming it. Some is reflected.
  2. The warm Earth emits infrared radiation (longer wavelength).
  3. Greenhouse gases — e.g. carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour — absorb some of this infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the Earth.
  4. This keeps the Earth warmer than it would otherwise be. Without any greenhouse effect the average surface temperature would be about −18 °C — too cold for most life.
Greenhouse gases do not "trap heat" or "reflect" radiation. Say they absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth and re-emit it in all directions. Also, the greenhouse effect is natural and necessary; it is the enhanced greenhouse effect from extra greenhouse gases that causes global warming. The ozone layer is a different issue.

Evidence for Human (Anthropogenic) Causes

Over the last 200 years human activities have increased the proportion of greenhouse gases:

  • Carbon dioxide: burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement manufacture.
  • Methane: cattle and other livestock, rice paddy fields, landfill sites, leaks from gas extraction.
  • Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution to over 410 ppm today. Data come from air bubbles trapped in ice cores (past) and direct measurements (since 1958).
  • There is a correlation between fossil fuel consumption, atmospheric CO₂ concentration and global average temperature (about 1 °C warmer than in 1850).
  • There is a plausible mechanism (the greenhouse effect) linking the extra CO₂ to warming — so most scientists accept that the correlation reflects cause and effect.

Uncertainties in the Evidence

  • Past temperatures and CO₂ levels are estimated indirectly (ice cores, tree rings), so they have larger uncertainties; early direct measurements were few and not global.
  • The climate is very complex, with many factors (e.g. changes in the Sun's output, volcanic eruptions, ocean currents, cloud cover) affecting temperature.
  • Climate models are computer models that use patterns in past data and known science to predict the future; different models give a range of predictions. As more data are collected, uncertainty decreases and confidence in the models increases.
Ideas about Science (IaS3) — correlation, cause and models. A correlation alone does not prove one factor causes another; you need a plausible mechanism. Individual cases (one hot summer, one cold winter) are not convincing evidence for or against a trend — long-term data from many places are needed. Climate science is checked by peer review, where other scientists evaluate claims before publication. Physical models (e.g. bottles of air and of CO₂ warmed by a lamp) and computer models are both used to investigate climate change.

Potential Effects of Increased CO₂ and Methane

  • Rising global temperatures and changes to where crops can be grown (some areas too hot or dry; others become suitable); food security issues.
  • More frequent and severe extreme weather — storms, droughts, heat waves, heavy rainfall.
  • Melting of polar ice and glaciers, and expansion of warmer sea water → rising sea levels → flooding of low-lying land and coastal erosion.
  • Changes to the distribution of wildlife and spread of disease; more CO₂ dissolving in the oceans makes them more acidic, harming coral and shellfish.

Mitigating the Effects

MethodScale, risk and environmental implications
Reduce fossil fuel use — renewable and nuclear electricity, energy efficiency, insulation, public transport, electric vehiclesLarge investment needed; renewables can be intermittent; nuclear produces radioactive waste; changes in lifestyle needed from billions of people.
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) — capture CO₂ from power stations and store it underground (e.g. in old gas fields)Expensive; uses energy; only demonstrated on a small scale compared with total emissions; risk of leakage.

Ideas about Science (IaS4) — risks, costs, benefits and decisions. Using fossil fuels brings benefits (cheap, reliable energy) but costs and risks (climate change, pollution, unsustainable use of a finite resource). Different countries, groups and individuals may make different decisions about climate change because their personal, social and economic circumstances differ — e.g. a developing country may prioritise economic growth, while a low-lying island nation faces flooding. Public regulation (targets for emissions) is needed because individual actions alone are not enough.

Using Data: Orders of Magnitude

You must be able to extract and interpret information from charts, graphs and tables, and use orders of magnitude (powers of ten) to judge whether a quantity is significant.

Global fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions are about 3.5 × 10¹⁰ tonnes per year. One car emits about 2 tonnes of CO₂ per year. A new low-emission scheme saves 2 × 10⁶ tonnes per year. Is it significant?
Fraction saved = (2 × 10⁶) ÷ (3.5 × 10¹⁰) ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁵, i.e. about 0.006% of global emissions — four orders of magnitude smaller. Useful, but on its own it will not have a measurable effect: many such measures are needed.
CO₂ makes up about 0.04% of dry air. Express this in parts per million (ppm).
0.04% = 0.04 / 100 = 4 × 10⁻⁴; × 10⁶ = 400 ppm.

C1.4 How Can Scientists Help Improve the Supply of Potable Water?

Potable water is water that is safe to drink: it has low levels of dissolved salts and microorganisms. It is not chemically pure — it still contains some dissolved substances.

The world's population is increasing, so there is a growing need for potable water. The method used depends on what source is available:

SourceTreatmentEase / cost
Ground water (from aquifers, rivers, lakes, reservoirs)Filtration through beds of sand and gravel removes solid particles; membrane filtration removes very small particles and microorganisms; chlorination kills remaining microorganismsEasiest and cheapest — relatively clean to start with
Waste water (sewage, industrial and agricultural waste)Screening and sedimentation remove grit and solids; aeration — air is bubbled through so aerobic bacteria break down organic matter; then filtration and chlorinationHarder — more stages needed to remove organic matter, harmful microorganisms and chemicals
Salt water (sea water) — desalinationDistillation (boil the water and condense the steam, leaving salts behind) or membrane filtration (reverse osmosis: water forced through a membrane that holds back dissolved salts)Hardest and most expensive — needs large amounts of energy; used where fresh water is scarce but energy is available
On distillation diagrams examiners check two details: the thermometer bulb is level with the side arm (it measures the temperature of the vapour going into the condenser), and the cooling water enters the condenser at the bottom (lower end) and leaves at the top, so the jacket stays full of cold water.

Chlorination

Chlorine is added to drinking water to kill microorganisms and stop the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid. Chlorine is toxic in larger amounts, and can react with organic matter to form small amounts of harmful by-products, but at the low concentrations used the benefits greatly outweigh the risks.

Test for Chlorine

GasTestPositive result
Chlorine, Cl₂Hold damp blue litmus paper in the gasTurns red (chlorine is acidic) and then is bleached white

Chlorine is toxic — carry out the test in a fume cupboard. The practical "identify unknown gases" (PAG2) brings together the tests for oxygen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and chlorine.

Ideas about Science (IaS4). Water treatment technologies make a big positive difference to people's lives by preventing disease. Access to treated water raises issues of risk, cost and benefit: in some countries chlorination is required by public regulation, but other parts of the world still lack treated water, leading to a higher risk of disease. Providing treated water for everyone raises ethical questions — who should pay, and is it right that some people have safe water while others do not? A common ethical argument is that the right decision gives the best outcome for the greatest number of people.
