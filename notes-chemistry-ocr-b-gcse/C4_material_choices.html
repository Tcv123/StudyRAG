C4.1 How Is Data Used to Choose a Material for a Particular Use?

Chemists assess materials by measuring their physical properties, then use the data to compare materials and match one to the specification of a product (the list of properties it needs). The huge range of materials developed by chemists improves our quality of life (IaS4).

Composites

A composite combines two or more materials to get properties neither has on its own. Materials may be combined on a bulk scale — e.g. reinforced concrete: concrete is strong in compression, steel rods are strong in tension, so beams resist both — or nanoparticles may be embedded in a matrix (e.g. carbon nanotubes in sports equipment, C4.4). Fibreglass (glass fibres in polymer resin) is light, strong and can be moulded into boat hulls.

Alloys

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements (usually other metals, or carbon). Alloys have different properties from pure metals because atoms of different sizes disrupt the regular layers of the metal lattice, making it harder for layers to slide — so alloys are usually harder and stronger than the pure metal.

Practical work: test a range of materials (e.g. for hardness by scratching, flexibility by bending, conductivity with a simple circuit, density by mass ÷ volume) and use the results to classify them.

C4.2 What Are the Different Types of Polymers?

Polymers are very long-chain molecules made by joining many small molecules called monomers. They occur naturally and can be made synthetically. The repeating unit is the section of chain that repeats over and over.

Addition Polymerisation

Monomers based on alkenes (made by cracking, C3.4) have a C=C double bond as their functional group. In addition polymerisation the double bonds open up and the monomers join together into a long chain. The polymer is the only product. These polymers are generally known as ‘plastics’.

A polymer has the repeating unit –[CH₂–CHCN]– (a CN group on the second carbon). Deduce the monomer.
Remove the brackets and the bonds sticking out, and put back the double bond between the two carbons: CH₂=CHCN (the monomer for an acrylic fibre).

Practical work: crack poly(ethene) by heating it over a hot catalyst and test the gas for unsaturation with bromine water (orange → colourless shows an alkene).

Condensation Polymerisation (HT)

(HT) Condensation polymers were developed to make substitutes for natural fibres such as wool and silk (e.g. polyesters and nylon).

  • (HT) They usually form from two different monomers, each with two functional groups (one at each end) — that is the minimum needed, so the chain can keep growing in both directions.
  • (HT) When two monomers join, the –OH from a carboxylic acid group and an H atom from the other monomer combine to form a small molecule, water, which is released at the same time as each link forms.
  • (HT) Dicarboxylic acid + diol (two –OH groups) → polyester (ester links, –COO–).
  • (HT) Dicarboxylic acid + diamine (two –NH₂ groups) → polyamide, e.g. nylon (amide links, –CONH–).
(HT) Monomer A is HOOC–▭–COOH and monomer B is HO–○–OH. Give the repeating unit and the other product.
Join the C of each acid group to the O of an alcohol group, removing OH from the acid and H from the alcohol each time.
Repeating unit: –[OC–▭–COO–○–O]–; product: water, 2 molecules for each repeating unit formed (2n H₂O). Working backwards, split every ester link and add back –OH to the C=O side and –H to the O side.

Practical work: test the properties (strength, stretchiness, absorbency, effect of heat) of different polymer fibres, e.g. nylon, polyester, wool, cotton.

Naturally Occurring Polymers

  • DNA is a polymer made from four different monomers called nucleotides (each with a different base: A, T, C, G). Genes are sections of DNA.
  • Proteins are polymers of amino acids — similar in structure to polyamides (amide links join the amino acids).
  • Carbohydrates such as starch and cellulose are polymers of sugars (glucose).

Practical work: break down starch using the enzyme amylase; use iodine solution (orange-brown → blue-black with starch) and Benedict’s reagent (blue → brick-red precipitate on heating with sugars) to show starch disappearing and sugar forming.

C4.3 How Do Bonding and Structure Affect Properties of Materials?

The bulk properties of a material (strength, melting point, electrical and thermal conductivity, brittleness, flexibility, hardness, ease of reshaping) depend on the types of bond it contains, their strength compared with intermolecular forces and the way the bonds are arranged. A single atom does not have these properties: one copper atom is not ‘shiny’ or ‘malleable’ — the properties come from how vast numbers of atoms are bonded together. That is why the same atoms can make very different materials (diamond and graphite are both pure carbon).

Carbon: Chains, Rings and Families

  • A carbon atom has 4 outer electrons, so it forms four covalent bonds.
  • Carbon atoms can bond strongly to each other, forming chains (straight or branched) and rings, with single or double bonds, and can bond to H, O, N, Cl and others.
  • This gives a vast array of natural and synthetic organic compounds, which fall into families of similar compounds — homologous series such as the alkanes (C3.4).

Polymers: Bonding and Properties

Polymer molecules have the same strong covalent bonds as simple molecules, but because the chains are so long there are many more intermolecular forces between neighbouring chains. The strength of these forces decides the properties of the solid:

  • Longer chains → more intermolecular forces and more tangling → stronger, higher melting (softening) point.
  • Unbranched chains pack closely → stronger forces → denser, stiffer, harder (HDPE). Branched chains cannot pack closely → weaker, more flexible, lower density (LDPE).
  • Cross-links (covalent bonds between chains) stop the chains sliding → rigid, hard, do not soften on heating (they char instead) — e.g. vulcanised rubber, melamine.
  • Plasticisers are small molecules that push chains apart, weakening the forces → more flexible (e.g. soft PVC).
Ideas about Science (IaS2). Look for patterns in data about polymers. For example (illustrative data): poly(ethene) samples with average chain lengths of 1000, 5000 and 20 000 units softening at 95 °C, 110 °C and 125 °C show that longer chains give higher softening temperatures — explained by more intermolecular forces between longer chains.

Giant Covalent Structures

In a giant covalent structure a very large number of atoms are joined by covalent bonds in a continuous three-dimensional network — there are no separate molecules. To melt one, strong covalent bonds must be broken, so melting points are very high. Carbon forms several giant structures called allotropes (different structural forms of the same element), including diamond and graphite. Silicon dioxide (sand) is another giant covalent structure.

C4.4 Why Are Nanoparticles So Useful?

Nanotechnology is the use and control of structures that are very small: 1 to 100 nanometres in size. 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m (a billionth of a metre). Nanoparticles have a similar scale to individual molecules.
Standard form and ratio. An atom is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across and a nanoparticle is 2 × 10⁻⁸ m across. How many atoms fit across the nanoparticle?
Ratio = (2 × 10⁻⁸) ÷ (1 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 2 × 10² = 200 atoms. In nanometres: 20 nm ÷ 0.1 nm = 200.
Order these from smallest: 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, 3 × 10⁻⁹ m, 8 × 10⁻⁸ m → 3 × 10⁻⁹ m (3 nm) < 8 × 10⁻⁸ m (80 nm) < 5 × 10⁻⁷ m (500 nm). Only the first two are in the nano range.

Surface Area to Volume Ratio

For a cube of side L: surface area = 6L², volume = L³, so SA : V = 6/L. As particles get smaller, the surface area to volume ratio gets bigger: making the side 10 times smaller makes SA : V 10 times larger.

Find SA : V for a cubic nanoparticle of side 10 nm and compare it with a cube of side 1 µm.
10 nm cube: SA = 6 × (10)² = 600 nm²; V = 10³ = 1000 nm³; SA : V = 0.6 nm⁻¹ (= 6 × 10⁸ m⁻¹).
1 µm = 1000 nm cube: SA : V = 6 ÷ 1000 = 0.006 nm⁻¹.
The nanoparticle’s ratio is 100 times larger (side is 100 times smaller).

Because so many of their atoms are at the surface, nanoparticles behave differently from the same material in bulk:

  • They are excellent catalysts — reactions happen at surfaces, so less material does the same job.
  • They can be more reactive than bulk material, and can have new properties (e.g. nanoparticles of titanium dioxide or zinc oxide in sunscreen block UV but look transparent; silver nanoparticles are antibacterial).
  • Their tiny size lets them penetrate biological tissue and be built into other materials to modify their properties.

Fullerenes and Graphene

Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms arranged in hollow balls or tubes, made of rings of carbon atoms (mainly hexagons, with pentagons where the surface curves). Each carbon is bonded to three others.

MaterialStructureProperties → uses
Buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀Hollow ball of 60 C atoms (simple molecule)Cage can carry small molecules → delivering drugs into the body; slippery molecules → lubricants; catalysts
Carbon nanotubesCylinders of carbon hexagons, nanometres wide but longVery strong, light, conduct electricity → reinforcing composites (tennis rackets, bike frames), electronics; tiny pores → molecular sieves
GrapheneSingle layer of graphite, one atom thickVery strong, transparent, high electrical and thermal conductivity → flexible electronics, touchscreens, batteries, composites
Ideas about Science (IaS3). Developing fullerenes and graphene relied on imaginative thinking. C₆₀ was discovered in 1985 when Kroto, Curl and Smalley imagined a football-shaped cage to explain an unexpected result; graphene was first isolated in 2004 by Geim and Novoselov — famously using sticky tape to peel layers off graphite.

Risks of Nanoparticles

  • (a) Possible health effects due to size and surface area: nanoparticles can be breathed in, absorbed through skin or cross cell membranes; their large surface area may make them reactive or catalyse harmful reactions in the body; some long, thin nanotubes may behave like asbestos fibres in the lungs.
  • (b) Why there is more data about uses than about health effects: nanotechnology is new; companies fund research into useful (profitable) products; health effects may take many years to show; tiny particles are hard to detect and track; each type, size and shape can behave differently; testing on humans raises ethical problems.
  • (c) Relative risks and benefits: nanoparticles in sunscreen give a large benefit (protection from skin cancer) for a probably small risk, so many people accept them; nanosilver in socks to reduce odour offers a small benefit while silver washed into rivers could harm useful microorganisms.
Ideas about Science (IaS4). Because not much is known about some nanoparticles’ effects on the body, judgements about each use depend on balancing the perceived benefit against the risk. In a 6-mark answer, discuss a benefit, a risk, the lack of data, and who bears the risk, then give a justified conclusion.

C4.5 What Happens to Products at the End of Their Useful Life?

Corrosion and Rusting

Iron is the most widely used metal in the world, but iron products have a limited life because iron corrodes. Corrosion is the gradual reaction of a metal with substances in its surroundings. Rusting is the corrosion of iron and steel; it is an oxidation reaction that needs both oxygen (air) and water.

iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)

Salt water speeds rusting up. Rust is flaky and crumbles away, exposing fresh iron underneath, so rusting continues until the iron is eaten through. (Aluminium also corrodes, but forms a thin, tough oxide layer that protects the metal beneath.)

Practical work: investigate the factors needed for rusting (tubes as above, left for about a week) or for the corrosion of other metals, and the effectiveness of corrosion prevention (e.g. nails painted, greased, wrapped in zinc or magnesium, wrapped in copper, left in salt water).

Preventing Corrosion

  • Barrier methods keep oxygen and water away from the metal: paint (bridges, cars), oil or grease (moving parts, bike chains), plastic coating (garden furniture, wire), electroplating with a less easily corroded metal (tin cans: steel coated with tin). If the barrier is scratched, the iron underneath starts to rust.
  • Sacrificial protection: iron is attached to, or coated with, a more reactive metal such as zinc or magnesium. The more reactive metal oxidises in preference to iron, so the iron is protected even if the coating is damaged. Examples: galvanising (zinc coating on steel buckets, fences, car bodies), zinc or magnesium blocks bolted to ships’ hulls and underground pipes (replaced as they corrode away).
Ideas about Science (IaS4). Preventing corrosion is a scientific solution that improves sustainability: products last longer, so fewer metal ores are mined and less energy is used making replacements — a positive outcome in a life cycle assessment.

Oxidation and Reduction in Terms of Oxygen

Oxidation is the gain of oxygen; reduction is the loss of oxygen. They always happen together (redox).
  • Rusting: iron gains oxygen → iron is oxidised.
  • Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂: iron(III) oxide loses oxygen → reduced; carbon monoxide gains oxygen → oxidised.
  • 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: magnesium is oxidised.

Oxidation and Reduction in Terms of Electrons (HT)

(HT) Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons (OIL RIG).
  • (HT) In rusting, iron atoms lose electrons: Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ (iron is oxidised); oxygen gains electrons: O₂ + 4e⁻ → 2O²⁻ (oxygen is reduced).
  • (HT) In sacrificial protection, zinc is oxidised instead of iron: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • (HT) In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu: zinc loses electrons (oxidised); Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons (reduced).

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)

A life cycle assessment considers the overall impact of making, using and disposing of a product: the use of resources (raw materials, water, energy) and the environmental impact at every stage, from raw materials to disposal, including transport between stages.

Disposal Options

Biodegradable materials (paper, wood, cotton, some bioplastics) are broken down by microorganisms; non-biodegradable materials (most addition polymers such as poly(ethene), glass) are not, so they persist in landfill and the environment.

Why LCAs are not fully secure (IaS4): there is not always enough reliable data; some effects (e.g. visual pollution) need value judgements; and people do not always follow recommended disposal advice, so the real end-of-life route differs from the assumed one. LCAs can also be selectively written to support a company’s claims.

Reusing and Recycling PET Drinks Bottles

PET (poly(ethylene terephthalate)) is a polyester. Bottles can be reused (refilled after cleaning) or recycled:

  1. Collected (kerbside or bottle banks) and transported to a plant.
  2. Sorted from other plastics (by recycling code 1, or automatic scanners) and often by colour.
  3. Caps and labels removed; bottles washed to remove impurities.
  4. Shredded into flakes; caps (a different polymer) separated by flotation — PET is denser than water and sinks.
  5. Melted and made into pellets, then into new products: fleece clothing fibres, carpets, filling for sleeping bags, strapping, and new food-grade bottles.

Why PET recycling is viable: PET bottles are used in huge numbers, so there is a large, steady supply; they are easy to identify and separate; PET is thermosoftening, so it can be melted and reshaped; there is strong demand for products made from it; and it saves crude oil and energy compared with making new PET.

Factors in Recycling Decisions

Recycling conserves resources such as crude oil and metal ores, but will not on its own meet future demand unless habits change. Whether recycling a material is worthwhile depends on:

  • the finite nature of the raw material deposits (metal ores, crude oil);
  • the availability of the used material to collect;
  • the economic and practical costs of collection and sorting;
  • removing impurities (mixed plastics, alloys, food contamination);
  • energy used in transport and processing;
  • the scale of demand for the recycled product;
  • the environmental impact of the recycling process itself.
Products from metal oresProducts from crude oil (plastics)
Metals can be melted and reused again and again without losing quality. Recycling aluminium needs only about 5% of the energy needed to extract it from its ore by electrolysis, so it is very worthwhile. Steel is easily separated with magnets.Many different polymers are mixed together and are hard to sort; contamination is common; chains can shorten on reprocessing so quality falls (‘downcycling’); some cannot be remelted (thermosets); low price of new plastic reduces demand for recycled plastic.
Ideas about Science (IaS4). Products made from recycled materials do not always have a lower environmental impact than those made from new resources — e.g. if collection involves long transport distances or the cleaning uses lots of energy and water. Use LCAs to compare the sustainability of products and processes.

C4 Summary & Key Equations

  • Choose materials by matching measured properties (strength, stiffness, density, melting point, conductivity…) to a product’s specification
  • Composites combine properties; alloys are harder because different-sized atoms stop layers sliding
  • Addition polymers: C=C opens, polymer only; condensation polymers (HT): two functional groups per monomer, water also formed
  • DNA = 4 nucleotides; proteins = amino acids; starch/cellulose = sugars
  • Properties come from bonding and structure, not from individual atoms
  • Diamond: 4 bonds each, hard, non-conductor; graphite: 3 bonds, layers, conducts, lubricant
  • Nano = 1–100 nm; SA : V = 6/L for a cube; smaller particles → larger SA : V
  • Rusting needs oxygen + water; barrier vs sacrificial (more reactive Zn/Mg) protection
  • Oxidation = gain of O (loss of e⁻, HT); reduction = loss of O (gain of e⁻, HT)
  • LCA: raw materials → manufacture → use → disposal, with transport, energy and water at each stage
In “choose a material” questions, quote numbers from the data and link each property to the job it must do — “low density (0.92 g/cm³) so the bottle is light to transport” scores; “it is light” does not.
