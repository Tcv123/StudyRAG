C5 — Chemical Analysis

OCR GCSE Chemistry B (Twenty First Century Science, J258) — All papers (J258/01–04, Breadth and Depth in chemistry, Foundation and Higher) · (HT) = Higher tier only

Chemical analysis answers two questions: what is in a sample (qualitative analysis) and how much of each substance is there (quantitative analysis). This chapter covers purity and separation (C5.1), identifying ions and elements (C5.2), calculating amounts in reactions (C5.3) and measuring amounts in solution by titration (C5.4).

C5.1 How are chemicals separated and tested for purity?

Formulations

A formulation is a useful mixture made by combining several pure substances in carefully measured, definite proportions so the product does its job. Each component has a purpose.

Examples: medicines (active drug + fillers + binders + coatings), paints (pigment + binder + solvent + additives), fuels, cleaning products, cosmetics, fertilisers and alloys. Changing the proportions changes how the product behaves, so manufacturers must control the amount of every ingredient — and keep out impurities.

What does "pure" mean?

Scientific meaningEveryday meaning
A pure substance contains only one element or one compound — nothing else mixed in."Pure" means natural, unprocessed or with nothing added, e.g. "pure orange juice" or "pure mountain water".
Pure water is only H₂O molecules."Pure" orange juice is a mixture of water, sugars, acids, vitamins… so it is not pure in the scientific sense.

Ideas about Science — the particle model: in a pure substance every particle (atom, molecule or formula unit) is the same. In a mixture there are two or more kinds of particle. Different particles get in the way of the regular arrangement of a solid, which is why a mixture melts at a lower temperature and over a range.

Using melting points to test purity

  • A pure substance melts (and boils) at a sharp, fixed temperature that matches the reference (data book) value.
  • An impure substance melts at a lower temperature and over a range of temperatures. (Impurities also raise the boiling point.)
Sample (illustrative data)Melting point measuredConclusion (reference value 136 °C)
Aspirin, sample A135.5–136 °CSharp, matches reference → pure
Aspirin, sample B124–131 °CLower and over a 7 °C range → impure; needs further purification (e.g. recrystallisation)

Chromatography

Chromatography separates the substances in a mixture using two phases: a stationary phase that does not move (e.g. the paper, or the water held in it) and a mobile phase that moves through it (the solvent). Separation depends on how each substance is distributed between the two phases.
  • A substance that is more soluble in the mobile phase (less attracted to the stationary phase) spends more time moving and travels further.
  • A substance more strongly attracted to the stationary phase travels a shorter distance.
  • A pure substance gives one spot (in every solvent tried); a mixture gives two or more spots.
Rf = distance moved by the substance (spot) ÷ distance moved by the solvent front
both measured from the baseline; Rf has no units and is always between 0 and 1
A spot moves 3.6 cm while the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. Find Rf, and identify it using reference values: caffeine 0.45, glucose 0.30 (illustrative values for this solvent).
Rf = 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45 → matches caffeine. To be confident, run the reference and the unknown side by side on the same paper, and repeat in a second solvent: they should match in both.

Choosing a chromatography method (PAG4)

  • Paper chromatography — spots placed on a pencil baseline (ink would itself run), baseline above the solvent level (otherwise the sample dissolves into the solvent in the beaker), lid on to keep the atmosphere saturated with solvent.
  • Aqueous and non-aqueous solvents — water works for water-soluble dyes (e.g. food colourings). Substances insoluble in water (e.g. permanent-marker inks, chlorophyll) need a non-aqueous solvent such as ethanol or propanone. Rf values depend on the solvent, so a reference Rf is only valid for the same solvent.
  • Locating agents — colourless substances (e.g. amino acids, sugars) do not show up. After drying, the chromatogram is sprayed with a locating agent (e.g. ninhydrin for amino acids) or put under UV light, which makes the spots visible.
  • Distinguishing pure from impure — a pure substance gives one spot that matches the reference; extra spots reveal impurities. Using two different solvents gives stronger evidence, because two substances could by chance have the same Rf in one solvent.

Separation techniques (PAG3, PAG7)

Separation methods exploit a difference in a property of the components: state, solubility in a solvent, or boiling point. Reactions in the lab and industry often give the wanted product mixed with by-products, waste and unreacted reactants, so separation is part of almost every process.

TechniqueSeparates…Property usedExample
FiltrationAn insoluble solid from a liquid/solutionSolid particles too big to pass through filter paperSand from salty water; excess copper(II) oxide from copper sulfate solution
CrystallisationA dissolved solid (solute) from its solutionSolute less soluble when the solution is concentrated and cooledCopper sulfate crystals from solution

Distillation

Simple distillation: the solution is heated; the liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates, the vapour passes into a water-cooled condenser, condenses and is collected as the distillate. Dissolved solids stay in the flask. The thermometer bulb is level with the side arm so it reads the temperature of the vapour leaving — a steady reading at the known boiling point shows a pure liquid is distilling.

Fractional distillation separates mixtures of liquids whose boiling points are close. A fractionating column packed with glass beads sits between the flask and the condenser. The column is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top. Vapours condense and re-evaporate many times on the beads; the liquid with the lowest boiling point reaches the top first and distils over while the thermometer reading stays steady. When the temperature rises to the next boiling point, the receiver is changed to collect the next fraction. Crude oil is separated this way on an industrial scale (C3.4).

Suggesting a purification technique

  • Insoluble solid + liquid → filtration.
  • Want the dissolved solid → crystallisation (evaporating to dryness can decompose some salts, e.g. hydrated salts lose water).
  • Want the solvent from a solution → simple distillation.
  • Two or more miscible liquids → fractional distillation.
  • Two solids, one soluble in a solvent → dissolve, filter, then crystallise the filtrate.
  • Analysing or separating tiny amounts of coloured/soluble substances → chromatography.

Separations are rarely completely successful: products often go through several stages, or the same process repeatedly (e.g. recrystallising), to reach an acceptable purity. Purity is then checked by melting point or chromatography.

For "suggest a method" questions, name the technique and give the property it relies on, using the data you are given: "fractional distillation, because the boiling points (78 °C and 100 °C) are different".

C5.2 How do chemists find the composition of unknown samples?

Representative sampling

The small sample analysed must represent the whole bulk of the material (a soil field, a batch of product, a river). Chemists take several samples from different places/times and mix or analyse them separately so that variations in the bulk are detected and the result is not biased by one unusual spot. Ideas about Science (IaS1): plan the equipment, techniques and strategy for a qualitative analysis — e.g. sample, flame test for the cation, then add reagents to test for anions.

Flame tests (PAG5)

Technique: clean a nichrome (or platinum) wire loop by dipping it in dilute hydrochloric acid and holding it in a hot (blue, roaring) Bunsen flame until it gives no colour. Dip the loop into the solid sample (or solution), hold it at the edge of the hot blue flame and observe the colour. Clean the loop between samples to avoid contamination.

Limitation: a mixture of ions can mask colours (sodium's strong yellow hides others), and small amounts may not be seen.

Precipitation tests for metal ions with dilute sodium hydroxide (PAG5)

Add a few drops of dilute NaOH(aq) to a solution of the sample, then add excess. Insoluble metal hydroxides precipitate.

Why acidify first? The acid removes carbonate ions, which would otherwise also form a precipitate and give a false positive. Use nitric acid (not hydrochloric) before silver nitrate, because HCl adds chloride ions; never use sulfuric acid before the barium test, because it adds sulfate ions.

Instrumental analysis: emission spectroscopy

A sample is heated strongly (e.g. in a flame or plasma) so its atoms emit light. The light is split into a line spectrum. Each element gives its own unique pattern of lines at particular wavelengths, like a fingerprint. Matching the lines of an unknown to reference spectra identifies the elements present. A mixture shows all the lines of every element in it. Uses: identifying elements in stars and checking the composition of alloys such as steel.

Instrumental vs laboratory (wet) methods

Instrumental methods (e.g. emission spectroscopy, gas chromatography)Standard laboratory tests (flame tests, precipitates)
Sensitive — detect tiny amounts of a substanceNeed larger samples; faint results easily missed
Accurate — results match the true value; can identify substances in mixtures and measure how muchRely on judging colours by eye; mixtures can mask each other
Rapid — fast, automated, many samples; data recorded automaticallySlower, one test at a time
Expensive, need trained operators, not widely availableCheap glassware and reagents, available in any lab
When interpreting a spectrum or chart (M4a), go line by line: say which reference lines match which lines in the unknown, then conclude. A single unmatched line means another element is present.

C5.3 How are the amounts of substances in reactions calculated?

Conservation of mass

Law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction no atoms are created or destroyed — they are only rearranged. So the total mass of products = total mass of reactants.

In a non-enclosed (open) system the mass can appear to change, because a gas can enter or leave:

  • Mass decreases when a gas product escapes, e.g. CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g). The CO₂ particles leave the flask into the air.
  • Mass increases when a reactant comes from the air, e.g. burning magnesium: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s). Oxygen atoms from the air are now combined in the solid.

In a sealed container the mass stays exactly the same, because all the particles are still there.

Relative formula mass, Mr

Add the relative atomic masses (Ar, from the Periodic Table) of all the atoms in the formula.

Mr of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100.
Mr of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74.
In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: mass of reactants = 2 × 2 + 32 = 36; mass of products = 2 × 18 = 36 — mass is conserved.

The mole and the Avogadro constant (HT)

The Avogadro constant is 6.02 × 10²³ per mole. One mole of a substance contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules, ions or formula units). The mass of one mole is its Mr in grams. (OCR accepts either definition: the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12, or exactly 6.02 × 10²³ particles.)
number of moles = mass of substance (g) ÷ relative formula mass
rearranged: mass = moles × Mr     number of particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³
(HT) How many molecules are in 9.0 g of water (Mr 18)? How many atoms?
moles = 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.50 mol. Molecules = 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³. Each molecule has 3 atoms, so atoms = 9.03 × 10²³.

Deducing an equation from reacting masses (HT)

(HT) 4.8 g of magnesium reacts with 3.2 g of oxygen, O₂, to give 8.0 g of magnesium oxide. Deduce the balanced equation (Ar: Mg 24, O 16).
Mg: 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.20 mol; O₂: 3.2 ÷ 32 = 0.10 mol; MgO: 8.0 ÷ 40 = 0.20 mol.
Divide by the smallest (0.10): ratio 2 : 1 : 2 → 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.

Limiting reactants (HT)

The limiting reactant is the one that is completely used up first; it stops the reaction and fixes the amount of product. The other reactant is in excess (some is left over). Doubling the limiting reactant doubles the product; adding more of the excess reactant makes no difference.

(HT) 2.4 g Mg is added to 0.10 mol HCl. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Which is limiting?
Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol, which needs 0.20 mol HCl. Only 0.10 mol HCl is present, so HCl is limiting and Mg is in excess. H₂ formed = 0.10 ÷ 2 = 0.050 mol.

Reacting-mass calculations (HT)

Steps: (1) moles of the known substance = mass ÷ Mr; (2) use the ratio in the balanced equation; (3) mass of the unknown = moles × Mr. This lets industry use reactants in the correct proportions so processes run efficiently with little waste.

(HT) What mass of calcium oxide is made by heating 50 g of calcium carbonate? CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
moles CaCO₃ = 50 ÷ 100 = 0.50 mol; ratio 1 : 1 so 0.50 mol CaO; mass = 0.50 × 56 = 28 g.

Theoretical yield, actual yield and percentage yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product calculated from the equation. The actual yield is the mass you really obtain.

percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
In the example above only 22.4 g of CaO was collected. Percentage yield = 22.4 ÷ 28 × 100 = 80%.

Without moles (all tiers) you can scale masses by ratio: if 100 g CaCO₃ gives 56 g CaO, then 50 g gives 56 × 50/100 = 28 g.

Reasons for low yields:

  • The reaction is reversible and does not go to completion (C6.3).
  • Impure reactants — less of the actual reactant than the mass suggests.
  • Product lost during the procedure: left on filter paper or in glassware, lost when transferring, some stays dissolved in the solution during crystallisation.
  • Side reactions make unwanted products.

Ideas about Science (IaS1, IaS2): use data to make quantitative predictions of yield, then compare them with actual yields — e.g. preparing a salt or an organic compound and comparing the mass made with the theoretical yield, or predicting the volume of gas made when an acid reacts and testing it. Information about actual yields is used to improve the procedure.

Moles and gas volumes (HT)

One mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) at room temperature and pressure (RTP).
number of moles of gas = volume of gas (dm³) ÷ 24 dm³    volume = moles × 24
  • For reactions where everything is a gas, the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the equation: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g), so 30 dm³ of H₂ makes 20 dm³ of NH₃ (with enough N₂).
  • For mixed states, combine mass and volume steps.
(HT) What volume of CO₂ (at RTP) is made when 10 g of CaCO₃ reacts with excess hydrochloric acid?
CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. moles CaCO₃ = 10 ÷ 100 = 0.10 mol → 0.10 mol CO₂ → volume = 0.10 × 24 = 2.4 dm³ (2400 cm³).
Rearranging formulae (M3b/M3c) is examined directly. Write the formula, rearrange, substitute with units, and give the answer to a sensible number of significant figures. Use standard form for Avogadro answers (e.g. 3.01 × 10²³, not 301 000 000 000 000 000 000 000).

C5.4 How are the amounts of chemicals in solution measured?

Qualitative vs quantitative analysis

Qualitative analysisQuantitative analysis
Identifies which substances/ions are present (e.g. flame tests, precipitate tests, chromatography to identify a dye)Measures how much of a substance is present (e.g. titration to find a concentration, measuring a mass or a gas volume)

Concentration of solutions (HT)

concentration (g/dm³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
concentration (mol/dm³) = number of moles of solute ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so volume in dm³ = volume in cm³ ÷ 1000

More solute in the same volume, or the same solute in a smaller volume, gives a higher concentration. Chemists usually prefer mol/dm³ because it links directly to the reacting ratios in the equation. To convert: concentration in mol/dm³ = concentration in g/dm³ ÷ Mr.

(HT) 4.0 g of sodium hydroxide (Mr 40) is dissolved to make 250 cm³ of solution.
Volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³. Concentration = 4.0 ÷ 0.250 = 16 g/dm³.
Moles = 4.0 ÷ 40 = 0.10 mol, so concentration = 0.10 ÷ 0.250 = 0.40 mol/dm³ (check: 16 ÷ 40 = 0.40).

Making a standard solution (a solution of accurately known concentration): weigh the solid accurately, dissolve it in a beaker of distilled water, transfer to a volumetric flask with rinsings, make up to the graduation mark with distilled water (bottom of meniscus on the line), stopper and invert to mix.

Neutralisation

Acids form hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), when they dissolve in water. Solutions of alkalis (soluble bases) contain hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq).

Neutralisation: acid + alkali → salt + water. Every aqueous neutralisation can be generalised as the ionic equation H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
Acid + alkaliBalanced equationSalt
Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxideHCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂Osodium chloride
Nitric acid + potassium hydroxideHNO₃ + KOH → KNO₃ + H₂Opotassium nitrate
Sulfuric acid + sodium hydroxideH₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂Osodium sulfate
Hydrochloric acid + calcium hydroxide2HCl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂Ocalcium chloride
Nitric acid + calcium hydroxide2HNO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂Ocalcium nitrate

Titration procedure (PAG6)

  1. Rinse the pipette with the alkali and use a pipette filler to transfer exactly 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask.
  2. Add a few drops of a suitable indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein: pink in alkali, colourless in acid; or methyl orange: yellow in alkali, red in acid). Universal indicator is not used — its gradual colour change gives no sharp end point.
  3. Rinse the burette with the acid, fill it (using a funnel, then remove the funnel) and make sure the jet below the tap is full. Record the starting reading.
  4. Place the flask on a white tile. Add acid, swirling, until the indicator just changes colour (the end point). This first, quick run is the rough titration.
  5. Repeat accurately: add acid quickly to within about 1 cm³ of the rough value, then drop by drop, swirling, until one drop changes the colour permanently.
  6. Repeat until you have concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other). Calculate the mean of the concordant titres, ignoring the rough.

Evaluating titration data

Run (illustrative data)Rough123
Final reading / cm³25.4024.7049.2524.65
Initial reading / cm³0.000.0024.600.00
Titre / cm³25.4024.7024.6524.65

The rough is discarded. Titres 1–3 have a range of 24.70 − 24.65 = 0.05 cm³, so they are concordant. Mean = (24.70 + 24.65 + 24.65) ÷ 3 = 24.67 cm³.

  • Precise / repeatable: repeat titres close together (small range).
  • Accurate: close to the true value — achieved with calibrated burette and pipette, standard solution of known concentration, reading the meniscus at eye level and adding dropwise at the end point.
  • Valid: the method measures what it claims to — same volume of alkali each time, same indicator, only the acid volume varies.
  • Risk: wear eye protection (acids and alkalis are irritant/corrosive); use a pipette filler, never mouth-pipette; fill the burette below eye level.

Ideas about Science (IaS1, IaS2): justify each step of the technique in terms of the precision, accuracy and validity of the data and minimising risk; use the range and mean to process titration results.

Titration calculations (HT)

The volume of a solution of known concentration that reacts exactly with another substance lets you find the other's concentration (or volume) using the reacting ratio:

  1. moles of the known solution = concentration × volume (dm³)
  2. use the equation ratio to find moles of the unknown
  3. concentration of the unknown = moles ÷ volume (dm³)
(HT) 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ NaOH is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid. Find the concentration of the acid.
moles NaOH = 0.100 × 25.0/1000 = 0.00250 mol. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, ratio 1 : 1 → 0.00250 mol HCl.
[HCl] = 0.00250 ÷ (20.0/1000) = 0.125 mol/dm³.
(HT) 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol/dm³ NaOH reacts exactly with 25.0 cm³ of sulfuric acid. H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O.
moles NaOH = 0.200 × 0.0250 = 0.00500 mol; ratio 1 H₂SO₄ : 2 NaOH → 0.00250 mol H₂SO₄.
[H₂SO₄] = 0.00250 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.100 mol/dm³.
Always convert cm³ to dm³ (÷ 1000) before using mol/dm³, and check the ratio in the equation — sulfuric acid is the classic trap because it reacts 1 : 2 with NaOH.

C5 Summary & Key Equations

  • Pure (science) = one substance only; pure substances have sharp melting points.
  • Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front
  • Filtration, crystallisation, simple and fractional distillation each exploit a different property.
  • Flame tests: Li crimson, Na yellow, K lilac, Ca orange-red, Cu green.
  • NaOH: Ca²⁺ white, Zn²⁺ white (dissolves in excess), Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ orange-brown.
  • Ag⁺ test: Cl⁻ white, Br⁻ cream, I⁻ yellow; Ba²⁺ test: SO₄²⁻ white; carbonate fizzes, CO₂ turns limewater milky.
  • (HT) moles = mass ÷ Mr; particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³; gas moles = volume (dm³) ÷ 24
  • percentage yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100
  • (HT) concentration (g/dm³) = mass ÷ volume; concentration (mol/dm³) = moles ÷ volume
  • Neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
In a 6-mark titration question, cover the apparatus (burette, pipette, indicator, tile), the method (rough, dropwise near end point, concordant repeats) and the processing (mean of concordant results) — each strand earns marks.
