C3.1 How Are the Atoms Held Together in a Metal?

Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a ‘sea’ of delocalised electrons (outer-shell electrons that are free to move through the whole structure).

In the model of metal structure, the metal atoms are packed closely together in a regular giant structure (a lattice). Each atom has lost its outer-shell electron(s), which become delocalised. The attraction acts in all directions, so the bonding holds the whole giant structure together — there are no separate molecules.

Bulk Properties Explained by the Model

PropertyExplanation using metallic bonding
High melting and boiling pointsStrong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons; a lot of energy is needed to overcome it.
Good electrical conductorsDelocalised electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge when a potential difference is applied.
Good thermal conductorsFree electrons carry energy quickly through the lattice (and the closely packed ions pass on vibrations).
Malleable (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (drawn into wires)Layers of ions slide over each other; the bonding is non-directional, so the electrons keep holding the ions together in their new positions.
Usually strong and denseIons are packed closely together and strongly bonded.

These properties make metals useful: copper for electrical wiring (conductor, ductile), aluminium and steel for pans (thermal conductor, high melting point), steel for car bodies (strong, malleable). Uses of metals are one of the spec’s examples of science improving lives (IaS4).

Ideas about Science (IaS3) — using a model. Chemists use this model to explain metal properties, but it has limits: real ions are not hard balls, the diagram is 2D while the lattice is 3D, electrons are not really fixed dots, and the simple model does not explain why, for example, iron has a much higher melting point than sodium. When asked to “use the model”, always mention ions, delocalised electrons and the attraction between them.

C3.2 How Are Metals with Different Reactivities Extracted?

Finding an Order of Reactivity

Metals can be put in order of reactivity using experimental results from three types of reaction:

  • With water — metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen (with steam: metal oxide + hydrogen).
  • With dilute acid — metal + acid → salt + hydrogen. The faster the fizzing (or the bigger the temperature rise), the more reactive the metal.
  • Displacement — a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its compound (or from its oxide when heated).
MetalCold waterDilute acid

Sodium, NaVigorous; melts into a ball and fizzes around

Magnesium, MgVery slow (reacts well with steam)Vigorous fizzing
Aluminium, AlNo visible reaction (protective oxide layer)Slow at first, then steady
Zinc, ZnNo reaction (reacts with steam)Steady fizzing

Copper, Cu / Silver, AgNo reactionNo reaction (below hydrogen)

Practical work: investigate the reactivity of metals with water and dilute acid (compare fizzing or temperature rise using the same mass/surface area of metal and the same volume and concentration of acid); and heat zinc, iron and copper with the oxides of the other two. For example, zinc powder heated with copper(II) oxide glows and leaves red-brown copper, but copper heated with zinc oxide shows no change. These activities can contribute to PAG1 (Reactivity trend).

zinc + copper(II) oxide → zinc oxide + copper
Zn(s) + CuO(s) → ZnO(s) + Cu(s)

Reactivity and Forming Positive Ions

When metals react they form ionic compounds: the metal atoms lose one or more outer electrons to become positive ions (e.g. Na → Na⁺ + e⁻; Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻). The more easily a metal forms its positive ion, the more reactive it is. Potassium loses its single outer electron very easily, so it reacts violently with water; copper and silver hold on to their electrons, so they do not react with water or dilute acids. Metals below hydrogen cannot displace hydrogen from water or acids.

Writing Formulae and Balanced Equations

Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a reaction (conservation of mass), so a balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides. Balance by changing the large numbers in front of formulae, never the small subscripts. Include state symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq).

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)
Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Ionic Equations (HT)

(HT) An ionic equation shows only the ions (and atoms) that change in the reaction, leaving out spectator ions that stay the same. Ionic equations are useful for displacement reactions because they show what happens to the metal ions — the more reactive metal atoms lose electrons and the less reactive metal ions gain them.

(HT) Write the ionic equation for zinc reacting with copper(II) sulfate solution.
Full equation: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + Cu(s)
Sulfate ions are unchanged (spectators) → cross them out:
Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
Zinc atoms each lose 2 electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻); copper ions each gain 2 electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu). Check: charges balance (2+ on each side).

(HT) Similarly, a metal with an acid: Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g) (chloride ions are spectators).

Extracting Metals: Where Does Carbon Fit?

Most metals are found in ores as compounds (often oxides or sulfides). How a metal is extracted depends on its reactivity. Carbon is a non-metal but can be placed in the reactivity series between aluminium and zinc.

  • Metals below carbon (zinc, iron, lead, copper) are extracted by heating the ore with carbon. Carbon displaces the metal: the metal compound is reduced (loses oxygen) and carbon is oxidised (gains oxygen).
  • Metals above carbon (potassium to aluminium) hold oxygen more strongly than carbon does, so carbon cannot reduce them. They are extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound (C3.3), which needs large amounts of electricity and so is expensive.
Extraction of zinc. The main ore, zinc blende, is zinc sulfide.
1. Roast the ore in air to make zinc oxide: 2ZnS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO₂(g)
2. Heat the zinc oxide with carbon (coke) in a furnace: ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(g) + CO(g)
The furnace is hotter than zinc’s boiling point (907 °C), so zinc forms as a vapour that is condensed and collected. Zinc oxide is reduced; carbon is oxidised. The sulfur dioxide from roasting must be captured (it causes acid rain) — it is usually turned into sulfuric acid.

Iron is extracted in the same way in a blast furnace, where carbon monoxide made from coke does the reducing: Fe₂O₃(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(l) + 3CO₂(g). (Much of today’s zinc is in fact made by electrolysing zinc sulfate solution, but the spec expects you to explain the carbon-reduction method.)

Biological Methods of Extraction (HT)

(HT) Rich ores are running out. Scientists are developing ways to extract the less reactive metals (such as copper) from low-grade ores and waste material (e.g. old mine spoil heaps) using living organisms.

Method (HT)How it works
Bacterial extraction (bioleaching)Bacteria feed on low-grade ore and produce a solution (leachate) containing metal ions such as Cu²⁺. The metal is then recovered from the solution by displacement with scrap iron or by electrolysis.
Phytoextraction (phytomining)Plants are grown on soil containing low concentrations of the metal; they absorb metal ions through their roots. The plants are harvested and burned; the ash contains metal compounds, from which the metal is extracted (e.g. by electrolysis or displacement).

(HT) Evaluating these methods — weigh the benefits against the drawbacks:

  • For: can use low-grade ores and waste, reducing the need to mine ‘new’ ores (less landscape damage, less waste rock); lower energy costs than smelting; less sulfur dioxide and CO₂; removes toxic metals from land and so reduces the amount of toxic metals in landfill.
  • Against: very slow (phytoextraction takes whole growing seasons) and produces only small quantities of metal; bioleaching solutions can be toxic and acidic and must not escape into groundwater; the metal still has to be extracted from the leachate or ash.
Ideas about Science (IaS4). Metal extraction has environmental impacts — quarrying and mining scar the landscape, smelting uses lots of energy and releases CO₂ and SO₂, waste heaps can leach toxic metals. Scientists mitigate these by recycling, capturing gases and developing biological methods. In “evaluate” questions, compare the risks, costs and benefits of each course of action and finish with a justified judgement.

C3.3 What Are Electrolytes and What Happens During Electrolysis?

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte by an electric current. An electrolyte is an ionic compound that is molten or dissolved in water, so its ions are free to move.
  • Solid ionic compounds are not electrolytes: their ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice.
  • Covalent substances such as sugar solution or ethanol do not conduct — they contain no ions.
  • The positive electrode is the anode; the negative electrode is the cathode. Inert electrodes (graphite or platinum) do not react themselves.
  • Positive ions (metal ions or H⁺) are attracted to the cathode, where they gain electrons to become neutral atoms. Negative non-metal ions are attracted to the anode, where they lose electrons.

Practical work: to investigate which substances are electrolytes, dip two electrodes connected in series with a cell and a bulb (or ammeter) into each substance — solid and dissolved salt, sugar solution, ethanol, molten wax — and look for current and for products at the electrodes.

Rule (inert electrodes): metals (or hydrogen) are formed at the cathode; non-metals are formed at the anode.

Molten Binary Ionic Compounds

A binary compound contains just two elements. When molten, the only ions present are the metal ion and the non-metal ion, so the products are simply the two elements:

Sodium chloride, NaClSodiumChlorine

Half Equations, Oxidation and Reduction (HT)

(HT) A half equation shows what happens at one electrode, using e⁻ for electrons. Atoms and charges must both balance (conservation of mass).

(HT) Cathode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb     Anode: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻
(HT) Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons (“OIL RIG”). In electrolysis, reduction happens at the cathode (positive ions gain electrons) and oxidation happens at the anode (negative ions lose electrons). So in molten lead bromide, Pb²⁺ ions are reduced and Br⁻ ions are oxidised.

Extracting Aluminium by Electrolysis

Aluminium is above carbon, so it is extracted by electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide (from the ore bauxite). The oxide melts above 2000 °C, so it is dissolved in molten cryolite, which lets the cell run at about 950 °C — still very hot.

  • At the cathode: aluminium ions gain electrons to form aluminium atoms; molten aluminium sinks and is tapped off.
  • At the anode: oxide ions lose electrons to form oxygen molecules. The hot oxygen reacts with the carbon anodes to form carbon dioxide (C + O₂ → CO₂), so the anodes burn away and must be replaced regularly.
  • The process uses a large amount of energy: to keep the electrolyte molten and for the electric current itself. This is why aluminium plants are often sited near cheap hydroelectric power, and why recycling aluminium is so worthwhile.
Overall: 2Al₂O₃(l) → 4Al(l) + 3O₂(g)
(HT) Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (reduction)    Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (oxidation)

Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions — Competing Reactions

Some extraction methods (for example recovering metals from waste heaps) give a dilute aqueous solution of metal ions. In any aqueous solution, water also provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions, so two kinds of ion compete at each electrode:

  • Cathode (−): the less reactive of the metal and hydrogen is discharged. Less reactive metals such as copper and silver are deposited. If the metal is more reactive than hydrogen (e.g. sodium, potassium), hydrogen gas forms instead.
  • Anode (+): oxygen usually forms (from hydroxide ions), unless the solution contains a concentrated halide — a concentrated chloride solution gives chlorine. Sulfate and nitrate ions are never discharged.

(HT) Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂    or    Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
(HT) Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻    or    4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻

Technique: Electrolysis of an Aqueous Salt Solution (PAG2)

  1. Pour the salt solution into the cell; fill two small test tubes with the solution and invert one over each inert (graphite) electrode.
  2. Connect the electrodes to a low-voltage d.c. supply (e.g. 4–6 V) and switch on.
  3. Observe each electrode; collect the gases and test them:
    • Hydrogen — burns with a squeaky pop with a lighted splint.
    • Oxygen — relights a glowing splint.
    • Chlorine — turns damp blue litmus paper red and then bleaches it white.
    • A metal deposit (e.g. pink-brown copper from CuSO₄) shows as a coating on the cathode.

Practical work: investigate how the concentration of the sodium chloride solution, the current and the voltage affect the products — e.g. measure the volume of gas at each electrode in a fixed time, and test whether chlorine or oxygen forms as the solution gets more dilute. Change one variable at a time and control the others (electrode size and spacing, time, temperature). Safety: chlorine is toxic — use low voltages, short run times and a well-ventilated room; wear eye protection. This is PAG2 (Electrolysis).

Name the electrodes by charge as well as name: “cathode (negative)”. A common slip is saying sodium forms at the cathode in aqueous sodium chloride — it doesn’t; sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen forms.

C3.4 Why Is Crude Oil Important as a Source of New Materials?

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons (compounds of hydrogen and carbon only), formed over millions of years from the remains of tiny sea organisms. It is the main source of hydrocarbons, used both as fuels and as a feedstock (raw material) for the petrochemical industry.

Modern Life Depends on Hydrocarbons

Fuels from crude oil power almost all transport (petrol, diesel, kerosene for jets) and much heating and electricity generation. As a feedstock, crude oil is made into polymers (plastics and synthetic fibres), solvents, detergents, lubricants, dyes, cosmetics and medicines. Almost every consumer product uses crude oil in its manufacture or its transport.

Crude oil is a finite (non-renewable) resource: it formed over millions of years and is being used far faster than it forms. At the current rate it will run out, so our present use is not sustainable. Most crude oil is burned as fuel; only a small fraction is used as feedstock.

Ideas about Science. Decisions about crude oil (IaS3 in the spec, linked to IaS4 sustainability) must balance short-term benefits (cheap fuel, jobs, convenience) against conserving a finite resource for the future — especially as the only source of feedstock for many chemicals. A strong answer considers different groups (consumers, industry, future generations) and suggests actions such as using alternative fuels for energy and keeping oil for making materials.

Fractional Distillation

Crude oil is separated into fractions — groups of hydrocarbons with similar-sized molecules and a narrow range of boiling points — by fractional distillation. It works because hydrocarbons have different boiling points, which depend on the size of the molecules and the intermolecular forces between them.

  1. Crude oil is heated in a furnace to about 350 °C so that most of it evaporates.
  2. The vapour enters near the bottom of a tall fractionating column, which is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top.
  3. Vapours rise. Each hydrocarbon condenses when it reaches a level where the temperature is below its boiling point, and is collected on trays at that level.
  4. Small molecules (low boiling points) rise to the top and leave as gases; large molecules (high boiling points) condense near the bottom. The heaviest part (bitumen) never evaporates and is drained from the base.

The fractions are still mixtures, mainly of alkanes (general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂). Going down the column, molecules get larger, so boiling point and viscosity increase while volatility and flammability decrease. In the lab, a small sample of ‘synthetic crude’ can be separated by fractional distillation, collecting fractions over set temperature ranges — this links to PAG3 (Separation techniques) and PAG4 (Distillation), for which the spec suggests distilling hydrocarbons. In the lab set-up the thermometer bulb sits level with the side arm and cooling water enters the condenser at the bottom.

Alkanes: a Homologous Series

A homologous series is a family of compounds that have the same general formula, in which each member differs from the next by CH₂, which have similar chemical properties and show a trend in physical properties.

PropaneC₃H₈−42gas

Alkanes are saturated (only C–C single bonds) and fairly unreactive, apart from burning.

Changes of State: Bonds versus Intermolecular Forces

Within a hydrocarbon molecule the atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds. Between molecules there are only weak intermolecular forces. When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, energy is transferred to the molecules so they move apart: only the intermolecular forces are overcome; no covalent bonds are broken. That takes little energy, so the melting and boiling points are low.

Bigger molecules have more contact with each other, so the total intermolecular forces are stronger; more energy is needed to separate them, so the boiling point is higher. This explains the trend in the alkanes table and the order of fractions in the column.

The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element.

Covalent Bonding in Simple Molecules

A covalent bond is a strong bond between two atoms formed by a shared pair of electrons. The two nuclei are both attracted to the shared pair. Covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms; sharing lets each atom reach a full outer shell.

A simple molecule contains a small, fixed number of atoms joined by covalent bonds (e.g. H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, Cl₂, and the small molecules met in C1 such as O₂, N₂, SO₂). Each bond is directional — it joins two particular atoms — so molecules have definite shapes.

Dot and Cross Diagrams

Draw the outer shells as overlapping circles; show one atom’s electrons as dots and the other’s as crosses; put each shared pair in the overlap. Hydrogen ends up with 2 electrons; the other atoms with 8.

Representing Molecules in 2D and 3D

Molecules can be shown as a molecular formula (C₂H₆), an empirical formula (CH₃), a displayed formula (every atom and every bond drawn as a line), a dot and cross diagram, or a 3D ball and stick or space-filling model. You need to translate between them, e.g. count atoms in a model to write a formula, or turn a 3D model into a flat displayed formula.

RepresentationUseful because…Limitations
Dot and crossShows where every outer electron is, which atom it came from, shared and lone pairsElectrons are actually identical; doesn’t show shape or bond angles; electrons are not fixed in position
Displayed formulaShows every atom and bond; easy to draw and countFlat (2D) — wrong angles; no lone pairs; no relative atom sizes

Ideas about Science (IaS3). No model is ‘right’; each is chosen for what it shows. None of these models shows the intermolecular forces between molecules, which are what decide melting and boiling points.

Properties of Simple Molecular Substances

  • Low melting and boiling points — only weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome; the strong covalent bonds inside the molecules are not broken. Many are gases or liquids at room temperature.
  • Do not conduct electricity — the molecules have no overall charge and there are no free electrons or ions.
  • Larger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → higher melting and boiling points.

Cracking

Fractional distillation gives more of the long-chain fractions than are needed, and not enough of the short-chain ones such as petrol, which are in great demand as fuels. Cracking breaks long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes.

  • The long-chain alkanes are heated to vaporise them and passed over a hot catalyst (e.g. aluminium oxide or silica), or mixed with steam at a very high temperature.
  • Alkenes are hydrocarbons with a C=C double bond. They are much more reactive than alkanes and are the starting materials for polymers and many other chemicals.
decane → octane + ethene
C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄

Test the gas with bromine water: alkenes turn it from orange to colourless; alkanes leave it orange.

Ideas about Science (IaS4). Cracking is a positive application of science: by matching supply to demand it means less crude oil has to be extracted for the same amount of petrol, so it helps conserve oil reserves and avoids wasting long-chain alkanes that are less useful.

Functional Groups and Homologous Series

A functional group is the atom or group of atoms that gives a homologous series its characteristic reactions. Compounds with the same functional group react in the same way.
SeriesFunctional groupGeneral formulaKey properties and uses
Alkanesnone (C–C and C–H single bonds only)CₙH₂ₙ₊₂Unreactive apart from burning; fuels
AlkenesC=C double bondCₙH₂ₙAddition reactions across the double bond; decolourise bromine water; make polymers
Alcohols–OH (one H of an alkane replaced by OH)CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OHBurn as fuels; solvents that are miscible with water; oxidised to carboxylic acids
Carboxylic acids–COOHCₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOHWeak acids (pH about 3–4); react with carbonates to give CO₂; in foods (vinegar is ethanoic acid, citric acid) and personal care products
Numbering matters for longer chains: but-1-ene has the C=C between carbons 1 and 2; propan-1-ol has the –OH on carbon 1. In a carboxylic acid, the C of the –COOH group counts in the name (ethanoic acid has 2 carbons). When drawing, check every C has exactly 4 bonds, every H 1 and every O 2.

Reactions of Alkanes, Alkenes and Alcohols

Combustion. Alkanes, alkenes and alcohols burn in plenty of air (complete combustion) to give carbon dioxide and water:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O
C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Addition across the double bond (alkenes). The C=C opens and a molecule adds on, one atom to each carbon. There is only one product.

ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane    C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂  (bromine water decolourised)
ethene + hydrogen → ethane    C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆  (nickel catalyst)
ethene + steam → ethanol    C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH  (acid catalyst)

Alkenes also add to each other to make addition polymers (C4.2).

Oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids. An oxidising agent (e.g. acidified potassium dichromate, or microbes in air when wine turns to vinegar) converts an alcohol into the carboxylic acid with the same number of carbon atoms:

ethanol + oxygen → ethanoic acid + water
C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CH₃COOH + H₂O

Predicting Products: the Generality of Functional Group Reactions

It is the functional group that decides how an organic compound reacts, and every member of a homologous series has the same functional group. So once you know one reaction, you can predict it for any member — including ones you have never met:

Predict the products for: (a) pentane burning completely; (b) propene + bromine; (c) oxidising butan-1-ol.
(a) C₅H₁₂ + 8O₂ → 5CO₂ + 6H₂O (check O: 16 = 10 + 6)
(b) C₃H₆ + Br₂ → C₃H₆Br₂ (1,2-dibromopropane: a Br on each carbon of the former C=C)
(c) C₄H₉OH → C₃H₇COOH, butanoic acid (same number of carbons; the end CH₂OH becomes COOH)

Practical work: investigate the reactions of alkanes, alkenes and alcohols — e.g. compare how they burn, shake each with bromine water, and test alcohols and their oxidation products with universal indicator.

Ideas about Science (IaS3). Chemists use models of functional groups to represent and predict the reactions of whole homologous series. A model like this is powerful because it lets you make predictions you can test.

C3 Summary & Key Equations

  • Metallic bonding: positive ions + delocalised electrons → conductors, malleable, high melting point
  • Reactivity: K Na Ca Mg Al [C] Zn Fe Pb [H] Cu Ag Au — more reactive = forms positive ions more easily
  • Below carbon → reduce with carbon (ZnO + C → Zn + CO); above carbon → electrolysis of molten compound
  • Electrolysis: cations to cathode (gain e⁻, reduced); anions to anode (lose e⁻, oxidised)
  • Aluminium: Al₂O₃ in molten cryolite; Al at cathode; O₂ at anode burns carbon anodes to CO₂
  • Aqueous: less reactive of metal/H at cathode; O₂ at anode unless concentrated halide (Cl₂)
  • Crude oil: finite mixture of hydrocarbons; fractional distillation by boiling point; alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
  • Boiling only overcomes intermolecular forces, never covalent bonds
  • Cracking: long alkane → shorter alkane + alkene; alkenes decolourise bromine water
  • Functional groups: C=C, –OH, –COOH decide reactions for the whole series
In “explain the boiling point” questions, the words intermolecular forces must appear. Saying “covalent bonds break when it boils” loses the mark every time.
