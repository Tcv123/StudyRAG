C6 — Making Useful Chemicals

OCR GCSE Chemistry B (Twenty First Century Science, J258) — All papers (J258/01–04, Breadth and Depth in chemistry, Foundation and Higher) · (HT) = Higher tier only

This chapter is about making useful chemicals, from the laboratory bench to the chemical plant: salts from acids (C6.1), controlling how fast reactions go (C6.2), reversible reactions and yield (C6.3), and how fertilisers and other bulk chemicals are made on an industrial scale (C6.4).

C6.1 What useful products can be made from acids?

Acids are used to make cleaning products, medicines, food additives and, on a huge scale, fertilisers. Their reactions all produce salts. The salt's name comes from the acid: hydrochloric acid → chlorides, nitric acid → nitrates, sulfuric acid → sulfates.

Making salts in the laboratory (PAG7)

A soluble salt from an insoluble base or carbonate (e.g. copper(II) sulfate crystals from copper(II) oxide and sulfuric acid):

  1. Warm the dilute acid in a beaker (speeds up the reaction).
  2. Add the insoluble solid a little at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves — the solid is in excess, so all the acid has reacted.
  3. Filter to remove the excess solid; the filtrate is the salt solution.
  4. Evaporate the filtrate gently in an evaporating basin until crystals start to form (point of crystallisation), then leave to cool so the salt crystallises.
  5. Filter off the crystals, rinse with a little cold distilled water and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven.

A soluble salt from an alkali (e.g. sodium chloride): both reactants are solutions, so excess cannot be filtered off. Do a titration with indicator to find the exact volumes, then repeat with the same volumes and no indicator, and crystallise.

An insoluble salt (e.g. barium sulfate): mix two solutions that contain its ions (precipitation), filter, wash the precipitate on the filter paper with distilled water and dry it.

Measuring pH

pH measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. Use universal indicator (colour compared with a chart; quick and cheap but subjective and to whole numbers only) or a pH meter (probe; more precise, reads to decimals, records data, but needs calibrating).

Strong and weak, dilute and concentrated acids (HT)

Strong / weak describes the degree of ionisation. A strong acid is completely ionised in water (e.g. hydrochloric, nitric, sulfuric). A weak acid is only partially ionised (e.g. ethanoic, citric, carbonic).
Dilute / concentrated describes the amount of acid per unit volume (mol/dm³). Both strong and weak acids can be made at any concentration.
  • At the same concentration, a strong acid has a much higher H⁺ concentration than a weak acid, so it has a lower pH (e.g. 0.1 mol/dm³ HCl is about pH 1; 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid is about pH 3).
  • The strong acid is more reactive: it fizzes faster with magnesium or calcium carbonate, because there are more H⁺ ions to collide with. With equal amounts of acid, both eventually make the same total volume of gas — the weak acid just takes longer, because more molecules ionise as H⁺ ions are used up.
  • "Dilute" does not mean "weak": a concentrated weak acid can have a lower pH than a very dilute strong acid.

pH and hydrogen ion concentration (HT)

  • Neutral (pH 7): H⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations are equal.
  • Acidic (pH below 7): more H⁺ than OH⁻; the higher the H⁺ concentration, the lower the pH.
  • Alkaline (pH above 7): more OH⁻ than H⁺; the lower the H⁺ concentration, the higher the pH.
  • When H⁺ concentration increases by a factor of 10, the pH decreases by 1.
(HT) A solution of hydrochloric acid has pH 2. It is diluted until its H⁺ concentration is 1/100 of the original. New pH? Each factor of 10 decrease raises pH by 1, so two factors of 10 → pH 4.

C6.2 How do chemists control the rate of reactions?

Controlling the rate lets chemists make a product as fast as is safe and economic. The rate can be changed by temperature, concentration (of solutions), pressure (of gases), surface area (of solids) and catalysts.

Collision theory

Particles react only when they collide with at least a minimum amount of energy, the activation energy. The rate depends on the frequency of collisions and the energy of the collisions (the proportion that are successful).
ChangeEffect on rateExplanation (particle model)
Increase temperatureIncreasesParticles move faster so collide more often; more importantly, more collisions have energy ≥ activation energy, so a larger proportion are successful
Increase concentration of a solutionIncreasesMore reacting particles in the same volume → more frequent collisions
Increase pressure of a gasIncreasesSame number of particles in a smaller volume → more frequent collisions
Increase surface area (smaller pieces, powder)IncreasesMore particles exposed on the surface → more frequent collisions
Add a catalystIncreasesAlternative pathway with a lower activation energy → more collisions are successful

Ideas about Science (IaS3): the particle (collision) model is a scientific explanation used to account for all of these observed patterns.

Surface area to volume ratio

Cutting a solid into smaller pieces does not change its total volume but increases its total surface area, so the surface area : volume ratio goes up and more particles are available to collide.

Catalysts

  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction but is not used up: it is chemically unchanged, and its mass is the same at the end, so it can be recovered and reused.
  • Only a small amount is needed, and catalysts are often specific to one reaction.
  • It works by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful.
  • Identifying a catalyst: it is not included in the overall equation (often written over the arrow), and it is present unchanged at the end. E.g. manganese(IV) oxide in 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂; iron in the Haber process; enzymes in living things.
  • Ideas about Science (IaS4): catalysts let industrial processes run at lower temperatures, saving energy and raw materials and cutting emissions — more sustainable, "green" chemistry.

Measuring the rate of a reaction (PAG8)

Rate can be found by measuring how fast a product forms or a reactant is used up.

  • Gas produced — volume: collect the gas in a gas syringe, or over water in an upturned measuring cylinder or burette, and record the volume at regular time intervals.
  • Gas produced — mass: stand the flask on a balance and record the loss in mass as gas escapes (a cotton wool plug lets gas out but stops acid spray). Works poorly for H₂ because it is so light.
  • Colour change (HT): time how long a colour takes to appear or disappear, by eye or with a colorimeter that measures how much light passes through the solution.
  • Formation of a precipitate (HT): time how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear as the solution goes cloudy, e.g. sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid → a yellow precipitate of sulfur. A light sensor/colorimeter removes the judgement of the eye.

Interpreting rate graphs

  • The curve is steepest at the start (highest concentration of reactants, fastest rate) and gets less steep as reactants are used up.
  • When the curve becomes horizontal, the reaction has stopped because the limiting reactant is used up.
  • A steeper curve means a faster rate. If the amounts of reactants are the same, the curves finish at the same final volume.
  • The rate at any moment = the gradient of the tangent to the curve at that time (change in y ÷ change in x).
  • Mean rate = amount of product formed (or reactant used) ÷ time taken. From the graph, curve A makes 50 cm³ in about 150 s: mean rate = 50 ÷ 150 = 0.33 cm³/s.
mean rate of reaction = quantity of product formed ÷ time taken
units: cm³/s or g/s    rate ∝ 1 ÷ time (when timing a fixed change, e.g. a disappearing cross)

Graphs of reaction conditions against rate (HT)

Plot a measure of rate (e.g. 1/time, or initial gradient) against the condition you changed.

  • Rate vs concentration is often a straight line through the origin: doubling the concentration doubles the frequency of collisions, so roughly doubles the rate (rate is proportional to concentration).
  • Rate vs temperature curves upwards: the rate increases more and more steeply as temperature rises (a common rule of thumb is that it roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise).
  • Rate vs surface area increases as particle size decreases.
Using proportionality (M1c): in a disappearing-cross experiment the cross vanishes in 80 s at a thiosulfate concentration of 0.10 mol/dm³ (illustrative data). Predict the time at 0.20 mol/dm³.
Doubling concentration doubles the rate; rate ∝ 1/time, so time halves: 40 s. Relative rate = 1/40 = 0.025 s⁻¹ compared with 1/80 = 0.0125 s⁻¹.

Practical work (PAG8): investigate the effect of surface area, concentration and temperature on rate (e.g. marble chips of different sizes with acid, measuring gas volume or mass loss), and compare two methods of following the same reaction.

Enzymes

  • Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts in living organisms (link to B3.1).
  • They work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH (the optimum); outside it they work slowly or are denatured (their shape changes permanently).
  • Industrial uses: yeast enzymes in fermentation to make ethanol; enzymes in biological washing powders that work at low temperatures; making sugar syrups for food from starch.
  • Enzymes limit the conditions that can be used, but this is often an advantage: processes run at low temperatures and normal pressure, so energy demand and costs are lower.

C6.3 What factors affect the yield of chemical reactions?

Reversible reactions

Some reactions can go in both directions, depending on the conditions. They are shown with the symbol ⇌.

  • Heating ammonium chloride: NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g) — it decomposes when heated, and the gases recombine on cooling.
  • Hydrated copper(II) sulfate (blue) ⇌ anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (white) + water. Heating drives water off; adding water turns it blue again (and releases heat).
  • The Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g).

In a closed system a reversible reaction never reaches 100% yield, because as products form some of them turn back into reactants. In industry this wastes reactants and means extra stages are needed to separate products from unreacted reactants.

Dynamic equilibrium

In a closed system, a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. Both reactions are still happening, so the amounts (concentrations) of reactants and products stay constant — but they are not necessarily equal.

Changing the position of equilibrium (HT)

If the conditions of a system at equilibrium are changed, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose the change:

ChangeEquilibrium shifts…Haber process N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (forward reaction exothermic)
Increase concentration of a reactantTowards the products (to use it up)Adding N₂ or H₂, or removing NH₃, increases the yield
Increase temperatureIn the endothermic directionShifts left → lower yield of NH₃ (but faster rate)
Decrease temperatureIn the exothermic directionShifts right → higher yield (but slower rate)
Increase pressure (gases)Towards the side with fewer gas molecules4 molecules → 2 molecules, so shifts right → higher yield
Add a catalystNo change in positionEquilibrium reached faster; yield unchanged
  • Catalysts speed up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so they increase the rate but do not affect the yield.
  • Disadvantages of very high temperatures or pressures: large energy costs to heat or compress the gases; expensive, thick-walled equipment that can still fail under extreme conditions; greater safety risk (explosion, leaks). High temperature may also lower the yield of an exothermic reaction.
  • Chemical engineers therefore choose conditions that give the best economic outcome: a good enough yield, fast enough, safely, with acceptable energy and equipment costs and as little waste as possible.

C6.4 How are chemicals made on an industrial scale?

Fertilisers and the importance of N, P and K

Plants need nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) compounds for healthy growth (N for proteins and leaf growth, P for roots and DNA, K for flowers, fruit and enzymes). Crops take these elements from the soil and they are removed when the crop is harvested, so fertilisers are added to replace them. NPK fertilisers are formulations containing salts such as ammonium nitrate NH₄NO₃, ammonium sulfate (NH₄)₂SO₄, ammonium phosphate (NH₄)₃PO₄ and potassium chloride KCl.

Benefits and costs of fertilisers

  • Demand and supply of food: the world's population could not be fed without synthetic fertilisers — they greatly increase crop yields from the same land. (IaS4: fertiliser production enhances the quality of people's lives.)
  • Synthetic vs natural fertilisers: natural fertilisers (manure, compost) recycle nutrients and add organic matter, but are not available in large enough amounts, are bulky and hard to transport and manage, and their composition varies. Synthetic fertilisers have a known, controlled composition and are concentrated, but making them uses non-renewable natural gas, lots of energy, and releases CO₂.
  • Over-use and eutrophication: excess fertiliser washes into rivers and lakes. Algae and water plants grow rapidly, block light, die and are decomposed by bacteria that use up the dissolved oxygen — fish and other organisms die.

The Haber process

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    forward reaction exothermic

Ammonia is the starting point for almost all nitrogen fertilisers, so the Haber process is vital to agriculture.

Choosing the conditions for the Haber process (HT)

  • Raw materials and feedstocks: nitrogen from the air (by fractional distillation of liquid air); hydrogen from natural gas (methane) and steam: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂. Air is free and abundant; natural gas is non-renewable and its price affects the cost of ammonia.
  • Temperature (~450 °C): the forward reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature gives a higher equilibrium yield — but the rate would be far too slow. 450 °C is a compromise between a reasonable yield and a fast enough rate.
  • Pressure (~200 atm): higher pressure increases both the yield (fewer gas molecules on the right) and the rate. But very high pressures need expensive, thick-walled plant, cost a lot of energy to compress the gases and raise safety risks, so a compromise pressure is used.
  • Catalyst (iron): increases the rate so a useful amount is made quickly at a moderate temperature; it does not change the yield.
  • Separation and recycling: the reaction mixture is cooled, ammonia condenses to a liquid and is removed, and unreacted N₂ and H₂ are recycled. In practice the gases never reach equilibrium in the reactor (only ~15% conversion per pass), but recycling gives an overall conversion of about 98%.
Rate vs equilibrium trade-off (HT): for an exothermic reaction, a lower temperature gives a better equilibrium yield but a slower rate. Industry chooses conditions that make the most product per hour at an acceptable cost — a fast rate at a moderate yield, with recycling, often beats a high yield made slowly. The same trade-off applies to other important processes, e.g. the Contact process for sulfuric acid (2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, also exothermic).

Atom economy

Atom economy is the percentage of the mass of all the atoms in the reactants that ends up in the desired product. It is a theoretical value calculated from the balanced equation, used alongside yield and rate when processes are evaluated.
atom economy = (mass of atoms in desired product ÷ total mass of atoms in reactants) × 100
using the relative formula masses (× the numbers in the balanced equation)
Making ammonium sulfate: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄. Only one product, so atom economy = 100%.
Making calcium oxide (lime): CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Atom economy = 56 ÷ 100 × 100 = 56%; the CO₂ is a waste product.

A high atom economy means less waste and a more sustainable process (fewer raw materials used per kg of product).

Industrial production of fertilisers

Fertiliser manufacture is a set of integrated processes using several raw materials (air, natural gas, water, sulfur, phosphate rock). Process flow charts summarise the raw materials, stages, products, by-products and waste.

  • More than one process meets demand: ammonium sulfate can be made by reacting ammonia with sulfuric acid (as in the lab), or obtained as a by-product or waste product of other processes (e.g. nylon manufacture, coke ovens, cleaning sulfur dioxide from power-station flue gases). Finding uses for by-products makes industry more sustainable.
  • Batch vs continuous: lab processes make chemicals in batches; industrial processes are usually continuous — raw materials fed in and product removed all the time, 24 hours a day.

C6 Summary & Key Equations

  • acid + metal → salt + hydrogen; acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO₂; acid + base/alkali → salt + water
  • (HT) strong = fully ionised; weak = partly ionised; concentrated/dilute = amount per volume
  • (HT) H⁺ concentration ×10 → pH falls by 1
  • Rate increases with temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area and a catalyst
  • mean rate = quantity of product ÷ time; instantaneous rate = gradient of the tangent
  • Catalysts lower the activation energy; they are not used up and do not change the yield
  • Dynamic equilibrium: forward rate = reverse rate in a closed system
  • Haber: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, iron catalyst, ~450 °C, ~200 atm, recycle unreacted gases
  • atom economy = mass of atoms in desired product ÷ total mass of atoms in reactants × 100
Don't confuse yield with rate. A catalyst affects only the rate; temperature and pressure can change both. And never write that a catalyst "takes part and is used up".
