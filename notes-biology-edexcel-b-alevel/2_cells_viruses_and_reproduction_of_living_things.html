2 — Cells, Viruses and Reproduction of Living Things

Edexcel B A-level Biology (9BI0), Topic 2. This topic covers the fine structure of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, how microscopes reveal it, the structure and replication of viruses, the cell cycle, mitosis and meiosis (and what happens when chromosomes go wrong), and sexual reproduction in mammals and flowering plants. It contains three core practicals: CP2 (microscopy and micrometers), CP3 (root tip squash) and CP4 (pollen tube growth).

2.1 Eukaryotic and Prokaryotic Cell Structure and Function

2.1 (i) Cell Theory

Cell theory: The idea that every living organism is made of one or more cells, that the cell is the basic unit of structure, function and organisation in living things, and that new cells only arise by the division of existing cells.

Cell theory is described as a unifying concept because it applies to every organism, from a single bacterium to a blue whale. It developed over roughly two centuries as microscopes improved: Robert Hooke described and named "cells" in cork in 1665; Schleiden (1838, plants) and Schwann (1839, animals) proposed that all organisms are built from cells; and Virchow (1855) popularised the principle that cells come only from pre-existing cells. It is a good example of how scientific ideas change as new technology produces new evidence.

Viruses sit outside cell theory: they are not made of cells and cannot carry out metabolism or reproduce on their own (see 2.2).

2.1 (ii) Levels of Organisation

In multicellular organisms, cells become specialised (differentiated) for particular roles, and specialised cells are grouped into higher levels of organisation:

2.1 (iv) Gram Positive and Gram Negative Cell Walls

The Gram stain (devised by Hans Christian Gram in 1884) sorts bacteria into two groups according to the structure of their cell walls. The smear is stained with crystal violet, treated with iodine (which forms a large complex with the dye), washed with alcohol, then counterstained with a red dye such as safranin.

FeatureGram positiveGram negative
Peptidoglycan layerThick (many layers)Thin (one or a few layers)
Outer membraneAbsentPresent outside the peptidoglycan; contains lipopolysaccharide
Effect of alcohol washCrystal violet–iodine complex is trapped in the thick wallAlcohol disrupts the outer membrane and the thin wall cannot hold the complex, so the violet washes out

ExamplesStaphylococcus aureus, StreptococcusEscherichia coli, Salmonella

Why they respond differently to antibiotics: Penicillin (and other β-lactam antibiotics) stops the enzymes that build the peptide cross-links in peptidoglycan. As a growing bacterium makes new wall, the wall becomes weak; water enters by osmosis and the cell bursts (osmotic lysis). In Gram positive bacteria the peptidoglycan is exposed on the surface, so penicillin reaches its target easily. In Gram negative bacteria the outer membrane acts as a barrier, keeping many antibiotics (including penicillin G) and the enzyme lysozyme away from the peptidoglycan, so Gram negative bacteria are generally less sensitive. Some antibiotics, such as tetracyclines, act on the 70S ribosome instead and work on both groups. Knowing a pathogen’s Gram type therefore helps doctors choose an effective drug quickly.

Exam Tip: A common mistake is to say that Gram negative bacteria have "no peptidoglycan" or that "the thick wall stops penicillin". It is the other way round: Gram positive bacteria have a thick, exposed peptidoglycan wall that penicillin can attack; Gram negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer protected by an outer membrane. Penicillin does not harm human cells because they have no peptidoglycan.

Do not say that plant cells lack mitochondria, or that ribosomes are membrane-bound — both are frequent errors.

2.1 (vi) Magnification and Resolution: Light and Electron Microscopy

Magnification: How many times larger the image is than the real object. Resolution: The smallest distance between two points at which they can still be seen as two separate points. Resolution sets how much detail can be seen; magnifying beyond the resolution limit just gives a bigger, blurred image.
magnification = image size ÷ actual size     so   actual size = image size ÷ magnification

Resolution is limited by the wavelength of the radiation used: the shorter the wavelength, the smaller the details that can be separated. Visible light has wavelengths of roughly 400–700 nm, which limits a light microscope to a resolution of about 200 nm (0.2 μm). A beam of electrons has a far shorter wavelength, so electron microscopes can resolve structures well below 1 nm in principle; in biological specimens the practical limit is a few nanometres.

Worked example (changing the subject; units): A mitochondrion is 36 mm long in a micrograph and is actually 12 μm long. Convert to the same unit: 36 mm = 36 000 μm. Magnification = 36 000 ÷ 12 = ×3000. Remember 1 mm = 1000 μm and 1 μm = 1000 nm.

Order of magnitude: a cell 20 μm across is 20 000 nm, which is 200 times a 100 nm virus — a difference of about two orders of magnitude (102). Sizes are often written in standard form, e.g. 2 × 10−5 m for the cell and 1 × 10−7 m for the virus.

2.1 (vii) Why Specimens Are Stained

Most cell components are colourless and nearly transparent, so they give very little contrast. Stains bind selectively to particular chemicals or structures, making them visible and allowing different parts to be told apart.

  • Light microscopy: iodine solution stains starch blue-black; methylene blue stains nuclei in animal cells; toluidine blue or acetic orcein stain DNA, making chromosomes visible in root tip squashes; the Gram stain distinguishes bacterial wall types. Differential stains colour different tissues differently.
  • Electron microscopy: specimens are treated with compounds of heavy metals (e.g. lead or uranium salts, osmium tetroxide) that scatter electrons, so stained structures such as membranes appear dark.

Staining usually requires fixing, which kills the cells, and the preparation can introduce artefacts (structures that are not present in the living cell).

Core Practical 2 (CP2): Use of the light microscope, including calibrating an eyepiece graticule with a stage micrometer, and drawing a few cells from a specialised tissue. For the full method, see Core Practicals — CP2.

Calibration example: A stage micrometer 1 mm long is divided into 100 divisions, so each division is 10 μm. At ×400, 25 eyepiece divisions line up exactly with 10 stage divisions (100 μm). One eyepiece division = 100 ÷ 25 = 4 μm at this magnification. A cell that spans 12 eyepiece divisions is 12 × 4 = 48 μm long. The graticule must be recalibrated for every objective lens.

Exam Tip: Biological drawings should use a sharp pencil, clear continuous lines with no shading, correct proportions, ruled label lines that do not cross, and a title with the magnification. Only draw what you can see. If a question says calculate, show every step, including the unit conversion; if it says explain, you need to give reasons (e.g. why electron microscopes resolve finer detail: shorter wavelength).

2.2 Viruses

2.2 (i) Classification of Viruses

Virus: A non-cellular infectious particle, usually 20–300 nm across, made of a nucleic acid genome (DNA or RNA) inside a protein coat (capsid), sometimes surrounded by a lipid envelope taken from the host cell membrane. Viruses can only replicate inside a living host cell.

Viruses are classified mainly by the type of nucleic acid they carry (DNA or RNA; single- or double-stranded; whether they use reverse transcription) and by their structure (shape of the capsid, and whether there is an envelope). The four examples you need:

VirusNucleic acidStructureHost
λ (lambda) phageDouble-stranded DNABacteriophage: polyhedral (icosahedral) head containing the DNA, attached to a long tail; no envelopeThe bacterium E. coli
Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)Single-stranded RNARigid rod: protein subunits arranged in a helix around the RNA; no envelopeTobacco and related plants, causing mottled (mosaic) leaves
Ebola virusSingle-stranded RNALong, thread-like (filamentous) particle, often curved or branched; enveloped, with glycoprotein spikesHumans and other primates (fruit bats are thought to be the natural reservoir)
Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)Single-stranded RNA (two copies); a retrovirusRoughly spherical; cone-shaped capsid containing RNA and the enzymes reverse transcriptase and integrase; enveloped, with glycoproteins (gp120) that bind to the CD4 receptorHumans; infects helper T cells

A retrovirus uses reverse transcriptase to make a DNA copy of its RNA genome inside the host cell. This DNA is then inserted into the host chromosome by integrase, where it is called a provirus.

2.2 (ii) The Lytic Cycle and Latency

In the lytic cycle a virus takes over the host cell, makes many copies of itself and then bursts (lyses) the cell:

  1. Attachment: proteins on the virus bind to specific receptor molecules on the host cell surface. This is why each virus infects only certain cell types (e.g. HIV binds CD4 on helper T cells).
  2. Entry: a phage injects its DNA through the bacterial wall, leaving the capsid outside; enveloped animal viruses enter when the envelope fuses with the host membrane or when the cell takes them in by endocytosis.
  3. Replication and synthesis: the host cell’s ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP are used to copy the viral genome and make viral proteins. The host’s own metabolism is disrupted.
  4. Assembly: new capsids form and are filled with viral nucleic acid.
  5. Release: the host cell bursts, releasing large numbers of new virus particles (for phages, lysis is caused by a viral enzyme that weakens the wall). Enveloped viruses often leave by budding, taking a piece of host membrane as their envelope.
Latency: A stage in which the viral genome stays inside the host cell without new virus particles being made, so there are no symptoms. In λ phage (the lysogenic pathway) the DNA is inserted into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage and is copied every time the bacterium divides. In HIV, the provirus sits in the DNA of the host cell. A trigger (such as stress or damage to the host cell) can later switch the virus into the lytic cycle.

Latency explains why some infections can reappear years later (e.g. cold sores from herpes simplex) and why HIV can remain in the body even when drug treatment has made the virus undetectable in blood.

2.2 (iii) Antivirals

Viruses are not cells: they have no cell wall, no 70S ribosomes and no metabolism of their own. Antibiotics target bacterial structures and processes, so they have no effect on viruses. Antiviral drugs must instead stop the virus replicating, usually by blocking a step or enzyme that is unique to the virus, so that host cells are harmed as little as possible. Examples:

  • Reverse transcriptase inhibitors (e.g. zidovudine, AZT) stop HIV making DNA from its RNA.
  • Protease inhibitors stop HIV cutting its proteins into their working forms, so new particles are not infectious.
  • Neuraminidase inhibitors (e.g. oseltamivir) stop new influenza particles being released from the host cell.
  • Other antivirals block attachment or entry, or are nucleotide analogues that stop the viral genome being copied (e.g. aciclovir for herpes).

Because viruses use the host’s machinery, there are relatively few viral targets, and viruses (especially RNA viruses) mutate rapidly, so resistance to a single drug can develop. HIV is therefore treated with combinations of drugs.

2.2 (iv) Preventing Spread: the 2014 Ebola Outbreak

Once a viral infection is established it can be hard to treat, because there are few effective antivirals and the virus is inside the body’s own cells. Disease control therefore concentrates on stopping transmission.

The West African Ebola epidemic began in Guinea in late 2013 and spread through 2014 to Sierra Leone and Liberia, the largest Ebola outbreak recorded. By the time it ended in 2016 the World Health Organization had recorded more than 28 000 cases and over 11 000 deaths. Ebola virus disease causes fever, vomiting, diarrhoea and in some patients bleeding; many patients died. The virus spreads through direct contact with the blood or other body fluids of infected people (living or dead), or with contaminated objects. Health workers, family carers and people handling bodies at funerals were at high risk.

Measures used to stop the spread included:

  • Isolating patients in specialised treatment centres, and quarantining people who had been in contact with them.
  • Contact tracing: finding and monitoring everyone who had been in contact with a case for the 21-day incubation period.
  • Personal protective equipment (gowns, gloves, masks, eye protection) and strict decontamination for health workers.
  • Safe burial teams, because traditional burial practices involving touching the body spread infection.
  • Public education about how the virus is spread; screening of travellers at airports and borders.

2.2 (v) Ethics of Using Untested Drugs in Epidemics

At the start of the 2014 outbreak there was no licensed drug or vaccine for Ebola. A few experimental treatments were given to a small number of patients on compassionate grounds, and in August 2014 a WHO ethics panel concluded that offering unproven treatments could be ethical if certain conditions were met (such as informed consent, openness about what is known, and collecting data). Later, an experimental vaccine was tested in a large trial in Guinea in 2015.

2.3 Eukaryotic Cell Cycle and Division

2.3 (i) The Cell Cycle

Cell cycle: The controlled sequence of events by which a eukaryotic cell grows, copies its DNA and divides to give two genetically identical daughter cells. It has three main stages: interphase, mitosis (nuclear division) and cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
  • G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins and organelles, and carries out its normal functions.
  • S phase (synthesis): DNA is replicated semi-conservatively, so each chromosome becomes two identical sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
  • G2 (second growth phase): the cell continues to grow and makes the proteins needed for division, such as tubulin for the spindle; organelles such as mitochondria are replicated.
  • Mitosis then cytokinesis.

The cycle is regulated at checkpoints (for example at the end of G1 and before mitosis) where the cell checks that it is large enough, that DNA is undamaged and that replication is complete. Cells that stop dividing leave the cycle into a resting state (G0), as many nerve and muscle cells do. When the controls fail, cells may divide uncontrollably and form a tumour.

2.3 (ii) Genetic Material During the Cell Cycle and the Stages of Mitosis

During interphase the DNA is spread out as chromatin, so individual chromosomes cannot be seen. After S phase the amount of DNA per nucleus has doubled (from 2x to 4x), though the chromosome number (2n) is unchanged, because each chromosome now has two chromatids. Mitosis shares these chromatids equally so each daughter nucleus returns to 2x with the full diploid set.

The nucleolus disappears and the nuclear envelope breaks down. In animal cells the centrioles move to opposite poles; spindle fibres (microtubules) form between the poles.
MetaphaseSpindle fibres attach to the centromere of each chromosome, and the chromosomes line up individually across the equator of the spindle.

Exam Tip: Distinguish between a chromosome, a chromatid and chromatin. DNA replication happens in S phase of interphase, not in prophase. In metaphase of mitosis, chromosomes line up singly; in metaphase I of meiosis they line up in homologous pairs. Examiners also penalise "the chromosomes split" — say the centromeres divide and the sister chromatids separate.

2.3 (iii) Roles of Mitosis

  • Growth: a multicellular organism develops from one zygote by repeated mitosis, all cells having the same genetic information. In plants, growth by mitosis happens in meristems, such as those at root and shoot tips.
  • Repair and replacement: damaged tissue is repaired and worn-out cells (skin, gut lining, blood cells) are replaced with identical cells that can do the same job.
  • Asexual reproduction: a single parent produces genetically identical offspring (clones) — for example budding in yeast and Hydra, and vegetative propagation in plants such as strawberry runners and potato tubers. This is fast and does not need a mate, but produces no genetic variation, so a whole population may be vulnerable to the same disease or environmental change.
Core Practical 3 (CP3): Prepare a temporary squash of a root tip, stain it and observe the stages of mitosis in the meristem with a light microscope. For the full method, see Core Practicals — CP3.
mitotic index = number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells counted

Worked example: In a field of view of a root tip, 24 of 200 cells are in a stage of mitosis. Mitotic index = 24 ÷ 200 = 0.12, or 12%. The proportion of cells in each stage is proportional to the time that stage lasts, so if the whole cycle takes 20 hours and 5% of cells are in metaphase, metaphase lasts about 0.05 × 20 = 1 hour.

2.3 (iv) Meiosis and Haploid Gametes

Meiosis: A type of nuclear division involving two successive divisions (meiosis I and meiosis II), after a single round of DNA replication, that produces four haploid (n) nuclei that are genetically different from each other and from the parent cell.

Halving the chromosome number is essential for sexual reproduction: when two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the diploid number is restored (in humans, 23 + 23 = 46) instead of doubling every generation.

2.3 (v) Meiosis and Genetic Variation

Meiosis produces new combinations of alleles (recombination) in two ways:

  • Independent assortment (metaphase I and anaphase I): each homologous pair lines up independently of every other pair, so which maternal and paternal chromosomes end up together in a gamete is random. The number of possible combinations is 2n, where n is the haploid number. For humans, 223 = 8 388 608 (about 8.4 million) combinations. Independent assortment in meiosis II also adds variation where crossing over has made the sister chromatids different.
  • Crossing over (prophase I): non-sister chromatids of a homologous pair break and rejoin at chiasmata, exchanging equivalent sections. This produces chromatids carrying new combinations of alleles of linked genes (genes on the same chromosome).

Random fertilisation then combines two such gametes, so the number of possible genetic combinations in offspring is enormous. This variation is the raw material for natural selection.

Exam Tip: Crossing over produces new combinations of alleles, not new alleles — only mutation creates new alleles. Crossing over happens between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. When a question asks you to calculate gamete combinations from independent assortment, use 2n with n as the number of homologous pairs.

2.3 (vi) Chromosome Mutations: Translocations

Chromosome mutation: A change in the structure or number of whole chromosomes, large enough in structural cases to affect many genes. Translocation: a section of one chromosome breaks off and becomes attached to another, non-homologous chromosome.

Translocations may be reciprocal (two non-homologous chromosomes swap sections) or non-reciprocal. In a balanced translocation no genetic material is lost or gained, so the carrier is usually healthy, but at meiosis the chromosomes may be shared out unevenly, producing gametes with missing or extra sections. This can cause miscarriage or genetic disorders in offspring. Translocations can also harm the person carrying them if a break point disrupts a gene or joins two genes together:

  • In most cases of chronic myeloid leukaemia, a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 produces the shortened "Philadelphia chromosome", carrying a fused gene (BCR-ABL) whose protein continually stimulates cell division.
  • A small percentage of cases of Down’s syndrome are caused by a translocation in which an extra copy of most of chromosome 21 is attached to another chromosome (often chromosome 14).

2.3 (vii) Non-disjunction: Polysomy and Monosomy

Non-disjunction: The failure of homologous chromosomes (in anaphase I) or sister chromatids (in anaphase II) to separate properly during meiosis, so that some gametes have an extra copy of a chromosome (n + 1) and some lack one (n − 1).

If an n + 1 gamete is fertilised by a normal gamete, the zygote has three copies of that chromosome (trisomy, a form of polysomy — more than two copies of a chromosome). If an n − 1 gamete is fertilised, the zygote has only one copy (monosomy). Most such zygotes do not survive, but some do:

When asked to describe non-disjunction, name the stage (anaphase I or II), say what fails to separate (homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids), and state the resulting gametes (n + 1 and n − 1).

2.4 Sexual Reproduction in Mammals

2.4 (i) Spermatogenesis and Oogenesis

Gametogenesis in both sexes follows the same pattern: diploid germ cells multiply by mitosis, grow, and then undergo meiosis to produce haploid cells. The differences lie in timing and in how the cytoplasm is shared.

Spermatogenesis takes place in the walls of the seminiferous tubules of the testes, supported and nourished by Sertoli cells:

  1. Diploid spermatogonia near the outer edge of the tubule divide repeatedly by mitosis.
  2. Some grow into diploid primary spermatocytes.
  3. Each primary spermatocyte completes meiosis I to form two haploid secondary spermatocytes.
  4. Meiosis II gives four haploid spermatids.
  5. Spermatids mature into spermatozoa, developing an acrosome, a mitochondria-rich midpiece and a flagellum, and they are released into the lumen of the tubule.

Sperm production continues throughout life from puberty onwards, producing millions of sperm every day.

Oogenesis takes place in the ovaries:

  1. Before birth, diploid oogonia in the fetal ovary divide by mitosis and grow into diploid primary oocytes, each surrounded by follicle cells (a primary follicle).
  2. The primary oocytes start meiosis I but stop in prophase I. A baby girl is born with all the primary oocytes she will ever have.
  3. From puberty, in each menstrual cycle, a follicle develops and its primary oocyte completes meiosis I. The cytoplasm divides unequally, forming a large haploid secondary oocyte and a small first polar body.
  4. The secondary oocyte starts meiosis II but stops at metaphase II. It is released from the ovary at ovulation.
  5. Meiosis II is completed only if a sperm enters, producing the ovum and a second polar body. Polar bodies eventually degenerate.

2.4 (ii) Fertilisation

Fertilisation in humans normally takes place in the oviduct. The sequence, from first contact to the fusion of nuclei, is:

  1. Capacitation: while in the female reproductive tract, chemical changes to the sperm cell surface membrane (such as removal of cholesterol and glycoproteins) make the sperm more active and able to undergo the acrosome reaction.
  2. Passing the follicle cells: the sperm swims through the layer of follicle cells (corona radiata) around the secondary oocyte.
  3. Acrosome reaction: when the sperm head binds to receptors on the zona pellucida (a glycoprotein layer around the oocyte), the acrosome releases hydrolytic (protease) enzymes by exocytosis. These digest a path through the zona pellucida.
  4. Fusion of membranes: the sperm cell surface membrane fuses with the oocyte membrane, and the sperm nucleus enters the oocyte cytoplasm.
  5. Cortical reaction: cortical granules just under the oocyte membrane release their contents by exocytosis into the space beneath the zona pellucida. Enzymes change the zona so that it hardens and its sperm receptors are destroyed, preventing further sperm entering (preventing polyspermy).
  6. Completion of meiosis II: sperm entry triggers the secondary oocyte to finish meiosis II, forming the ovum nucleus and the second polar body.
  7. Fusion of nuclei: the haploid male and female nuclei move together and fuse, forming a diploid zygote.
Exam Tip: Remember the correct order: acrosome reaction then membrane fusion then cortical reaction. The acrosome reaction happens in the sperm; the cortical reaction happens in the oocyte. The cell released at ovulation is a secondary oocyte, not a mature ovum. The cortical reaction does not "kill" other sperm — it changes the zona pellucida so that no more can bind or get through.

2.4 (iii) Early Development to the Blastocyst

After fertilisation the zygote moves down the oviduct towards the uterus, dividing by mitosis as it goes. These first divisions are called cleavage: the cells do not grow between divisions, so the embryo stays about the same overall size while its cells get smaller.

  • The zygote divides to form 2, then 4, then 8 cells, and so on.
  • After roughly 3–4 days a solid ball of about 16 or more cells, the morula, reaches the uterus.
  • By about day 5 fluid collects inside it and a hollow blastocyst forms, with three parts:
    • an outer layer of cells, the trophoblast, which will help implant the embryo and form part of the placenta;
    • the inner cell mass, a cluster of cells at one side that will develop into the embryo (these are embryonic stem cells);
    • a fluid-filled cavity (the blastocoel).
  • The blastocyst breaks out of the zona pellucida and, about 6–7 days after fertilisation, begins to implant in the endometrium (lining) of the uterus.

2.5 Sexual Reproduction in Plants

2.5 (i) Formation of Pollen Grains and the Embryo Sac

Pollen grains (male gametophytes) in the anther:

  1. Each anther contains four pollen sacs filled with diploid pollen mother cells.
  2. Each pollen mother cell divides by meiosis to form a tetrad of four haploid cells (microspores).
  3. The tetrad cells separate and each develops a tough, resistant outer wall (exine, made of sporopollenin) and a thin inner wall (intine).
  4. The haploid nucleus divides by mitosis to give a tube nucleus and a generative nucleus. The pollen grain is now mature.
  5. The anther wall dries and splits (dehiscence), releasing the pollen.

The embryo sac (female gametophyte) in the ovule:

  1. Inside each ovule, within the nucellus and surrounded by the integuments (which leave a small gap, the micropyle), a diploid megaspore mother cell divides by meiosis, producing four haploid cells.
  2. Three of these degenerate; the surviving megaspore enlarges.
  3. Its nucleus divides by mitosis three times, giving eight haploid nuclei.
  4. These become arranged as: the egg cell and two synergids at the micropyle end; three antipodal cells at the opposite end; and two polar nuclei in the centre (in the central cell). This is the mature embryo sac.
Exam Tip: In flowering plants meiosis produces spores, and it is mitosis that then produces the gamete nuclei. Do not write that the generative nucleus or egg cell is made by meiosis. The pollen grain is not itself a gamete; the male gametes are the two nuclei formed from the generative nucleus.

2.5 (ii) How the Male Nuclei Reach the Embryo Sac

  1. Pollination transfers pollen from an anther to a compatible stigma (by insects or wind, for example).
  2. The pollen grain absorbs water and sugars from the sticky stigma surface and germinates: a pollen tube grows out through a pore (aperture) in the exine.
  3. The tube grows down through the style. Its tip secretes digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes which break down the tissues of the style; the products of digestion are absorbed and used for growth of the tube.
  4. The tube nucleus stays near the growing tip and is thought to control the growth of the tube, by controlling the production of the enzymes and proteins needed.
  5. The tube grows towards the ovule, guided by chemical signals released from the ovule (chemotropism).
  6. As it grows, the generative nucleus divides by mitosis to form two male nuclei (male gametes), which follow the tube nucleus down the tube.
  7. The tube enters the ovule through the micropyle; the tube nucleus degenerates and the tip of the tube bursts, releasing the two male nuclei into the embryo sac.
Core Practical 4 (CP4): Investigate how sucrose concentration affects pollen tube growth or germination. For the full method, see Core Practicals — CP4.

In CP4, the sucrose concentration is the independent variable; pollen tube length (or the percentage of grains that have germinated) is the dependent variable, measured using a calibrated eyepiece graticule after a fixed time. Plotting the two variables on a graph shows the trend. Sucrose supplies respiratory substrate and affects the water potential of the medium, so if the concentration is too high the pollen loses water by osmosis and tube growth falls.

2.5 (iii) Double Fertilisation

Double fertilisation: The process, unique to flowering plants, in which two separate fusions take place in the embryo sac, each involving one of the two male nuclei from a single pollen tube.
  • One male nucleus (n) fuses with the egg cell nucleus (n) to form a diploid zygote (2n), which develops into the embryo (plumule, radicle and cotyledons).
  • The other male nucleus (n) fuses with the two polar nuclei (n + n) to form a triploid endosperm nucleus (3n), which divides repeatedly to form the endosperm, a food store (starch, protein, lipids) for the developing embryo.

After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed and its integuments become the seed coat (testa); the ovary develops into the fruit.

Exam Tip: "Double fertilisation" does not mean two pollen grains or two eggs. One pollen tube delivers two male nuclei: one fertilises the egg (zygote, 2n), the other fuses with the two polar nuclei (endosperm, 3n). If a question asks you to state the ploidy of each, give the number (n, 2n or 3n) with no need for explanation; if it asks you to explain why the endosperm is triploid, say which three haploid nuclei fused.
