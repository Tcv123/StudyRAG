5: Energy for Biological Processes

Edexcel B A-level Biology (9BI0), Topic 5. This topic follows energy from light to ATP: how cells release energy from respiratory substrates in aerobic and anaerobic respiration, how photosynthetic pigments capture light, and how the two stages of photosynthesis turn carbon dioxide into the raw materials for every other organic molecule in a plant. It builds on carbohydrates and enzymes from earlier topics and includes three core practicals (CP9, CP10 and CP11).

5.1 Aerobic Respiration

5.1 (i) ATP and Heat from Respiration

Cellular respiration: A series of enzyme-controlled reactions inside cells in which organic molecules (respiratory substrates, most often glucose) are oxidised step by step. The energy released is used to make ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi); the rest is lost as heat.

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the immediate energy source for metabolic reactions in every cell. It is a phosphorylated nucleotide: adenine joined to ribose, with a chain of three phosphate groups. When ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and Pi (a reaction catalysed by ATPase), energy is released, around 30 kJ per mole under standard conditions. That energy is coupled to reactions and processes that need it, such as active transport, muscle contraction, protein synthesis, DNA replication and the movement of vesicles. ATP is then resynthesised from ADP and Pi using energy from respiration, so each molecule is recycled many times.

ATP is well suited to this role because:

  • energy is released in small, manageable amounts, so little is wasted as heat at any one time;
  • hydrolysis is a single, fast reaction catalysed by one enzyme, so energy is available quickly;
  • it is soluble and small, so it moves easily within the cell to where energy is needed;
  • it can phosphorylate other molecules, making them more reactive (for example glucose at the start of glycolysis).

Respiration is not perfectly efficient. Only part of the chemical energy in glucose ends up in ATP; the remainder is released as heat. In endotherms such as birds and mammals, this heat is used to keep body temperature high and stable. In all organisms, the heat raises local temperature a little, which is why germinating seeds in a vacuum flask warm up.

Exam Tip: Never write that respiration "produces energy" or "makes energy". Energy cannot be created. Respiration releases energy from respiratory substrates and uses it to produce ATP. Similarly, avoid "ATP stores large amounts of energy": ATP is an immediate, short-term energy carrier, not an energy store (that role belongs to starch, glycogen and triglycerides).

5.2 Glycolysis

5.2 (i) Converting Hexose to Pyruvate

Glycolysis ("sugar splitting") is the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration and happens in the cytoplasm of every living cell. It does not use oxygen. It converts one hexose (6C) monosaccharide such as glucose into two molecules of pyruvate (3C). The specification needs only the key steps, not the names of every intermediate:

  1. Phosphorylation of the hexose. Two ATP molecules are hydrolysed, and their phosphate groups are added to the hexose to give a hexose bisphosphate. This raises the energy level of the sugar, making it more reactive and easier to split. The charged phosphorylated sugar also cannot diffuse back out of the cell.
  2. Splitting into two 3C molecules. The hexose bisphosphate is split into two three-carbon sugar phosphates (triose phosphate).
  3. Oxidation to glycerate 3-phosphate (GP). Each triose phosphate is oxidised: hydrogen is removed by the coenzyme NAD, which becomes reduced NAD (NADH). A phosphate group is added and the molecule is then converted to glycerate 3-phosphate. During this step ATP is made directly from ADP (substrate-level phosphorylation).
  4. Conversion to pyruvate. GP is converted to pyruvate, and a further ATP is made from each 3C molecule by substrate-level phosphorylation.

The pyruvate still contains most of the chemical energy of the original glucose. What happens next depends on oxygen. With oxygen, pyruvate is actively transported into the mitochondrial matrix for the link reaction. Without oxygen, pyruvate is converted to lactate or ethanol in the cytoplasm (see 5.5), which regenerates the NAD that glycolysis needs.

Substrate-level phosphorylation: Formation of ATP by the direct transfer of a phosphate group from a phosphorylated intermediate to ADP, catalysed by an enzyme. It happens in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle and does not involve an electron transport chain.
Exam Tip: "Net" matters. Glycolysis makes 4 ATP but uses 2, so the net yield is 2 ATP per glucose. Questions often ask for the net figure. Also remember that "NAD is reduced" (it gains hydrogen) at the same time as the triose phosphate "is oxidised" (it loses hydrogen); a good answer names both halves of the redox reaction.

5.3 The Link Reaction and Krebs Cycle

5.3 (i) Location: the Mitochondrial Matrix

Both the link reaction and the Krebs cycle happen in the matrix, the gel-like fluid enclosed by the inner mitochondrial membrane. The matrix contains the enzymes for these reactions, a supply of the coenzymes NAD and FAD and coenzyme A, as well as mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes. Keeping these reactions in the matrix places them right next to the inner membrane, where the reduced coenzymes they produce are reoxidised.

5.3 (ii) Complete Oxidation of Pyruvate

The link reaction. Each pyruvate (3C) is:

  • decarboxylated: one carbon atom is removed as carbon dioxide;
  • oxidised: hydrogen is removed and used to reduce NAD to NADH;
  • left as a 2C acetyl group, which combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl coenzyme A.
pyruvate (3C) + NAD + CoA → acetyl CoA (2C) + CO2 + reduced NAD

No ATP is made in the link reaction. It happens twice for each glucose, because glycolysis gives two pyruvate.

The Krebs cycle. Coenzyme A delivers the acetyl group to the cycle and is released to be reused. The 2C acetyl group combines with a 4C compound to form a 6C compound. In a series of enzyme-catalysed steps, this 6C compound is broken back down to the 4C compound, so the cycle can continue. During one turn of the cycle:

  • two carbon atoms are removed as two molecules of CO2 (decarboxylation);
  • hydrogen atoms are removed (dehydrogenation) and passed to coenzymes: three reduced NAD and one reduced FAD are formed;
  • one ATP is formed by substrate-level phosphorylation.

The intermediate compounds of the Krebs cycle do not need to be named. The important point is where the carbon atoms go: of the six carbon atoms in glucose, two leave as CO2 in the two link reactions and four leave as CO2 in the two turns of the Krebs cycle. After the Krebs cycle, the glucose has been completely oxidised. Its energy is now held mainly in the reduced coenzymes, which carry hydrogen (protons and electrons) to the electron transport chain.

They stop in the absence of oxygen because the electron transport chain stops, so reduced NAD cannot be reoxidised and there is no free NAD left to accept hydrogen. 5.4 Oxidative Phosphorylation

5.4 (i) The Electron Transport Chain Is in the Inner Mitochondrial Membrane

The electron transport chain (ETC) is a series of protein carrier molecules embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, together with the enzyme ATP synthase. The inner membrane is folded into cristae, which give a large surface area for many copies of the chain and of ATP synthase.

5.4 (ii) How the Electron Transport Chain Generates ATP

  1. Reduced NAD and reduced FAD deliver hydrogen atoms to the chain. The hydrogen atoms split into protons (H+) and electrons (e−); the coenzymes are reoxidised and return to the matrix to pick up more hydrogen.
  2. The electrons pass from one carrier to the next in a series of redox reactions. Each carrier is reduced when it gains an electron and oxidised when it passes the electron on.
  3. At each step the electrons move to a lower energy level. The energy released is used by some of the carriers to pump protons from the matrix across the inner membrane into the intermembrane space.
  4. This builds up a higher concentration of protons in the intermembrane space than in the matrix: an electrochemical (proton) gradient, which is a store of potential energy.
  5. Protons diffuse back into the matrix through channels in ATP synthase. Their flow drives the enzyme to join ADP and Pi to make ATP.
Oxidative phosphorylation: The synthesis of ATP using energy released as electrons from reduced coenzymes pass along the electron transport chain to oxygen. It is "oxidative" because the coenzymes are oxidised and oxygen is the final acceptor, and "phosphorylation" because ADP gains a phosphate group.

5.4 (iii) Oxygen as the Terminal Electron Acceptor

At the end of the chain, electrons are passed to oxygen. Each oxygen atom combines with two electrons and two protons from the matrix to form water:

½O2 + 2H+ + 2e− → H2O

Oxygen is essential because it removes electrons from the last carrier. If oxygen is absent, the last carrier stays reduced and cannot accept more electrons, so the whole chain backs up and stops. Reduced NAD and FAD are then no longer reoxidised, and the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop too, because they run out of oxidised coenzymes. The same effect is seen with respiratory poisons such as cyanide, which blocks the final carrier in the chain.

5.4 (iv) Chemiosmosis

Chemiosmosis: The movement of protons down their electrochemical gradient across a membrane, through the enzyme ATP synthase, which releases energy used to synthesise ATP from ADP and Pi. The theory was proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961 and applies to both mitochondria and chloroplasts.

5.4 (v) Why the Mitochondrial Membranes Matter

  • The inner membrane is impermeable to protons except through ATP synthase. Without this barrier the gradient could not be built up or maintained, and protons would leak back without making ATP.
  • The intermembrane space is small, so pumping relatively few protons quickly produces a steep concentration gradient.
  • Cristae provide a large surface area for many electron transport chains and ATP synthase molecules, increasing the rate of ATP production. Cells with a high energy demand, such as muscle and sperm cells, have many mitochondria with densely packed cristae.
  • The outer membrane separates the mitochondrion from the cytoplasm and has transport proteins that let pyruvate, ADP and ATP pass in and out.
  • Compartmentalisation: the enzymes of the link reaction and Krebs cycle are in the matrix, next to the membrane where the reduced coenzymes are reoxidised.

ATP Yield from Aerobic Respiration

Most of the ATP from aerobic respiration comes from oxidative phosphorylation. Older textbooks give a theoretical maximum of 38 ATP per glucose, assuming each reduced NAD gives 3 ATP and each reduced FAD gives 2. Current estimates are lower, about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, because the ratio of protons pumped to ATP made is not a whole number, some protons leak across the inner membrane, and some of the gradient is used to transport pyruvate, ADP and phosphate into the mitochondrion. In an exam, use any figure given in the question; otherwise show the principle that aerobic respiration yields roughly fifteen times as much ATP per glucose as anaerobic respiration.

Exam Tip: For a 4 to 6 mark "explain how ATP is made in oxidative phosphorylation" question, work through the sequence: reduced coenzymes release H → electrons along carriers (redox) → energy released → protons pumped into intermembrane space → proton gradient → protons diffuse back through ATP synthase → ADP + Pi → ATP → oxygen accepts electrons and protons to form water. Missing out where the protons go (into the intermembrane space, not "out of the mitochondrion") is a very common way to lose marks.

5.5 Anaerobic Respiration

5.5 (i) Partial Breakdown of Hexoses

Anaerobic respiration: Respiration without oxygen. The hexose is only partly broken down, to lactate (in mammals) or to ethanol and carbon dioxide (in plants and yeast). Only glycolysis produces ATP, so the yield is small.

Without oxygen the electron transport chain stops (5.4 iii), and so do the link reaction and Krebs cycle. Glycolysis can continue, but only if the NADH it makes is reoxidised so that NAD is available to accept hydrogen again. Anaerobic pathways do exactly this: pyruvate, or a product made from it, accepts hydrogen from reduced NAD. The pathway itself makes no ATP; its purpose is to regenerate NAD so that glycolysis can keep going.

The difference arises because in anaerobic conditions the pyruvate is not oxidised further. Most of the energy in the hexose stays in lactate or ethanol, and the reduced NAD from glycolysis is used to reduce pyruvate instead of passing its electrons to the ETC. Taking 32 ATP as the aerobic figure, anaerobic respiration releases only 2 ÷ 32 × 100 ≈ 6% as much ATP per hexose. Using 30 ATP, aerobic respiration yields 15 times as much; using 38, the anaerobic figure is about 5%.

5.5 (iii) Lactate and Mammalian Muscle Contraction

During intense exercise, oxygen cannot be delivered to muscle fibres fast enough to meet the demand for ATP. Muscle cells then rely more on anaerobic respiration:

pyruvate + reduced NAD → lactate + NAD   (enzyme: lactate dehydrogenase)

This lets muscles keep contracting for a short time when oxygen is limiting, but it has costs:

  • Lactate and hydrogen ions build up in the muscle and blood, lowering the pH. The fall in pH can affect enzymes involved in respiration and contraction, reducing the force of contraction. Along with low ATP levels, this contributes to muscle fatigue and pain.
  • ATP yield is small, so muscles cannot sustain maximum effort for long.
  • After exercise, extra oxygen is taken in (the oxygen debt, or excess post-exercise oxygen consumption). Lactate is carried in the blood to the liver, where it is converted back to pyruvate. Some pyruvate is oxidised aerobically; the rest is converted to glucose and glycogen.

Because lactate can be converted back to pyruvate, the lactate pathway is reversible. No carbon dioxide is lost in forming lactate.

5.5 (iv) Ethanol Formation in Plants

In plant cells (for example in waterlogged roots) and in yeast, anaerobic respiration produces ethanol in two steps:

pyruvate → ethanal + CO2   (decarboxylation)
ethanal + reduced NAD → ethanol + NAD   (reduction)

Here ethanal, not pyruvate, is the hydrogen acceptor that regenerates NAD. Because a carbon atom is lost as CO2, the pathway cannot be reversed. Ethanol is toxic at high concentrations, so tissues can survive this way only for a limited time. Some plants that live in waterlogged soils, such as rice, tolerate ethanol better or have air spaces (aerenchyma) that bring oxygen to the roots.

Core Practical 9: Respirometers

Core Practical 9 (CP9): Using a respirometer to find how a chosen factor (such as temperature) changes the rate of aerobic or anaerobic respiration, with organisms handled safely and ethically. For the full method, variables, risk assessment and analysis, see CP9 on the core practicals page.

In a simple respirometer, small organisms (such as germinating seeds or invertebrates) are sealed in a tube with a CO2 absorber such as soda lime. As they take in oxygen, and the CO2 they release is absorbed, the volume of gas falls and a coloured liquid moves along a capillary tube. A control tube with inert material (glass beads of the same volume) corrects for changes in temperature and pressure. Treat animals humanely: keep exposure short, stop them touching the soda lime, and return them to their habitat afterwards.

Worked calculation. The liquid moves 25 mm along a capillary tube with an internal radius of 0.5 mm in 10 minutes. The volume of oxygen taken up is the volume of a cylinder:

V = πr2l = π × 0.52 × 25 = 19.6 mm3
rate = 19.6 ÷ 10 = 1.96 mm3 O2 min−1

To compare samples of different size, divide by the mass of the organisms to give a rate per gram. If distance moved is plotted against time, the gradient of the straight part of the line gives the rate. For a straight line y = mx + c, the gradient m is the rate and c is the intercept on the y-axis (the reading at time zero). For example, if a line passes through (0 min, 3 mm) and (20 min, 11 mm), m = (11 − 3) ÷ 20 = 0.4 mm min−1 and c = 3 mm.

5.6 Photosynthetic Pigments

5.6 (i) Absorption and Action Spectra

Absorption spectrum: A graph of how much light a pigment (or a mixture of pigments) absorbs at each wavelength.
Action spectrum: A graph of the rate of photosynthesis (for example, oxygen production) at each wavelength of light.

Visible light spans roughly 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). Chlorophylls absorb strongly in the blue-violet and red parts of the spectrum and poorly in the green, which is mostly reflected or transmitted; this is why leaves look green. Carotenoids absorb mainly blue and blue-green light (roughly 400 to 500 nm).

When the action spectrum is plotted on the same axes as the combined absorption spectrum of the pigments, the two curves have very similar shapes: wavelengths that are strongly absorbed give high rates of photosynthesis. This close match is evidence that the absorbed light is what drives photosynthesis, and that the pigments are responsible. The match is not perfect; for instance, not all energy absorbed by accessory pigments is transferred to the reaction centres, and some green light is still used.

Core Practical 10 (CP10): How the colour (wavelength) of light affects the rate of photosynthesis. For the method (for example, coloured filters over an aquatic plant or leaf discs, with light intensity and temperature controlled), see CP10 on the core practicals page.

In CP10 the rate is often measured as the volume of oxygen collected per minute, or as 1/t where t is the time for leaf discs to float. Filters of different colours transmit different wavelengths, but they also transmit different amounts of light, so light intensity should be measured with a light meter and controlled if possible. Blue and red filters should give higher rates than a green filter.

5.6 (ii) Why Plants Have Several Pigments

Chloroplasts contain several pigments arranged in photosystems (light-harvesting complexes) in the thylakoid membranes. Most pigment molecules act as an antenna: they absorb light and pass the energy from molecule to molecule until it reaches a pair of chlorophyll a molecules at the reaction centre, where electrons are excited and emitted.

Having a variety of pigments is an advantage because:

  • A wider range of wavelengths is absorbed. Each pigment has a different absorption spectrum, so together they capture more of the light energy available and pass it to chlorophyll a. This increases the rate of photosynthesis, particularly when light is limited.
  • Adaptation to different light environments. Plants in shade receive light that has already passed through other leaves, so it is relatively rich in green and far-red light. Shade leaves typically have more chlorophyll b relative to chlorophyll a. In water, red light is absorbed near the surface; red algae living deeper contain extra pigments that absorb the blue-green light that penetrates further.
  • Protection. Carotenoids help protect chlorophyll from damage by excess light energy.
Core Practical 11 (CP11): Separating and identifying the pigments in chloroplasts by chromatography. For the full method (extracting pigments with a solvent, spotting, running and calculating Rf values), see CP11 on the core practicals page.

Pigments separate because they differ in solubility in the running solvent and in how strongly they are attracted to the stationary phase (paper or a thin-layer plate). Each pigment is identified by its Rf value:

Rf = distance moved by pigment spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front

Worked example. In one chromatogram the solvent front moved 9.2 cm. Spots were found at 8.6 cm (orange), 5.2 cm (yellow), 4.1 cm (blue-green) and 2.5 cm (yellow-green). The Rf values are 0.93, 0.57, 0.45 and 0.27. The fastest-moving orange spot is carotene; the yellow spot is a xanthophyll; the blue-green spot is chlorophyll a; the yellow-green spot, which moved least, is chlorophyll b. Exact Rf values depend on the solvent and the stationary phase, so compare with standards run under the same conditions. Rf values have no units and always lie between 0 and 1.

Exam Tip: Measure to the centre of each spot and from the origin (pencil line), not from the bottom of the paper. Mark the solvent front as soon as the chromatogram is removed, before it evaporates. If asked to "explain" why the action spectrum matches the absorption spectrum, you must say that absorbed light energy is used in photosynthesis; simply describing the peaks is "describe", not "explain".

5.7 Photosynthesis

6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2   (light energy absorbed by chlorophyll)

Photosynthesis has two linked stages: the light-dependent stage on the thylakoid membranes, which uses light energy to make ATP and reduced NADP and releases oxygen, and the light-independent stage (Calvin cycle) in the stroma, which uses that ATP and reduced NADP to turn carbon dioxide into sugar phosphates.

5.7 (i) Chloroplast Structure

  • Envelope: a double membrane (inner and outer) that surrounds the chloroplast and controls which substances enter and leave.
  • Stroma: the fluid inside the envelope. It contains the enzymes of the light-independent stage (including RUBISCO), starch grains, lipid droplets, circular DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs, each enclosing a space (the thylakoid lumen). Their membranes hold the photosystems, electron carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Grana (singular granum): stacks of thylakoids, which give a large membrane area for absorbing light.
  • Lamellae (intergranal lamellae): thylakoid membranes that extend through the stroma and connect one granum to another. The whole internal membrane system is often called the lamellar structure.

The Light-Dependent Stage

5.7 (ii) The Role of the Thylakoid Membranes

The thylakoid membranes are the site of the light-dependent stage because they hold, in a fixed arrangement:

  • the photosystems (photosystem I and photosystem II), each with antenna pigments and a reaction centre, which absorb light;
  • the electron carriers that pass excited electrons between the photosystems and to NADP;
  • the enzyme complex that splits water on the lumen side of photosystem II;
  • ATP synthase, which spans the membrane.

The membrane is impermeable to protons, so protons pumped into the thylakoid lumen by the electron carriers (and released there by the splitting of water) build up a gradient. Protons flow back into the stroma through ATP synthase, making ATP by chemiosmosis in the same way as in mitochondria. Stacking the thylakoids into grana packs a large area of membrane into a small space.

5.7 (iii) Cyclic and Non-Cyclic Photophosphorylation

Photophosphorylation: The synthesis of ATP from ADP and Pi using energy from light, by chemiosmosis across the thylakoid membrane.

Non-cyclic photophosphorylation involves both photosystems:

  1. Light energy absorbed by photosystem II excites a pair of electrons in the chlorophyll at the reaction centre. They reach a higher energy level and leave the chlorophyll, which is oxidised.
  2. The excited electrons pass along a chain of electron carriers to photosystem I. The energy they lose is used to pump protons into the thylakoid lumen; the resulting proton gradient drives ATP synthesis through ATP synthase.
  3. Photolysis: the electrons lost from photosystem II are replaced by splitting water: 2H2O → 4H+ + 4e− + O2. The oxygen diffuses out of the leaf as a by-product (or is used in respiration); the protons add to the gradient.
  4. Light absorbed by photosystem I re-excites the electrons, which pass via further carriers to NADP. NADP accepts the electrons and a proton from the stroma and becomes reduced NADP (catalysed by NADP reductase).

Electrons flow in one direction, from water to NADP, so the pathway is "non-cyclic". Its products are ATP, reduced NADP and oxygen.

Cyclic photophosphorylation involves only photosystem I. Excited electrons from photosystem I pass to the electron carriers and then return to photosystem I instead of reducing NADP. Energy released as the electrons move along the carriers is used to pump protons, so ATP is made. No water is split, so no oxygen is produced, and no reduced NADP is formed. Cyclic photophosphorylation tops up ATP supply, because the Calvin cycle needs more ATP than reduced NADP.

The Light-Independent Stage

5.7 (iv) The Role of the Stroma

The light-independent stage takes place in the stroma because this is where the enzymes of the Calvin cycle, including RUBISCO, are found, along with the pool of cycle intermediates. The stroma surrounds the thylakoids, so the ATP and reduced NADP made on the stromal side of the thylakoid membranes are released straight into it. The stroma also receives carbon dioxide, which diffuses in through the envelope. Starch made from the products is stored in the stroma as starch grains.

This stage does not use light directly, but it depends on a continuous supply of ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent stage, so in the dark it stops within a short time.

5.7 (v) Carbon Fixation by RUBISCO

Carbon dioxide combines with the 5-carbon compound ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP). The reaction is catalysed by the enzyme ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase (RUBISCO). The 6-carbon product is unstable and immediately splits into two molecules of the 3-carbon compound glycerate 3-phosphate (GP). This step is called carbon fixation because inorganic carbon becomes part of an organic molecule.

RuBP (5C) + CO2 → [unstable 6C] → 2 × GP (3C)   (enzyme: RUBISCO)

5.7 (vi) Using ATP and Reduced NADP: the Calvin Cycle

  • Reduction of GP to GALP. GP is reduced to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GALP), a triose phosphate. Reduced NADP supplies the hydrogen (and is reoxidised to NADP, which returns to the thylakoids); ATP supplies the energy (and is hydrolysed to ADP and Pi).
  • Regeneration of RuBP. Most of the GALP (five out of every six molecules) is used to regenerate RuBP, so more CO2 can be fixed. This step uses further ATP. The intermediate compounds are not required.
  • The remaining GALP (one in every six) is the output of the cycle and is used to make other substances (5.7 vii).

Balancing the carbon. Three turns of the cycle fix three CO2 onto three RuBP (15 carbon atoms), giving six GP and so six GALP (18 carbon atoms). Five GALP (15 carbon atoms) are rearranged into three RuBP, leaving one GALP (3 carbon atoms) as the net gain. Six turns are needed for enough GALP to make one hexose. Per three CO2 fixed, the cycle uses nine ATP (six for reduction and three for regeneration) and six reduced NADP, which is why the extra ATP from cyclic photophosphorylation is useful.

5.7 (vii) Uses of GALP

GALP leaving the Calvin cycle is the starting point for making almost all other organic molecules in the plant:

  • Monosaccharides: two GALP combine to make a hexose such as glucose or fructose. Hexoses are then used in respiration, joined to make sucrose (the main transport sugar in phloem), or polymerised into starch (storage) and cellulose (cell walls).
  • Lipids: GALP can be converted to glycerol, and to acetyl coenzyme A, from which fatty acids are made; these combine to form triglycerides and phospholipids (for membranes).
  • Amino acids: GALP is converted to other organic acids (for example via pyruvate and acetyl CoA), which are combined with nitrogen from nitrate or ammonium ions absorbed from the soil to form amino acids and then proteins.
  • Other molecules: nucleotides (for DNA, RNA and ATP) also need carbon skeletons from photosynthesis, together with nitrogen and phosphate from the soil.

5.7 (viii) Limiting Factors

Limiting factor: The factor that is in shortest supply relative to demand and so holds back the rate of a process. Increasing a limiting factor increases the rate; increasing any other factor does not.
  • Light intensity limits the light-dependent stage: less light means fewer excited electrons, so less ATP and reduced NADP are made, and GP cannot be reduced as fast. At low light intensity the rate rises roughly in proportion to light intensity.
  • Carbon dioxide concentration limits fixation by RUBISCO. Atmospheric CO2 is about 0.04%, and it is often the limiting factor on bright days. Growers of glasshouse crops raise the CO2 concentration to increase yields.
  • Temperature affects the enzyme-controlled reactions, especially the Calvin cycle. At low temperature, enzymes and substrates have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer successful collisions. Above the optimum, enzymes such as RUBISCO begin to denature and the rate falls; stomata may also close to reduce water loss, limiting CO2 uptake.

On the graph, the curves share the same initial slope, because at low light intensity light is the limiting factor whatever the CO2 concentration or temperature. Each curve then levels off. Where it levels off, a factor other than light is limiting: raising the CO2 concentration or the temperature raises the plateau. The graph illustrates the maths skills in this topic: the gradient of the straight region is the rate of change of photosynthesis with light intensity, and a curved region can be analysed by drawing a tangent and finding its gradient. For example, a tangent passing through (4, 2) and (12, 14) has a gradient of (14 − 2) ÷ (12 − 4) = 1.5 units of rate per unit of light intensity.

Light intensity from a point source falls with the square of the distance, so in practicals where a lamp is moved, light intensity is proportional to 1/d2. A heat filter (for example, a beaker of water) or an LED lamp is used so that moving the lamp does not also change the temperature.

Exam Tip: When interpreting a limiting-factor graph, name the limiting factor for each region and justify it from the graph: "between 0 and X, light intensity is limiting because the rate increases as light intensity increases; above X the rate stays constant, so another factor such as CO2 concentration is limiting." For "suggest" questions about glasshouses, weigh the cost of extra heating, lighting or CO2 against the increase in yield; there is no benefit in raising a factor that is not limiting.

  • "Anaerobic respiration makes lactate to get ATP." No: it regenerates NAD so that glycolysis, which makes the ATP, can continue.
  • "The light-independent stage happens at night." No: it runs in the light, using ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent stage, and stops soon after dark.
  • "Oxygen from photosynthesis comes from CO2." No: it comes from photolysis of water.
  • "Plants photosynthesise instead of respiring." No: plant cells respire all the time; photosynthesis happens only in the light and only in cells with chloroplasts.
  • "Protons are pumped out of the mitochondrion." They are pumped into the intermembrane space; in chloroplasts they are pumped into the thylakoid lumen.
  • "Glycolysis happens in the mitochondria." It happens in the cytoplasm; only pyruvate enters the mitochondrion.
