1: Biological Molecules

Edexcel B A-level Biology (9BI0), Topic 1. Living things are built from a small number of repeating building blocks. This topic covers the organic molecules of life (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids), the enzymes that control their reactions, the inorganic ions plants need, and the properties of water that make life possible. A recurring theme is that the structure of each molecule explains its function.

Monomer and polymer: A monomer is a small unit molecule that can be joined to others of the same kind. A polymer is a long molecule made of many monomers joined in a chain. Glucose is the monomer of starch, glycogen and cellulose; amino acids are the monomers of proteins; nucleotides are the monomers of DNA and RNA. (Triglycerides are not polymers: they are made of four smaller molecules, not a repeating chain.)
Condensation and hydrolysis: A condensation reaction joins two molecules with a new covalent bond and releases one molecule of water. A hydrolysis reaction breaks a covalent bond by adding a molecule of water. Glycosidic, ester, peptide and phosphodiester bonds are all made by condensation and broken by hydrolysis.

1.1 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, usually with hydrogen and oxygen in a 2:1 ratio (as in water). They act as respiratory substrates, as energy stores and, in plants, as structural material.

1.1 (ii) Alpha and beta glucose, and ribose

Glucose (C6H12O6) is a hexose: it has six carbon atoms. In solution it exists mostly as a ring of five carbons and one oxygen, with carbon 6 sticking out above the ring as a CH2OH group. Carbon atoms are numbered clockwise from carbon 1, which sits just to the right of the ring oxygen.

There are two ring forms, called isomers, that differ only at carbon 1:

  • α-glucose: the –OH on carbon 1 points below the ring (on the opposite side from the CH2OH on carbon 5).
  • β-glucose: the –OH on carbon 1 points above the ring (on the same side as the CH2OH).

This small difference has big consequences: α-glucose builds starch and glycogen, which can be coiled and branched, while β-glucose builds cellulose, which forms straight, strong chains.

Ribose (C5H10O5) is a pentose, with five carbons. Its ring has four carbons and one oxygen, with carbon 5 outside the ring as CH2OH. Ribose is the sugar in RNA, ATP and ADP. Deoxyribose, the sugar in DNA, is the same except that carbon 2 carries an H instead of an –OH (it has one fewer oxygen atom, hence "deoxy").

Exam Tip: If asked to draw or compare α- and β-glucose, the only difference is the orientation of the H and OH on carbon 1. Do not move any other group. A common error is to say they have different formulae: both are C6H12O6.

1.1 (iii) Building and breaking disaccharides and polysaccharides

Two monosaccharides join when an –OH on one reacts with an –OH on the other. A molecule of water (H2O) is removed and the two rings are left linked through a single oxygen atom. This C–O–C link is a glycosidic bond, and the reaction is a condensation. Bonds are named after the carbons they connect: a bond between carbon 1 of one glucose and carbon 4 of the next is a 1,4-glycosidic bond.

Hydrolysis is the reverse. Adding a water molecule across the glycosidic bond splits it, releasing the monosaccharides. In organisms, hydrolysis is catalysed by specific enzymes (maltase, sucrase, lactase, amylase). In the laboratory it can be done by boiling with dilute acid.

Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides are made by repeated condensation reactions. Every new glucose added forms one more glycosidic bond and releases one more water molecule.

  • Amylose (one component of starch): a long, unbranched chain of α-glucose joined by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds. The angle of the bonds makes the chain coil into a helix, held by hydrogen bonds within the chain.
  • Amylopectin (the other component of starch): α-glucose chains with α-1,4 bonds, plus α-1,6 glycosidic bonds that create side branches roughly every 20–30 glucose units.
  • Glycogen (the animal and fungal store): like amylopectin, with α-1,4 and α-1,6 bonds, but much more branched (a branch roughly every 8–12 units) and with shorter chains.
  • Cellulose (plant cell walls): long, straight, unbranched chains of β-glucose joined by β-1,4 glycosidic bonds. Because of the β-OH position, each glucose must be flipped 180° relative to its neighbour for the bond to form.
Exam Tip: Count bonds carefully in calculations. A chain of n monosaccharides contains n − 1 glycosidic bonds, and its synthesis releases n − 1 water molecules. Hydrolysing it completely needs n − 1 water molecules. Worked example: maltose has Mr = 2 × 180 − 18 = 342, because one water (Mr 18) is lost when the two glucose molecules (Mr 180 each) join.

For full marks, explain the consequence: it does not lower the water potential of the cell, so water is not drawn in by osmosis and the cell does not swell. Similarly, a single hydrogen bond is weak; the strength of cellulose comes from the very large number of hydrogen bonds between chains.

1.2 Lipids

Lipids are a varied group of molecules that are insoluble in water but dissolve in organic solvents such as ethanol. Like carbohydrates they contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, but they have far less oxygen relative to carbon and hydrogen.

1.2 (i) Synthesis of a triglyceride

A triglyceride is made from one molecule of glycerol (a three-carbon alcohol with three –OH groups) and three fatty acids. Each fatty acid has a carboxyl group (–COOH) attached to a hydrocarbon chain, usually written R. In a condensation reaction, the –OH of a fatty acid carboxyl group reacts with the H of one of the glycerol –OH groups; water is released and an ester bond (–COO–) forms. This happens three times, so each triglyceride contains three ester bonds and three water molecules are released. Lipase enzymes hydrolyse ester bonds to reverse the reaction.

Exam Tip: Name the bond precisely: triglycerides contain ester bonds, not glycosidic or peptide bonds. For "describe how a triglyceride is formed", four marking points usually appear: one glycerol and three fatty acids; condensation; three water molecules removed; three ester bonds formed.

1.2 (iii) Energy storage, waterproofing and insulation

  • Energy storage: fatty acid chains are long hydrocarbons with a very high proportion of C–H bonds and little oxygen. Oxidising them in respiration releases about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate (roughly 37 kJ g−1 compared with about 16–17 kJ g−1). Triglycerides are insoluble, so they do not affect the water potential of cells, and they are stored without the water that binds to glycogen, so they are a light, compact store. This matters for animals that move, and for seeds that need to be small. Their oxidation also releases metabolic water, useful to desert animals.
  • Waterproofing: because lipids are hydrophobic, a layer of lipid repels water. Waxes (lipids made from fatty acids and long-chain alcohols) form the cuticle on leaves and the waxy layer on insect exoskeletons, reducing water loss by evaporation. Oily secretions keep mammal fur and bird feathers water-repellent.
  • Insulation: fat is a poor conductor of heat, so a thick layer of adipose tissue under the skin (e.g. blubber in whales and seals) reduces heat loss. Lipids in the myelin sheath around many neurones act as electrical insulation, speeding up nerve impulses.

1.2 (iv) Phospholipids and cell membranes

In a phospholipid, glycerol is bonded to only two fatty acids; the third –OH of glycerol is bonded to a phosphate group (often with a further small charged group attached). The result has two very different ends:

  • a hydrophilic "head": the charged phosphate group is attracted to water;
  • two hydrophobic "tails": the uncharged fatty acid chains are repelled by water.

A molecule with a water-loving part and a water-hating part is described as amphipathic. When phospholipids are placed in water, they arrange themselves so that the heads face the water and the tails are hidden from it. In cells this produces a phospholipid bilayer: two layers with heads facing the watery cytoplasm and tissue fluid on either side and tails pointing inwards to form a hydrophobic core.

How the properties of phospholipids suit their role in membranes:

  • The hydrophobic core is a barrier to water-soluble substances: ions and polar molecules such as glucose cannot easily pass through, so the membrane is partially permeable and the cell can control what enters and leaves using proteins.
  • Small non-polar molecules (O2, CO2) and lipid-soluble substances can diffuse directly through the bilayer.
  • The bilayer forms spontaneously and reseals itself if damaged, because exposing tails to water is unstable.
  • Phospholipids are not bonded to each other, so they can move sideways: the membrane is fluid and flexible, allowing vesicles to form and fuse and cells to change shape. More unsaturated tails make the membrane more fluid. Proteins float among the phospholipids (the fluid mosaic arrangement).
Exam Tip: Do not write that the phospholipid head is "hydrophilic because it likes water". Say that the phosphate group is charged (polar) so it forms attractions with water molecules, whereas the fatty acid tails are non-polar. When explaining why phospholipids form a bilayer, say which part faces where and why.

1.3 Proteins

1.3 (i) Structure of an amino acid

Every amino acid has the same basic structure around a central carbon atom (the α-carbon), to which four groups are attached:

  • an amine group, –NH2;
  • a carboxyl group, –COOH;
  • a hydrogen atom, –H;
  • a variable R group (side chain).

There are 20 amino acids commonly found in proteins, and they differ only in their R groups. R groups may be small or large, charged, polar or non-polar (hydrophobic), and some contain sulfur. You do not need to learn the structures of individual amino acids.

H2N – CH(R) – COOH

1.3 (ii) Forming polypeptides

Two amino acids join in a condensation reaction between the carboxyl group of one and the amine group of the other. The –OH from the carboxyl group and an H from the amine group are removed as water, and a peptide bond (–CO–NH–) forms. Two amino acids make a dipeptide; many make a polypeptide. The chain always has a free amine group at one end and a free carboxyl group at the other. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a specific three-dimensional shape. Peptide bonds are broken by hydrolysis, catalysed by protease enzymes.

Exam Tip: In the cell, peptide bonds are formed at the ribosome during translation (1.4 vi). A polypeptide of 150 amino acids contains 149 peptide bonds, and 149 water molecules are released when it is made.

1.3 (iii) Bonds that hold proteins in shape

Once a chain has been made, it folds. The final shape is held by bonds and interactions, most of them between R groups:

BondBetweenStrength and sensitivity
Hydrogen bondsSlightly positive H atoms and slightly negative O or N atoms, both in the backbone (–NH and C=O) and between polar R groupsIndividually weak, but there are very many of them; broken by heat and by pH changes
Ionic bondsR groups carrying opposite charges (e.g. –NH3+ and –COO−)Stronger than hydrogen bonds, but disrupted by changes in pH, which alter the charges on R groups
Disulfide bonds (bridges)The sulfur atoms of two cysteine R groups, forming a covalent S–S linkStrong covalent bonds; not broken by moderate heat, so they give the most stable parts of a structure

In addition, hydrophobic interactions cause non-polar R groups to cluster together away from water, usually in the centre of a soluble protein, while polar and charged R groups end up on the outside facing water.

1.3 (iv) Levels of protein structure and fibrous vs globular proteins

  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide, held by peptide bonds. It is set by the gene. Because the primary structure decides where every R group sits, it determines all the higher levels of structure. Changing a single amino acid can change the shape and function of a protein.
  • Secondary structure: regular local folding of the chain, held by hydrogen bonds between the –NH and C=O groups of the backbone. The two main forms are the α-helix (a coil) and the β-pleated sheet (sections of chain lying side by side).
  • Tertiary structure: the overall three-dimensional folding of the whole polypeptide, held by hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions between R groups. This gives a protein its precise shape, such as the shape of an enzyme active site.
  • Quaternary structure: the arrangement of two or more polypeptide chains in one functional protein, held by the same types of bond. It can include non-protein prosthetic groups, such as the haem groups in haemoglobin.

1.3 (v) Collagen and haemoglobin: structure and function

Collagen is the main structural protein of tendons, ligaments, skin, bone, cartilage and artery walls.

  • Each molecule consists of three polypeptide chains, each a tightly wound helix, twisted around each other into a rope-like triple helix.
  • Glycine, the amino acid with the smallest R group (a single H), occurs at every third position. This lets the three chains pack very closely together.
  • The chains are held together by many hydrogen bonds.
  • Neighbouring collagen molecules are joined by covalent cross-links, and they are staggered so that their ends do not line up. This avoids weak points. Molecules combine into fibrils, and fibrils into fibres.
  • Result: very high tensile strength (it resists being stretched), flexibility and insolubility, ideal for transmitting the pull of muscle to bone through tendons or for withstanding the stretch in artery walls.

Haemoglobin is the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells.

  • It is a globular protein with quaternary structure: four polypeptide chains, two α-globin and two β-globin.
  • Each chain has a haem group (a prosthetic group) containing an iron ion (Fe2+). Each haem binds one O2 molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule carries up to four O2 molecules.
  • Hydrophobic R groups point into the centre of the molecule and hydrophilic R groups point outwards, so haemoglobin is soluble in the cytoplasm of red blood cells, where it is packed at high concentration.
  • When one O2 binds, the shape of the molecule changes slightly, making it easier for the next O2 to bind. This is cooperative binding, and it lets haemoglobin load oxygen readily in the lungs and unload it readily in respiring tissues.
  • The precise tertiary and quaternary structure is essential: a single amino acid change in the β-chain causes sickle cell anaemia (see 1.4 ix).
Exam Tip: When a question says "explain how the structure of collagen is related to its function", link each feature to a property: glycine every third residue → tight packing; hydrogen bonds and cross-links → strength; staggered molecules → no weak points; insoluble → stays in place in tissues. Listing features without the link scores poorly.

1.4 DNA and Protein Synthesis

1.4 (i) The structure of DNA

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a polymer of nucleotides. Each DNA nucleotide has three parts joined by covalent bonds:

  • a phosphate group;
  • the pentose sugar deoxyribose;
  • one of four nitrogen-containing bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) or thymine (T).

The bases fall into two groups by size. Purines (adenine and guanine) have a double-ring structure. Pyrimidines (cytosine and thymine, plus uracil in RNA) have a smaller single ring. Every base pair contains one purine and one pyrimidine, so all pairs are the same width and the two strands stay a constant distance apart.

Nucleotides are joined by condensation reactions between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next, forming phosphodiester bonds. This produces a sugar-phosphate backbone with the bases sticking out to one side. A DNA molecule has two such strands:

  • The strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and C pairs with G (three hydrogen bonds). This is complementary base pairing.
  • The strands run in opposite directions: they are antiparallel. One runs 5′ to 3′ and the other 3′ to 5′ (the numbers refer to carbons of deoxyribose).
  • The two strands coil around each other to form a double helix.
Exam Tip: Base pairing rules give an easy calculation. In double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals T and the amount of C equals G. If 30% of the bases are adenine, then thymine is also 30%, leaving 40% for C and G together, so cytosine = guanine = 20%. Do not apply this to a single strand or to RNA, where the rule does not hold.

1.4 (ii) Semi-conservative replication

Before a cell divides, its DNA must be copied exactly. Replication is semi-conservative: each new DNA molecule contains one strand from the original molecule and one newly made strand.

  1. DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, separating the two strands. Both strands act as templates.
  2. Free DNA nucleotides in the nucleus are attracted to their complementary exposed bases (A to T, C to G) and are held by hydrogen bonds.
  3. DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand by catalysing condensation reactions that form phosphodiester bonds. DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides in one direction (to the 3′ end of the growing strand), so the two new strands are built in opposite directions.
  4. On one template the new strand is built continuously. On the other it is built in short sections, which DNA ligase joins by forming phosphodiester bonds between them, creating a continuous strand.
  5. Each daughter molecule rewinds into a double helix: one old strand and one new.
Exam Tip: Keep the enzymes straight. Helicase breaks hydrogen bonds; DNA polymerase forms phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides of the new strand; the hydrogen bonds between new and template bases form without an enzyme. Ligase joins fragments. Saying "DNA polymerase pairs the bases" loses the mark.

1.4 (iii) What a gene is

Gene: A section of a DNA molecule whose sequence of bases codes for the sequence of amino acids in one polypeptide. (Some genes instead code for functional RNA such as tRNA or rRNA.) The position of a gene on a chromosome is its locus.

1.4 (iv) and (v) The structure of mRNA and tRNA

RNA differs from DNA in three ways: its nucleotides contain ribose instead of deoxyribose, it uses the base uracil (U) instead of thymine, and it is usually single-stranded. RNA nucleotides are also joined by phosphodiester bonds into a sugar-phosphate backbone.

1.4 (vi) Transcription and translation

Transcription (in the nucleus) makes an mRNA copy of a gene:

  1. The hydrogen bonds between the two DNA strands are broken at the gene, so the double helix unwinds and separates locally.
  2. Only one strand is used as the template. This is the antisense (template) strand. The other, the sense (coding) strand, has the same base sequence as the mRNA that will be made, except that it has T where the mRNA has U.
  3. Free RNA nucleotides pair with their complementary bases on the antisense strand (A with U, T with A, C with G, G with C).
  4. RNA polymerase moves along the template and joins the RNA nucleotides with phosphodiester bonds.
  5. At the end of the gene, the mRNA detaches, the DNA rewinds, and the mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore to reach a ribosome. (In eukaryotes the first transcript is modified before it leaves: non-coding sections called introns are removed.)

Translation (at a ribosome in the cytoplasm or on rough endoplasmic reticulum) uses the mRNA to build a polypeptide:

  1. The mRNA binds to a ribosome. Translation begins at the start codon, AUG.
  2. A tRNA with the complementary anticodon (UAC), carrying its specific amino acid (methionine), pairs with the start codon by hydrogen bonding.
  3. A second tRNA pairs with the next codon. The ribosome holds two tRNAs at a time.
  4. A peptide bond forms between the two amino acids by condensation, catalysed within the ribosome. ATP provides energy for the process.
  5. The ribosome moves along the mRNA by one codon. The first tRNA is released and can collect another amino acid; a new tRNA arrives at the next codon.
  6. This repeats until the ribosome reaches a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA), which has no tRNA. The polypeptide is released and folds into its final shape.
Exam Tip: Give sequence-conversion questions careful attention. If the antisense (template) strand reads TAC GGA, the mRNA is AUG CCU, the tRNA anticodons are UAC GGA, and the sense strand is ATG CCT. The sense strand and mRNA match (T → U); the antisense strand and anticodons match (T → U).

1.4 (vii) The genetic code

  • Triplet code: each amino acid is coded by a sequence of three bases, a codon on the mRNA. With four bases there are 43 = 64 possible codons, easily enough for 20 amino acids. (A doublet code would give only 42 = 16.)
  • Start and stop codons: AUG signals the start of translation (and codes for methionine); UAA, UAG and UGA mark the end of the polypeptide and code for no amino acid.
  • Degenerate: most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon. There are 61 codons for amino acids but only 20 amino acids.
  • Non-overlapping: each base belongs to only one codon; the sequence is read in consecutive, separate triplets from a fixed starting point.
  • Universal: the same codons code for the same amino acids in almost all organisms.
  • Not all DNA codes for proteins: much of the genome is non-coding. It includes introns, regulatory sequences that control when genes are switched on, genes for tRNA and rRNA, and repeated sequences. In humans only around 1–2% of the genome codes for protein.

1.4 (viii) Gene mutations

Gene mutation: A change in the base sequence of DNA within a gene. Mutations arise mainly from errors during DNA replication, and the rate is increased by mutagens such as ionising radiation, UV light and certain chemicals.
TypeWhat happensTypical effect on the polypeptide
SubstitutionOne base is replaced by a different baseChanges only one codon. Because of degeneracy it may be silent (same amino acid); it may change one amino acid (missense); or it may create a stop codon (nonsense), giving a shortened polypeptide
InsertionAn extra base is addedCauses a frameshift: every codon after the mutation is read differently, so many amino acids change and a premature stop codon often appears
DeletionA base is lostAlso causes a frameshift, usually with severe effects on the protein

A mutation matters only if it changes the protein's primary structure in a way that changes its tertiary structure and so its function. An insertion or deletion of three bases adds or removes a single amino acid without a frameshift.

1.4 (ix) Point mutations and sickle cell anaemia

A point mutation affects a single base. Sickle cell anaemia is caused by a substitution in the gene for the β-globin chain of haemoglobin:

  • In the sense strand, the triplet GAG becomes GTG (in the mRNA, codon GAG becomes GUG).
  • As a result, the sixth amino acid of the β-chain changes from glutamic acid to valine.
  • Glutamic acid has a charged, hydrophilic R group; valine has a non-polar, hydrophobic R group. This produces a hydrophobic patch on the outside of the molecule.
  • When oxygen levels are low, the abnormal haemoglobin molecules (haemoglobin S) stick together and form long, insoluble fibres inside red blood cells.
  • The red blood cells become rigid and sickle-shaped. They carry less oxygen, can block capillaries (causing pain and tissue damage) and are destroyed quickly, leading to anaemia.

People who are homozygous for the mutant allele have the disease. Heterozygous carriers have sickle cell trait: they are usually healthy and have some protection against malaria, which is why the allele is common in regions where malaria occurs.

Exam Tip: For a mutation chain question, work through every link: change in base sequence → change in codon → different amino acid → different R group → change in bonding and folding (tertiary/quaternary structure) → change in function. Missing the middle steps is the most common reason for losing marks.

1.5 Enzymes

1.5 (i) Enzymes are globular proteins

Enzymes are globular proteins that act as biological catalysts. The polypeptide is folded into a precise tertiary (sometimes quaternary) structure. A small region, the active site, is a cleft or pocket formed by a few amino acids whose R groups can bind the substrate and take part in the reaction. The shape and charge distribution of the active site depend on the bonds holding the tertiary structure, which in turn depend on the primary structure. Because they are globular, most enzymes are soluble and work in aqueous solution.

1.5 (ii) Specificity and the induced fit hypothesis

Each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or a small group of very similar reactions. This specificity exists because only a substrate with a shape and charge pattern complementary to the active site can bind to it and form an enzyme-substrate complex.

The lock and key model pictured the active site as a rigid shape that exactly matches the substrate. The current induced fit hypothesis improves on this:

  1. The active site is not a perfectly matching fixed shape. The substrate binds, and its binding causes the active site to change shape slightly so that it moulds more closely around the substrate.
  2. This change places strain on particular bonds in the substrate (or brings two substrate molecules close together in the right orientation), making the reaction easier.
  3. The products are released, and the active site returns to its original shape, ready for another substrate molecule.

1.5 (iii) Enzymes lower activation energy

Activation energy: The minimum energy that reactants must have for a reaction to start. Enzymes provide an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at a given temperature. The enzyme is unchanged at the end, and the overall energy change of the reaction is the same.

1.5 (iv) Factors affecting the rate of enzyme activity

Temperature. As temperature rises, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide more often, and more collisions result in enzyme-substrate complexes. The rate rises, roughly doubling for every 10 °C rise at lower temperatures (a temperature coefficient, Q10, of about 2). Above the optimum temperature, vibrations become strong enough to break hydrogen and ionic bonds in the tertiary structure. The active site changes shape and is no longer complementary to the substrate, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. This is denaturation, and it is usually permanent. The rate falls steeply. Many human enzymes have optima near 37–40 °C; enzymes of thermophilic bacteria can have optima above 70 °C.

pH. Each enzyme has an optimum pH (e.g. pepsin in the stomach works best near pH 2, while most intracellular enzymes work best near pH 7). A change in H+ concentration alters the charges on R groups, including those in the active site. This disrupts ionic and hydrogen bonds, changing the shape of the active site and preventing the substrate from binding or reacting. Small changes are often reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme.

Substrate concentration. With a fixed amount of enzyme, increasing substrate concentration increases the rate, because collisions between substrate and active sites become more frequent. Eventually all active sites are occupied at any moment: the enzyme is saturated, the rate reaches its maximum (Vmax) and levels off. Enzyme concentration is now the limiting factor.

Enzyme concentration. Provided substrate is in excess, the rate is directly proportional to enzyme concentration, because more active sites are available. If substrate is limited, adding more enzyme eventually has no further effect, since substrate concentration becomes the limiting factor.

Exam Tip: Never say an enzyme is "killed" (it was never alive) or that it "stops working" at low temperatures. At low temperatures the enzyme is inactive but not denatured: warming it restores activity. Above the optimum, name the bonds that break (hydrogen and ionic), say the active site changes shape and that the substrate is no longer complementary, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.
Core Practical 1 (CP1): Investigate a factor affecting the initial rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction. The full method, variables, risk assessment and analysis are on the Core Practicals page (CP1). Key points for this topic: change only one factor (temperature, pH, enzyme concentration or substrate concentration), control the others with water baths and buffers, measure product formed or substrate used over time, and use the results to find the initial rate.

1.5 (v) Measuring the initial rate, and why it matters

The rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction can be followed by measuring either how quickly a product appears (e.g. the volume of oxygen released when catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide, collected in a gas syringe or measuring cylinder) or how quickly a substrate disappears (e.g. starch broken down by amylase, followed with iodine on a spotting tile or by the fall in absorbance in a colorimeter).

If product formed is plotted against time, the curve is steepest at the very start and then flattens as the reaction slows. The initial rate is the gradient at time zero. It is found by drawing a tangent to the curve at t = 0 and calculating its gradient (change in y ÷ change in x).

initial rate = Δy ÷ Δx = 10 cm3 ÷ 25 s = 0.40 cm3 s−1

In this example, the volume after 50 s is about 8.6 cm3, so the mean rate over the first 50 s is only about 0.17 cm3 s−1. That is less than half the initial rate, which shows why a mean rate over a long period misrepresents how fast the enzyme works.

Why the initial rate is used:

  • At the start, the substrate concentration is known and is the same in every trial. As the reaction proceeds, substrate is used up at different speeds under different conditions, so later rates are no longer a fair comparison.
  • Later in the reaction, the rate is limited by falling substrate concentration, and products may accumulate and inhibit the enzyme or the reverse reaction may start to matter. The initial rate reflects only the factor being investigated.
  • If the enzyme denatures gradually (e.g. at high temperature), the initial rate is measured before much denaturation has taken place.

When a reaction is timed to an end point (e.g. the time for starch to disappear), the rate can be expressed as 1/time (units s−1). This is a useful comparative measure but is strictly an average rate, so it only estimates the initial rate.

Exam Tip: To draw a tangent, place the ruler so that it touches the curve at the chosen point without crossing it, and draw a long line so the gradient can be read accurately. Show your working: pick two points on the tangent far apart, state Δy and Δx with units, and give the rate with units (e.g. cm3 s−1) to a sensible number of significant figures. Straight-line graphs follow y = mx + c, where m is the gradient (the rate) and c is the y-intercept.

1.5 (vi) Enzyme inhibition

Inhibition can be reversible (the inhibitor binds weakly and leaves again) or irreversible (the inhibitor binds permanently, often covalently).

End-product inhibition is a form of negative feedback that controls metabolic pathways. In a pathway A → B → C → D, each step catalysed by a different enzyme, the final product D inhibits the enzyme that catalyses the first step. As D builds up, the pathway slows; as D is used up, the inhibition is released and the pathway speeds up again. This avoids wasting substrate and energy on product that is not needed. The inhibition is reversible and is usually non-competitive (the end product binds at an allosteric site). A well-known example is in respiration, where a high concentration of ATP inhibits phosphofructokinase, an enzyme early in glycolysis.

Exam Tip: On a graph of rate against substrate concentration, a competitive inhibitor lowers the curve most at low substrate concentrations, and the curve eventually approaches the uninhibited Vmax. A non-competitive inhibitor lowers the plateau itself. When asked to "explain" a graph, describe the pattern with figures from the axes, then give the reason in terms of active sites.

1.5 (vii) Intracellular and extracellular enzymes

  • Intracellular enzymes work inside the cell that made them, often inside a particular organelle. Examples: DNA polymerase and RNA polymerase in the nucleus; the enzymes of respiration in the cytoplasm and mitochondria; catalase, which breaks down hydrogen peroxide (a toxic by-product of metabolism) into water and oxygen; the enzymes of the Calvin cycle in chloroplasts.
  • Extracellular enzymes are secreted by exocytosis and work outside the cell. Examples: amylase in saliva and from the pancreas; proteases such as pepsin and trypsin; lipase in the small intestine; enzymes secreted by fungi and bacteria that digest organic matter outside their cells (extracellular digestion), with the soluble products then absorbed.

1.7 Water

1.7 (i) The dipole nature of water and hydrogen bonding

A water molecule consists of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms, with the molecule bent at an angle of about 105°. Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does, so the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge (δ−) and each hydrogen carries a slight positive charge (δ+). A molecule with this uneven distribution of charge is a dipole, or polar molecule.

The δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring molecule. This attraction is a hydrogen bond. Each hydrogen bond is weak, but in liquid water there are enormous numbers of them, constantly breaking and re-forming. Together they explain water's unusual and biologically important properties.

Exam Tip: Every water-property answer has the same three-part shape: property → caused by hydrogen bonding / polarity → benefit to a named organism or process. Distinguish the bond types: the O–H bonds within a molecule are covalent; hydrogen bonds are the weak attractions between molecules.

Maths Skills in Topic 1

The specification lists several maths skills that questions on this topic can test. Worked examples:

  • Units: rates must carry units, such as cm3 s−1 of gas or s−1 for 1/time. Convert consistently: 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3; 1 minute = 60 s. A rate of 0.40 cm3 s−1 equals 24 cm3 min−1.
  • Ratios, fractions and percentages: if a DNA sample has 18% guanine, cytosine is 18%, and A + T = 100 − 36 = 64%, so adenine = thymine = 32%. Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100: if a rate rises from 0.25 to 0.40 cm3 s−1, that is a 60% increase.
  • Frequency tables and diagrams: e.g. the table of codons per amino acid in 1.4 (vii), or the number of trials giving each end-point time in an enzyme experiment.
  • Translating between graphs, numbers and algebra: y = mx + c describes a straight line. If product increases linearly with time, m is the rate and c the intercept (ideally zero, since no product is present at t = 0). An enzyme-concentration graph that is linear through the origin shows direct proportionality.
  • Intercepts and rates from graphs: read the intercept where the line crosses an axis; calculate the rate as the gradient of a straight line, or of a tangent to a curve at the point of interest (see 1.5 v).
  • Counting in polymers: a polypeptide of 150 amino acids needs 150 codons (450 bases) in the mRNA, plus a stop codon, giving 453 bases; it contains 149 peptide bonds.

Exam Tip: common misconceptions in Topic 1
  • Hydrolysis uses water; condensation releases it. Do not swap them.
  • Glycogen is made of α-glucose, not β-glucose; only cellulose (of the named polysaccharides) uses β-glucose.
  • The active site and substrate are complementary, not "the same shape".
  • Denatured enzymes have a changed active site; the enzyme is not destroyed or dissolved.
  • Uracil replaces thymine in RNA only; DNA never contains uracil in these questions.
  • The sense strand is not the template; the antisense strand is.
  • A substitution does not always change the protein, because the code is degenerate.
  • Hydrogen bonds hold the two DNA strands together; phosphodiester bonds hold each strand's backbone together.
