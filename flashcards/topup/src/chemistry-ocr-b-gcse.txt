# OCR Twenty First Century Science Chemistry B (J258) — chapters C1–C6, written from the specification.

## C1
What does the particle model say about solids, liquids and gases? ~~ Solids: particles touching in a fixed, regular arrangement, vibrating. Liquids: particles touching but randomly arranged and able to move past each other. Gases: particles far apart, moving quickly in random directions.
What is the difference between a physical change and a chemical change? ~~ A physical change (e.g. a change of state) makes no new substance and is easily reversed; a chemical change makes new substances.
Why do different substances melt and boil at different temperatures? ~~ The stronger the forces between the particles, the more energy must be transferred to overcome them, so the higher the melting or boiling point.
Do individual particles have the same properties as the bulk substance? ~~ No — e.g. a single particle of copper is not coloured or hot or cold; bulk properties come from many particles together.
Give a limitation of the particle model when particles are drawn as inelastic spheres. ~~ It does not show the scale, real shape or movement of particles, ignores the forces between them and assumes the particles bounce without energy loss.
What were the main gases in the Earth's early atmosphere and where did they come from? ~~ Mainly carbon dioxide and water vapour (with some nitrogen and small amounts of other gases), released by intense volcanic activity.
How did the oceans form? ~~ As the Earth cooled, water vapour condensed to form the oceans.
How did oxygen build up in the atmosphere? ~~ Photosynthesising organisms (algae, then plants) took in carbon dioxide and released oxygen.
How did the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere fall? ~~ It was taken in by photosynthesis, dissolved in the oceans, and locked up in sedimentary rocks (e.g. limestone), fossil fuels such as oil and gas.
What evidence is used to work out how the atmosphere has changed? ~~ The types and chemical composition of ancient rocks and fossil evidence of early life.
What is the approximate composition of today's dry air? ~~ About 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, about 1% argon and about 0.04% carbon dioxide.
What causes carbon monoxide and particulates to form from fuels? ~~ Incomplete combustion — burning a fuel in a limited supply of oxygen.
How is sulfur dioxide produced when fuels burn? ~~ Sulfur impurities in the fuel react with oxygen.
How are oxides of nitrogen formed in engines? ~~ At high temperatures nitrogen from the air is oxidised; nitrogen monoxide is then further oxidised in the air.
What harm do sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen cause? ~~ They cause acid rain and breathing problems; nitrogen oxides also help form photochemical smog.
Why is carbon monoxide dangerous? ~~ It is toxic — it reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen.
Name three ways of decreasing pollutant emissions from fuels. ~~ Catalytic converters, low-sulfur petrol and gas scrubbers (e.g. in power station chimneys).
What is oxidation in terms of oxygen? ~~ Gain of oxygen — so combustion of a fuel is an oxidation reaction.
Describe the tests for oxygen, hydrogen and carbon dioxide. ~~ Oxygen relights a glowing splint; hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop with a lighted splint; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky (cloudy).
What is an exothermic reaction? ~~ A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases (e.g. combustion).
What is an endothermic reaction? ~~ A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
What is activation energy? ~~ The minimum energy needed to break bonds so that a reaction can start.
On a reaction profile for an exothermic reaction, where are the products? ~~ Lower in energy than the reactants; the difference is the energy given out.
Is bond breaking exothermic or endothermic? ~~ Bond breaking takes in energy (endothermic); bond making gives out energy (exothermic).
How do you calculate the energy change from bond energies? ~~ Energy change = energy taken in to break bonds − energy given out making bonds. A negative answer means the reaction is exothermic.
How does a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell compare with burning hydrogen? ~~ It uses the same overall reaction and makes only water, but transfers the energy electrically to drive a motor rather than in an engine.
What is the greenhouse effect? ~~ The warm Earth emits infrared radiation; greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane absorb it and re-emit it in all directions, keeping the Earth warmer than it would otherwise be.
What evidence links human activity to climate change? ~~ A correlation between rising atmospheric CO₂ concentration, fossil fuel consumption and global temperatures over the last 200 years.
Give four possible effects of increased CO₂ and methane on the climate. ~~ Changes to where crops can be grown, more extreme weather, melting polar ice and flooding of low-lying land.
Describe how potable water is produced and how chlorine is tested for. ~~ Ground/waste water is filtered (including membrane filtration), aerated, treated with bacteria and chlorinated; salt water can be distilled. Chlorine turns damp blue litmus paper red and then bleaches it white.

## C2
What was Dalton's model of the atom? ~~ Atoms are tiny, solid, indivisible spheres; each element has its own type of atom.
What was Thomson's 'plum pudding' model? ~~ A ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it, after he discovered the electron.
What did Rutherford's experiment show? ~~ Most of the atom is empty space, with a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre that contains most of the mass.
What did Bohr add to the atomic model? ~~ Electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed shells (energy levels).
Give the relative charges and masses of protons, neutrons and electrons. ~~ Proton +1, mass 1; neutron 0, mass 1; electron −1, mass about 1/2000 (very small).
What is the typical size of an atom? ~~ About 10⁻¹⁰ m across; the nucleus is about 100 000 times smaller than the atom.
How do you work out the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom? ~~ Protons = atomic number; electrons = protons (in a neutral atom); neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
What are isotopes? ~~ Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
What is the electron arrangement of sodium (atomic number 11)? ~~ 2.8.1
How is the position of an element in the Periodic Table linked to its electrons? ~~ The group number equals the number of outer-shell electrons; the period number equals the number of occupied shells.
How did Mendeleev arrange the elements? ~~ By relative atomic mass and by their properties, putting similar elements in groups.
Why did Mendeleev leave gaps and swap some elements? ~~ So elements lined up with others with similar properties; he predicted that the gaps were undiscovered elements and predicted their properties.
What later evidence supported Mendeleev's table? ~~ Newly discovered elements (e.g. germanium) fitted his gaps and properties, and ordering by atomic number (proton number) explained his reorderings.
Compare the typical physical properties of metals and non-metals. ~~ Metals: shiny, high melting points, dense, good electrical conductors. Non-metals: dull, often low melting points, low density, poor conductors (except graphite).
What are the Group 1 metals like? ~~ Soft metals with low density that are stored under oil; they tarnish quickly in moist air and react vigorously with water and chlorine.
What is the trend in reactivity of Group 1 and why? ~~ Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more easily lost.
What is produced when a Group 1 metal reacts with water? ~~ A metal hydroxide (an alkaline solution) and hydrogen, e.g. 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.
Give the states and colours of chlorine, bromine and iodine at room temperature. ~~ Chlorine: pale green gas. Bromine: red-brown liquid (orange-brown vapour). Iodine: grey-black solid (purple vapour).
What is the trend in reactivity of Group 7 and why? ~~ Reactivity decreases down the group because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so it is harder to gain an electron.
What is a halogen displacement reaction? ~~ A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its metal halide, e.g. chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine.
Why are the Group 0 elements unreactive? ~~ Their atoms have a full outer shell of electrons, so they do not need to gain, lose or share electrons; they exist as single atoms.
What properties do the Group 0 elements have? ~~ Colourless gases at room temperature with very low melting and boiling points.
How does ionic bonding form? ~~ A metal atom transfers electron(s) to a non-metal atom, forming a positive and a negative ion with full outer shells, which attract strongly.
What charge do Group 1 and Group 7 ions have? ~~ Group 1 ions are 1+ (lose one electron); Group 7 ions are 1− (gain one electron).
Describe the structure of an ionic compound. ~~ A giant lattice of positive and negative ions held together by strong electrostatic attractions in all directions.
Why do ionic compounds have high melting points? ~~ A lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
When do ionic compounds conduct electricity? ~~ When molten or dissolved in water, because the ions can move; not when solid, because the ions are fixed.
Give a limitation of dot-and-cross diagrams of ionic compounds. ~~ They do not show the 3-D arrangement, relative sizes of ions, or that the ions form a giant lattice.
What do the state symbols (s), (l), (g) and (aq) mean? ~~ Solid, liquid, gas and aqueous (dissolved in water).
What are the general properties of transition metals? ~~ High melting points and densities, less reactive than Group 1, form coloured ions with different charges (e.g. Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺) and are used as catalysts.

## C3
Describe metallic bonding. ~~ A giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a 'sea' of delocalised electrons; strong electrostatic attraction holds the structure together.
Why are metals malleable and ductile? ~~ The layers of ions can slide over each other while still being held together by the delocalised electrons.
Why do metals conduct electricity and heat? ~~ The delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure.
Why do metals have high melting points? ~~ There is strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons, which takes a lot of energy to overcome.
How can metals be placed in a reactivity series? ~~ By comparing their reactions with water and dilute acid and their displacement reactions with compounds of other metals.
Give the reactivity series from potassium to silver. ~~ Potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, lead, (hydrogen), copper, silver.
Why are some metals more reactive than others? ~~ More reactive metals lose electrons to form positive ions more easily.
What is a metal displacement reaction? ~~ A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a compound, e.g. zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper.
Write the ionic equation for zinc displacing copper from copper sulfate solution. ~~ Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
Which metals can be extracted by heating with carbon? ~~ Metals below carbon in the reactivity series, e.g. zinc, iron, lead and copper.
In extraction with carbon, what is oxidised and what is reduced? ~~ The metal oxide is reduced (loses oxygen) and carbon is oxidised (gains oxygen).
Why are some metals extracted by electrolysis? ~~ They are more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot reduce their oxides (e.g. aluminium).
What are bacterial extraction and phytoextraction? ~~ Biological methods that use bacteria or plants to extract metals (e.g. copper) from low-grade ores or waste; they save energy and new ore but are slow.
What is electrolysis? ~~ The decomposition of an electrolyte (a molten or dissolved ionic compound) by an electric current.
Why must an ionic compound be molten or dissolved to be electrolysed? ~~ So that the ions are free to move to the electrodes.
What forms at each electrode with inert electrodes? ~~ Metals (or hydrogen) form at the cathode (negative); non-metals form at the anode (positive).
What are the products of electrolysis of molten lead bromide? ~~ Lead at the cathode and bromine at the anode.
In electrolysis, what are oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons? ~~ Oxidation is loss of electrons (at the anode); reduction is gain of electrons (at the cathode). OIL RIG.
Write the half equation at the cathode in aluminium extraction. ~~ Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
Why must the carbon anodes be replaced in aluminium extraction? ~~ Oxygen formed at the anode reacts with the hot carbon to form carbon dioxide, so the anodes burn away.
In the electrolysis of an aqueous salt, when does hydrogen form at the cathode? ~~ When the metal is more reactive than hydrogen (e.g. sodium); less reactive metals such as copper or silver are deposited instead.
What forms at the anode in electrolysis of aqueous solutions? ~~ Oxygen usually forms (from hydroxide ions from water), but a concentrated chloride solution gives chlorine.
What are the products of electrolysis of concentrated sodium chloride solution? ~~ Hydrogen at the cathode and chlorine at the anode; sodium hydroxide is left in solution.
What is crude oil and why is it important? ~~ A finite mixture of hydrocarbons; it is the main source of fuels and a feedstock for the petrochemical industry (including polymers).
How does fractional distillation separate crude oil? ~~ Crude oil is vaporised; the column is hot at the bottom and cool at the top, so fractions condense at different heights according to their boiling points.
Why do larger hydrocarbon molecules have higher boiling points? ~~ They have stronger intermolecular forces, so more energy is needed to separate the molecules.
What is the general formula of the alkanes? ~~ CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ — e.g. methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀.
What is a covalent bond? ~~ A strong bond formed when two atoms share a pair of electrons.
Why do simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points? ~~ Only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules need to be overcome; the covalent bonds do not break.
What is cracking and why is it done? ~~ Breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes (e.g. for petrol) and alkenes, which are used to make polymers; it matches supply to demand.

## C4
Name the main classes of material compared in C4. ~~ Glass and clay ceramics, polymers, composites and metals.
What is a composite material? ~~ A material made of two or more materials combined to give useful properties, e.g. reinforced concrete (steel rods in concrete).
Why are ceramics used for plates and insulators? ~~ They are hard, stiff, have high melting points and are electrical insulators, though brittle.
What is an alloy? ~~ A mixture of a metal with other elements (usually other metals or carbon).
Why are alloys harder than pure metals? ~~ Atoms of different sizes disrupt the regular layers, so the layers cannot slide over each other as easily.
What is steel? ~~ An alloy of iron with carbon (and sometimes other metals, e.g. chromium in stainless steel), stronger and harder than iron.
What is addition polymerisation? ~~ Many small alkene monomers join together when their C=C double bonds open, forming one long chain with no other product.
What is the repeating unit of poly(ethene)? ~~ –CH₂–CH₂– (shown in brackets with 'n'), made from ethene monomers CH₂=CH₂.
What is condensation polymerisation? ~~ Monomers with two functional groups each join together, and a small molecule (usually water) is released at each link.
What do polyesters and polyamides form from? ~~ Polyesters: dicarboxylic acids and diols. Polyamides: dicarboxylic acids and diamines.
Name three naturally occurring polymers and their monomers. ~~ DNA (four nucleotides), proteins (amino acids) and starch/cellulose (sugars).
Why does carbon form so many compounds? ~~ Each carbon atom can form four covalent bonds and carbon atoms can bond to each other in chains and rings.
Why do long polymer chains have higher melting points than small molecules? ~~ Longer chains have more intermolecular forces between them, which need more energy to overcome.
What is the structure of diamond? ~~ A giant covalent structure in which each carbon atom is bonded to four others in a rigid 3-D lattice.
Why is diamond very hard with a very high melting point? ~~ Many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
What is the structure of graphite? ~~ Layers of hexagonal rings in which each carbon atom bonds to three others, with weak forces between the layers and one delocalised electron per atom.
Why is graphite soft and slippery and why does it conduct? ~~ The layers slide over each other easily; the delocalised electrons can move through the structure.
What are allotropes? ~~ Different structural forms of the same element, e.g. diamond, graphite, graphene and fullerenes.
Do giant covalent substances conduct electricity? ~~ Usually not (e.g. diamond), because there are no free electrons or ions — graphite and graphene are exceptions.
What size are nanoparticles? ~~ 1–100 nm (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m) — similar in scale to molecules, containing a few hundred atoms.
How does surface area to volume ratio change as particle size decreases? ~~ It increases — e.g. halving the side of a cube doubles its surface area to volume ratio.
Why are nanoparticles good catalysts? ~~ Their very high surface area to volume ratio exposes many more atoms to react.
What are fullerenes used for? ~~ Hollow cages (e.g. buckyballs) can carry drug molecules into the body; nanotubes are used as molecular sieves and to strengthen materials such as sports equipment.
What are the properties of graphene? ~~ A single layer of graphite one atom thick; very strong, and an excellent conductor of electricity and heat.
What are the possible risks of nanoparticles? ~~ Their tiny size lets them enter cells and tissues, and their large surface area may make them reactive or toxic; their long-term health effects are not well known.
What conditions are needed for iron to rust? ~~ Both oxygen (air) and water.
How do barrier methods prevent rusting? ~~ Paint, oil, grease or plastic keep air and water away from the iron.
How does sacrificial protection work? ~~ A more reactive metal such as zinc is attached or coated; it is oxidised instead of the iron, even if the coating is scratched.
What stages are considered in a life-cycle assessment (LCA)? ~~ Extracting and processing raw materials, manufacture, transport, use, and disposal (landfill, incineration, recycling) — including water, energy and environmental impact.
Why is reusing a PET bottle better than recycling it? ~~ Reuse needs less energy than breaking down and reprocessing the material; recycling still saves crude oil compared with making new PET.

## C5
What is a pure substance in chemistry? ~~ A single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance.
How does 'pure' in everyday language differ from the chemical meaning? ~~ Everyday 'pure' means natural or with nothing added (e.g. 'pure orange juice'), which can still be a mixture.
What is a formulation? ~~ A mixture designed as a useful product, with components in measured proportions, e.g. paints, medicines, fertilisers.
How can melting point show whether a substance is pure? ~~ A pure substance melts at a sharp, fixed temperature that matches data values; impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a range.
What are the two phases in chromatography? ~~ The stationary phase (e.g. paper) and the mobile phase (the solvent); substances separate by their distribution between the two.
How do you calculate an Rf value? ~~ Rf = distance moved by the substance ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
How does chromatography show whether a substance is pure? ~~ A pure substance gives a single spot in all solvents; a mixture gives two or more spots.
When is a locating agent needed in chromatography? ~~ When the substances are colourless, so the spots can be seen after the chromatogram has run.
Why might a non-aqueous solvent be used in chromatography? ~~ Some substances are insoluble in water, so another solvent (e.g. ethanol) is needed to move them.
How is an insoluble solid separated from a liquid? ~~ Filtration — the solid stays on the filter paper as the residue and the liquid passes through as the filtrate.
How is a soluble solid obtained from its solution as crystals? ~~ Crystallisation — warm to evaporate some solvent until crystals start to form, then leave to cool and filter off the crystals.
When is simple distillation used? ~~ To separate a solvent from a solution, e.g. pure water from salt water.
When is fractional distillation used? ~~ To separate a mixture of miscible liquids with different boiling points, e.g. ethanol and water or crude oil.
Why is representative sampling needed in analysis? ~~ So the sample tested has the same composition as the bulk material and any variation is detected.
Give the flame test colours for Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cu²⁺. ~~ Lithium crimson red, sodium yellow, potassium lilac, calcium orange-red, copper green (blue-green).
What precipitates do Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ give with sodium hydroxide solution? ~~ Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ orange-brown.
How do you distinguish Ca²⁺ and Zn²⁺ with sodium hydroxide? ~~ Both give a white precipitate; the zinc hydroxide precipitate dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide, the calcium one does not.
How do you test for carbonate ions? ~~ Add dilute acid — bubbles of gas form that turn limewater milky (carbon dioxide).
How do you test for chloride, bromide and iodide ions? ~~ Add dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate solution: chloride gives a white precipitate, bromide cream, iodide yellow.
How do you test for sulfate ions? ~~ Add dilute hydrochloric acid then barium chloride solution — a white precipitate of barium sulfate forms.
How is emission spectroscopy used to identify elements? ~~ A hot sample emits light with a unique pattern of lines; matching the lines to reference spectra identifies the elements.
What are the advantages of instrumental methods of analysis? ~~ They are more sensitive, faster and more accurate, and record data automatically — but are expensive.
What is the law of conservation of mass? ~~ No atoms are made or lost in a reaction, so the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants.
Why can the mass appear to change in an open container? ~~ A gas may escape (mass decreases) or a gas from the air may react with a reactant (mass increases).
How do you calculate relative formula mass (Mr)? ~~ Add the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula, e.g. Mr of H₂O = 2 + 16 = 18.
What is the Avogadro constant? ~~ 6.02 × 10²³ — the number of particles in one mole of a substance.
How do you calculate the number of moles from a mass? ~~ Moles = mass (g) ÷ relative formula mass.
What volume does one mole of any gas occupy at RTP? ~~ 24 dm³ (moles of gas = volume in dm³ ÷ 24).
How do you calculate concentration in mol/dm³? ~~ Concentration = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution in dm³.
How is a titration carried out for precise, accurate results? ~~ Pipette a fixed volume of alkali into a flask, add indicator, add acid from a burette with swirling until the colour just changes; do a rough titre then repeat until concordant (within 0.10 cm³) and take the mean.

## C6
What are the products when an acid reacts with a metal? ~~ A salt and hydrogen, e.g. magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen.
What are the products when an acid reacts with a metal carbonate? ~~ A salt, water and carbon dioxide.
What are the products when an acid reacts with an alkali? ~~ A salt and water (neutralisation).
Which salts do hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acid make? ~~ Chlorides, nitrates and sulfates respectively.
What is the ionic equation for neutralisation? ~~ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
How do you make a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt from an insoluble base? ~~ Add excess base to warm acid, filter off the excess, heat the filtrate to evaporate some water, leave to crystallise, then filter and dry the crystals.
How is an insoluble salt made? ~~ Mix two solutions that contain its ions (precipitation), filter, wash the precipitate with distilled water and dry it.
How is pH measured? ~~ With universal indicator (compare the colour with a chart) or with a pH meter.
What is the difference between a strong and a weak acid? ~~ A strong acid is completely ionised in solution; a weak acid is only partly ionised.
What is the difference between concentrated and dilute? ~~ Concentration is the amount of acid per volume of solution — not the same as strength.
How does pH change if the hydrogen ion concentration increases by a factor of 10? ~~ The pH decreases by 1.
Why do weak acids react more slowly than strong acids of the same concentration? ~~ They have a lower concentration of H⁺ ions.
Name four factors that affect the rate of a reaction. ~~ Temperature, concentration (or pressure for gases), surface area of solids, and catalysts.
How does increasing temperature increase the rate? ~~ Particles move faster, so they collide more often and, more importantly, more collisions have energy greater than the activation energy.
Why does increasing concentration or pressure increase the rate? ~~ There are more particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent.
Why does powdering a solid increase the rate? ~~ Smaller pieces have a larger surface area to volume ratio, so more particles are exposed and collisions are more frequent.
What is a catalyst? ~~ A substance that increases the rate of a reaction but is unchanged chemically and in mass at the end.
How does a catalyst work? ~~ It provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
What are enzymes? ~~ Proteins that act as biological catalysts, working best within a narrow range of temperature and pH.
How can the rate of a reaction that produces a gas be measured? ~~ Measure the volume of gas with a gas syringe (or collection over water), or measure the loss in mass on a balance, at regular time intervals.
How can rate be measured for a reaction that forms a precipitate or changes colour? ~~ Time how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear, or follow the colour change by eye or with a colorimeter.
How is rate found from a graph of product against time? ~~ The gradient of the curve; the gradient of a tangent gives the rate at a particular time.
What does the symbol ⇌ mean? ~~ The reaction is reversible — it can go in both directions.
What is dynamic equilibrium? ~~ In a closed system, the forward and reverse reactions happen at the same rate, so the amounts of reactants and products stay constant.
Does a catalyst change the yield of a reversible reaction? ~~ No — it increases the rate of both forward and reverse reactions, so equilibrium is reached faster but the yield is unchanged.
What is the equation for the Haber process? ~~ N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) — nitrogen from the air, hydrogen from natural gas and steam.
What conditions are used in the Haber process? ~~ About 450 °C, about 200 atmospheres and an iron catalyst; unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled.
Why is a compromise temperature used in the Haber process? ~~ The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature gives a higher yield but too slow a rate; very high pressures are also expensive and risky.
Why are fertilisers needed and what problem can they cause? ~~ They replace nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium taken up by crops; overuse washes them into rivers, causing eutrophication.
How do you calculate atom economy? ~~ Atom economy = (Mr of desired product ÷ total Mr of all reactants) × 100%; a higher atom economy is more sustainable.
