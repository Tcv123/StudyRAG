## 1.1
What is a market? ~~ Any place or system where buyers and sellers come together to exchange goods and services.
What is the difference between a physical and an electronic market? ~~ Physical markets are places where buyers and sellers meet face to face; electronic markets operate online.
What is a dynamic market? ~~ A market that changes rapidly because of innovation, changing tastes or new competitors.
How has online retailing changed markets? ~~ It has increased competition, price transparency and convenience, and reduced barriers to entry, but hit high-street retailers.
What is a competitive advantage? ~~ A feature that allows a business to outperform rivals, e.g. lower costs, a strong brand or unique products.
What is product differentiation? ~~ Making a product distinctive from competitors' through design, quality, branding or features.
What is adding value? ~~ Increasing the difference between the cost of inputs and the price customers are willing to pay, e.g. through branding, convenience or quality.
How is market growth calculated? ~~ (Change in market size ÷ original market size) × 100.
What is a sample in market research? ~~ A group of people selected from the target population to represent it in research.
What is random sampling? ~~ Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.
What is quota sampling? ~~ Selecting a set number of respondents from each segment of the population, e.g. by age or gender.
What is sampling bias? ~~ When the sample doesn't represent the population, leading to inaccurate results.
What is a focus group? ~~ A small group discussion led by a facilitator to gather qualitative views on a product or idea.
What is a questionnaire's main limitation? ~~ Respondents may give inaccurate or biased answers, and poorly designed questions lead to misleading data.
What is the role of ICT in market research? ~~ Online surveys, social media analytics and loyalty card data make research faster, cheaper and more detailed.
What is big data in market research? ~~ Very large data sets from online activity and transactions, analysed to identify customer patterns and trends.
What is demographic segmentation? ~~ Dividing a market by characteristics such as age, gender, income and family size.
What is geographic segmentation? ~~ Dividing a market by location, e.g. region, country or urban/rural.
What is psychographic segmentation? ~~ Dividing a market by lifestyle, personality, values and interests.
What is behavioural segmentation? ~~ Dividing a market by buying behaviour, e.g. usage rate, brand loyalty and occasion.
What are the benefits of market orientation? ~~ Products meet customer needs, so demand is higher and the firm can respond to market changes.
What are the benefits of product orientation? ~~ It can lead to innovative, high-quality products and technological breakthroughs.

## 1.2
What is demand? ~~ The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a period.
What factors shift the demand curve? ~~ Changes in income, tastes, prices of substitutes and complements, advertising, population and seasonality.
What is supply? ~~ The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at a given price over a period.
What happens to equilibrium when supply increases? ~~ The equilibrium price falls and the equilibrium quantity rises.
What happens to equilibrium when demand falls? ~~ Both the equilibrium price and quantity fall.
What is excess demand? ~~ When quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price, putting upward pressure on price.
What is excess supply? ~~ When quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, putting downward pressure on price.
What does a PED of −0.5 mean? ~~ Demand is price-inelastic: a 10% rise in price leads to a 5% fall in quantity demanded.
What does a PED of −2 mean? ~~ Demand is price-elastic: a 10% rise in price leads to a 20% fall in quantity demanded.
Why should a firm with inelastic demand raise prices? ~~ The percentage fall in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage rise in price, so total revenue increases.
Why should a firm with elastic demand cut prices? ~~ The percentage rise in quantity demanded exceeds the percentage fall in price, so revenue increases.
What does a positive YED of more than 1 mean? ~~ The product is a luxury (income-elastic): demand rises proportionately more than income.
What does a YED between 0 and 1 mean? ~~ The product is a normal necessity: demand rises less than proportionately with income.
What does a negative YED mean? ~~ The product is an inferior good: demand falls as income rises.
Why is YED important for businesses? ~~ It helps predict how demand will change during booms and recessions, informing production and marketing plans.
What factors influence YED? ~~ Whether the product is a necessity or luxury, and the proportion of income spent on it.
What is the effect of an indirect tax on supply? ~~ It raises producers' costs, shifting the supply curve to the left, raising price and reducing quantity.
What is the effect of a subsidy on supply? ~~ It lowers producers' costs, shifting supply to the right, lowering price and increasing quantity.
Why do weather conditions affect supply? ~~ They affect the output of agricultural products, e.g. poor harvests reduce supply.
How does technology affect supply? ~~ New technology usually lowers costs and increases productivity, shifting supply to the right.
How can a firm make demand for its product more inelastic? ~~ Branding, differentiation, building customer loyalty and reducing the availability of substitutes.
What are the limitations of using PED? ~~ It is hard to calculate accurately, it changes over time, and it assumes other factors stay constant.

## 1.3
What is the marketing mix? ~~ The 4Ps: product, price, place and promotion (7Ps for services add people, process and physical evidence).
What are the elements of the design mix? ~~ Function, aesthetics and economic manufacture (cost).
What is ethical sourcing? ~~ Buying materials from suppliers who treat workers fairly and protect the environment.
What is a USP? ~~ A unique selling point: a feature that makes a product different from competitors.
What is above-the-line promotion? ~~ Promotion through mass media such as TV, radio, cinema and online display advertising.
What is below-the-line promotion? ~~ Promotion not using mass media, e.g. sales promotions, direct mail, public relations and sponsorship.
What is viral marketing? ~~ Marketing that spreads rapidly through social media sharing.
What is social media marketing? ~~ Using platforms such as Instagram and TikTok to promote products and engage customers.
What is influencer marketing? ~~ Paying individuals with a large online following to promote products.
What is price skimming? ~~ Launching a new product at a high price to maximise revenue from early adopters, then reducing it over time.
What is penetration pricing? ~~ Setting a low initial price to gain market share quickly, then raising it later.
What is psychological pricing? ~~ Setting prices that appear lower, e.g. £9.99 instead of £10.
What is competitive pricing? ~~ Setting prices based on those charged by competitors.
What is multichannel distribution? ~~ Selling through several channels, e.g. shops, website and apps.
What is a retailer? ~~ A business that sells products directly to consumers.
What is a wholesaler? ~~ A business that buys in bulk from manufacturers and sells in smaller quantities to retailers.
What is a question mark in the Boston Matrix? ~~ A product with low market share in a high-growth market, needing investment to grow.
What is a star in the Boston Matrix? ~~ A product with high market share in a high-growth market.
What is a cash cow? ~~ A product with high market share in a low-growth market, generating cash to fund other products.
What is a dog in the Boston Matrix? ~~ A product with low market share in a low-growth market, often discontinued.
What is marketing strategy for a mass market? ~~ Wide distribution, mass media promotion, competitive pricing and a standardised product.
What is marketing strategy for a niche market? ~~ Targeted promotion, specialist distribution, premium pricing and a differentiated product.

## 1.4
What is workforce planning? ~~ Predicting the future number and skills of workers a business needs and planning how to meet them.
What is a part-time worker? ~~ An employee who works fewer hours than full-time staff.
What is a temporary contract? ~~ An employment contract for a fixed period or a specific task.
What is a zero-hours contract? ~~ A contract where the employer doesn't guarantee a minimum number of hours.
What is multi-skilling? ~~ Training workers in several skills so they can perform different tasks, increasing flexibility.
What is outsourcing in HR? ~~ Using external firms to carry out tasks previously done in-house, e.g. payroll.
What is induction training? ~~ Training for new staff to introduce them to the business, its procedures and colleagues.
What is on-the-job training? ~~ Training while working, e.g. shadowing experienced staff.
What is off-the-job training? ~~ Training away from the workplace, e.g. college courses.
What is a matrix structure? ~~ An organisational structure where staff report to both a functional manager and a project manager.
What is delegation? ~~ Passing authority to a subordinate to carry out a task.
What is centralisation? ~~ Decision-making concentrated at the top of the organisation.
What is decentralisation? ~~ Decision-making authority passed down to lower levels or branches.
What is Taylor's view of motivation? ~~ Workers are motivated by money; work should be broken into simple tasks with piece-rate pay (scientific management).
What is Mayo's view of motivation? ~~ Workers are motivated by social factors, teamwork and attention from managers (Hawthorne effect).
What are Herzberg's hygiene factors? ~~ Factors that prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate, e.g. pay, working conditions and company policy.
What are Herzberg's motivators? ~~ Factors that increase job satisfaction, e.g. achievement, recognition, responsibility and the work itself.
What is job enrichment? ~~ Giving workers more responsibility and challenging tasks to increase motivation.
What is job rotation? ~~ Moving workers between different tasks to reduce boredom.
What is an autocratic leadership style? ~~ The leader makes decisions alone, with little input from staff.
What is a democratic leadership style? ~~ The leader involves staff in decision-making.
What is a laissez-faire leadership style? ~~ The leader gives staff freedom to make decisions with minimal guidance.

## 1.5
What is enterprise? ~~ The willingness and ability to take risks to start and run a business.
What is a business idea's source? ~~ Personal experience, hobbies, gaps in the market, and copying ideas from elsewhere.
What are the risks of starting a business? ~~ Financial loss, failure, stress and lack of income.
What are the rewards of starting a business? ~~ Profit, independence, personal satisfaction and flexibility.
What is unlimited liability? ~~ The owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts.
What is limited liability? ~~ Shareholders can only lose the amount they invested in the business.
What is a partnership? ~~ A business owned by two to twenty people, usually with unlimited liability, sharing profits.
What is a private limited company? ~~ A business owned by shareholders with limited liability; shares cannot be sold to the public.
What is a public limited company? ~~ A company whose shares are traded on the stock exchange.
What are the advantages of becoming a plc? ~~ Access to large amounts of finance, increased status, and limited liability.
What are the disadvantages of becoming a plc? ~~ Risk of takeover, loss of control, cost of flotation, and pressure for short-term results.
What is a franchise? ~~ A business arrangement where a franchisee pays to use the franchisor's brand, products and systems.
What are the benefits of franchising to the franchisee? ~~ An established brand, training and support, and lower risk of failure.
What is a social enterprise? ~~ A business with social or environmental objectives that reinvests profits into its mission.
What is a lifestyle business? ~~ A business set up mainly to provide the owner with a desired lifestyle and income, not to grow.
What is a mutual? ~~ A business owned by its members (customers or employees), e.g. building societies.
What are the qualities of a successful entrepreneur? ~~ Creativity, resilience, risk-taking, hard work, and initiative.
Why do some people become entrepreneurs? ~~ Independence, profit, personal satisfaction, and to make a difference.
What is a barrier to entrepreneurship? ~~ Lack of finance, fear of failure, lack of skills, and regulation.
What is the role of government in supporting entrepreneurs? ~~ Grants, loans, tax reliefs, advice, and reducing regulation.
What is business survival? ~~ The ability of a business to continue operating, which is the main objective for many new businesses.
Why might an entrepreneur find it hard to delegate? ~~ They may be reluctant to give up control of the business they created.

## 2.1
What is retained profit? ~~ Profit kept in the business after tax and dividends, used for investment.
What is sale of assets as a source of finance? ~~ Selling assets such as property or machinery the business no longer needs to raise cash.
What is a bank loan? ~~ A sum of money borrowed for a fixed period with interest.
What is a mortgage? ~~ A long-term loan secured on property.
What is share capital? ~~ Finance raised by selling shares in the company.
What are the advantages of share capital? ~~ No interest payments, and it doesn't need to be repaid.
What are the disadvantages of share capital? ~~ Loss of control and dividends expected by shareholders.
What is a business angel? ~~ A wealthy individual who invests in small businesses in return for equity and often provides advice.
What is crowdfunding? ~~ Raising money from many people, usually online.
What is peer-to-peer lending? ~~ Borrowing money directly from individuals through an online platform.
What is leasing? ~~ Renting assets such as vehicles and equipment rather than buying them.
What is trade credit? ~~ An agreement with suppliers to pay for goods at a later date.
What is a government grant? ~~ Money given by the government that doesn't have to be repaid, often to support small businesses or particular areas.
What is an overdraft? ~~ A flexible short-term borrowing facility on a bank account.
What is the difference between short-term and long-term finance? ~~ Short-term finance is for day-to-day needs (overdrafts, trade credit); long-term finance is for investment (loans, shares).
What is the purpose of a business plan? ~~ It sets out the objectives, strategies and financial forecasts, helping to raise finance and guide decisions.
What does a business plan include? ~~ The business idea, market research, marketing plan, financial forecasts and management team.
What is a cash-flow forecast? ~~ A prediction of cash inflows and outflows over a period.
What is net cash flow? ~~ Cash inflows minus cash outflows in a period.
What is the closing balance? ~~ Opening balance plus net cash flow.
Why are cash-flow forecasts useful? ~~ They identify periods of cash shortage so the business can arrange finance in advance.
What are the limitations of cash-flow forecasts? ~~ They rely on estimates that may be inaccurate, and external changes can make them outdated.

## 2.2
What is a sales forecast? ~~ A prediction of future sales based on past data, market research and trends.
What factors affect sales forecasts? ~~ Consumer trends, economic changes, and competitors' actions.
What is sales volume? ~~ The number of units sold.
What is sales revenue? ~~ Price × quantity sold.
What is total cost? ~~ Fixed costs + variable costs.
What is a fixed cost? ~~ A cost that doesn't change with output, e.g. rent.
What is a variable cost? ~~ A cost that changes directly with output, e.g. raw materials.
What is contribution per unit? ~~ Selling price − variable cost per unit.
What is total contribution? ~~ Contribution per unit × number of units sold, or total revenue − total variable costs.
What is the break-even output formula? ~~ Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
What is profit using contribution? ~~ Total contribution − fixed costs.
What is the margin of safety? ~~ Actual output − break-even output.
What happens to break-even if fixed costs rise? ~~ The break-even point increases.
What happens to break-even if price rises? ~~ Contribution per unit rises, so break-even output falls.
What are the benefits of break-even analysis? ~~ It is simple, shows the output needed to cover costs, and helps set targets and assess risk.
What is a budget? ~~ A financial plan for the future, setting targets for revenue and costs.
What is an income budget? ~~ A forecast of expected revenue.
What is an expenditure budget? ~~ A forecast of expected costs.
What is a historical budget? ~~ A budget based on the previous year's figures, adjusted for expected changes.
What is a zero-based budget? ~~ A budget starting from zero each year, with all spending justified.
What is a favourable variance? ~~ When actual results are better than budgeted, e.g. higher revenue or lower costs.
What is an adverse variance? ~~ When actual results are worse than budgeted, e.g. lower revenue or higher costs.

## 2.3
What is gross profit? ~~ Sales revenue − cost of sales.
What is operating profit? ~~ Gross profit − operating expenses.
What is profit for the year? ~~ Operating profit − interest − tax (± other income and expenses).
What is the gross profit margin formula? ~~ (Gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100.
What is the operating profit margin formula? ~~ (Operating profit ÷ revenue) × 100.
What is the profit for the year margin? ~~ (Profit for the year ÷ revenue) × 100.
How can a business increase gross profit margin? ~~ Raise prices, reduce cost of sales by finding cheaper suppliers, or reduce waste.
How can a business increase operating profit margin? ~~ Reduce overheads such as rent, admin and marketing costs.
What is the difference between profit and cash? ~~ Profit is revenue minus costs; cash is money available to the business. A profitable firm can run out of cash.
What is liquidity? ~~ The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts.
What is the current ratio formula? ~~ Current assets ÷ current liabilities.
What is the ideal current ratio? ~~ Between 1.5:1 and 2:1.
What is the acid test ratio formula? ~~ (Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities.
What is the ideal acid test ratio? ~~ About 1:1.
What is working capital? ~~ Current assets − current liabilities.
How can a business improve liquidity? ~~ Reduce stock, chase debtors, extend credit from suppliers, sell assets, or use sale and leaseback.
What is an internal cause of business failure? ~~ Poor management, cash-flow problems, poor planning or overtrading.
What is an external cause of business failure? ~~ Recession, increased competition, changing consumer tastes, or new regulations.
What is overtrading? ~~ Expanding too quickly without enough working capital to fund it.
What is a financial cause of business failure? ~~ Poor cash flow, too much debt, or inadequate finance.
What is a non-financial cause of business failure? ~~ Poor management, weak marketing, or low-quality products.
What is a receivables (debtors) problem? ~~ Customers taking too long to pay, reducing cash available.

## 2.4
What is job production? ~~ Making one-off products to individual customer requirements, e.g. a wedding dress.
What is batch production? ~~ Making a set number of identical products before switching to a different batch.
What is flow production? ~~ Continuous production of standardised products on a production line.
What is cell production? ~~ Production divided into cells or teams, each responsible for a complete unit of work.
What is the labour productivity formula? ~~ Output ÷ number of workers (or hours worked).
What factors influence productivity? ~~ Technology, training, motivation, management and the quality of materials.
What is labour-intensive production? ~~ Production using mainly labour rather than machinery.
What is capital-intensive production? ~~ Production using mainly machinery and technology.
What is the capacity utilisation formula? ~~ (Current output ÷ maximum possible output) × 100.
What are the problems of high capacity utilisation? ~~ Pressure on staff, no time for maintenance, and inability to meet extra orders.
What are the problems of low capacity utilisation? ~~ Higher fixed costs per unit and wasted resources.
How can a business increase capacity utilisation? ~~ Increase sales through marketing, rationalise capacity, or subcontract work in.
What is buffer stock? ~~ The minimum level of stock kept to cover unexpected demand or supply delays.
What is the reorder level? ~~ The stock level at which a new order is placed.
What is lead time? ~~ The time between ordering stock and receiving it.
What is just in time? ~~ Receiving stock only when it is needed, minimising stock holding.
What are the disadvantages of JIT? ~~ Relies on reliable suppliers; any delay can halt production; no buffer for unexpected demand.
What is lean production? ~~ Reducing waste in all forms (time, materials and space) to improve efficiency.
What is quality control? ~~ Inspecting products at the end of production to find defects.
What is quality assurance? ~~ Building quality into every stage of production to prevent defects.
What is Kaizen? ~~ Continuous improvement through small changes, often suggested by workers.
What is Total Quality Management? ~~ An organisation-wide approach in which every employee is responsible for quality.

## 2.5
What is inflation? ~~ A sustained rise in the general price level.
How does inflation affect business costs? ~~ It raises the cost of raw materials and wages, reducing profit margins.
What is deflation's effect on business? ~~ Falling prices can reduce revenues and cause customers to delay purchases.
What is an appreciation of the pound? ~~ An increase in the value of the pound, making UK exports more expensive and imports cheaper.
How does a depreciation of the pound affect UK exporters? ~~ Exports become cheaper abroad, increasing demand.
What is an interest rate? ~~ The cost of borrowing and the reward for saving.
How does a rise in interest rates affect businesses? ~~ Higher borrowing costs, lower consumer spending, and a stronger pound.
What is corporation tax? ~~ A tax on company profits.
What is VAT's effect on business? ~~ It raises prices for consumers, which may reduce demand.
What is government spending's effect on business? ~~ It can increase demand, especially for firms supplying the public sector.
What is a boom? ~~ A period of high growth, rising incomes and high demand.
What is a recession? ~~ Two consecutive quarters of falling GDP.
What is economic uncertainty? ~~ Unpredictability in the economy, which makes businesses delay investment and hiring.
What is consumer protection law? ~~ Laws ensuring goods are of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described.
What is employment law? ~~ Laws protecting workers' rights, e.g. minimum wage, discrimination and working hours.
What is health and safety law? ~~ Laws requiring employers to provide a safe working environment.
What is competition policy? ~~ Laws and regulations to prevent anti-competitive practices, enforced by the CMA.
What is environmental legislation? ~~ Laws limiting pollution and waste, e.g. emissions targets and recycling requirements.
What is market size's effect on business? ~~ Larger markets offer more sales potential but often attract more competition.
What is a monopoly? ~~ A single firm dominating a market (legally a market share over 25% in the UK).
What is the effect of increased competition on a business? ~~ Pressure to lower prices, improve quality, and innovate.
What is the difference between a competitive and an oligopolistic market? ~~ Competitive markets have many firms; oligopolies are dominated by a few large firms.

## 3.1
What is a mission statement? ~~ A statement of a business's purpose and values.
What is a corporate objective? ~~ A goal set for the whole business, derived from the mission, e.g. growth or market share.
What is a functional objective? ~~ A goal for a specific department, supporting corporate objectives.
What are SMART objectives? ~~ Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-bound.
What is a corporate strategy? ~~ A long-term plan to achieve corporate objectives.
What is market penetration in Ansoff's Matrix? ~~ Selling more of existing products in existing markets; the lowest risk strategy.
What is market development in Ansoff's Matrix? ~~ Selling existing products in new markets.
What is product development in Ansoff's Matrix? ~~ Selling new products in existing markets.
What is diversification in Ansoff's Matrix? ~~ Selling new products in new markets; the highest risk strategy.
What is cost leadership (Porter)? ~~ Achieving the lowest costs in the industry to charge lower prices or earn higher margins.
What is differentiation (Porter)? ~~ Offering unique products that customers value and will pay more for.
What is a focus strategy (Porter)? ~~ Targeting a niche market with either cost focus or differentiation focus.
What is distinctive capability? ~~ A feature that is hard for competitors to copy, e.g. reputation, architecture or innovation (Kay).
What is a strategic decision? ~~ A long-term, high-risk decision made by senior managers that affects the whole business.
What is a tactical decision? ~~ A medium-term decision to implement strategy, made by middle managers.
What is an operational decision? ~~ A routine, short-term decision made by junior managers.
What is SWOT analysis? ~~ An analysis of strengths, weaknesses (internal), opportunities and threats (external).
What is PESTLE analysis? ~~ Analysis of political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental factors.
What is Porter's Five Forces? ~~ Rivalry among existing firms, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer power and supplier power.
What does high buyer power mean? ~~ Customers can demand lower prices or better quality, reducing profitability.
What does a high threat of substitutes mean? ~~ Customers can easily switch to alternative products, limiting pricing power.
What does high barriers to entry mean? ~~ Fewer new competitors, allowing existing firms to be more profitable.

## 3.2
What is the benefit of growth to a business? ~~ Economies of scale, higher market share, more power over suppliers, and greater security.
What is a technical economy of scale? ~~ Cost savings from using larger, more efficient machinery.
What is a purchasing economy of scale? ~~ Lower unit costs from buying in bulk.
What is a financial economy of scale? ~~ Larger firms can borrow at lower interest rates.
What is a managerial economy of scale? ~~ Large firms can employ specialist managers.
What is a marketing economy of scale? ~~ Spreading the cost of advertising over a larger output.
What is an external economy of scale? ~~ Cost savings from the growth of the whole industry in an area, e.g. local suppliers and skilled workers.
What is a diseconomy of scale? ~~ Rising average costs as a firm grows too large.
What causes diseconomies of scale? ~~ Poor communication, coordination problems, and low motivation.
What is organic growth? ~~ Growth from within the business, e.g. opening new branches.
What is inorganic growth? ~~ Growth through mergers and takeovers.
What is a horizontal integration? ~~ A merger between firms at the same stage of production in the same industry.
What is backward vertical integration? ~~ Merging with a supplier.
What is forward vertical integration? ~~ Merging with a customer or distributor.
What is conglomerate integration? ~~ Merging with a firm in an unrelated industry.
What is a joint venture? ~~ Two or more businesses creating a new business together, sharing costs and risks.
What are the risks of mergers? ~~ Culture clashes, redundancies, high costs, and failure to achieve synergies.
What is synergy? ~~ When the combined business is more valuable than the two firms separately.
What is overtrading's cause? ~~ Growing too fast without enough working capital.
How can small businesses survive? ~~ Personal service, niche markets, flexibility, and building strong customer relationships.
What is a niche market's advantage for small businesses? ~~ Less competition from large firms and the ability to charge premium prices.
What is the role of the internet for small firms? ~~ It allows small firms to reach wider markets at low cost.

## 3.3
What is a moving average? ~~ A method of smoothing out fluctuations in data to identify the underlying trend.
What is extrapolation? ~~ Extending a trend line into the future to forecast.
What is correlation? ~~ A statistical relationship between two variables.
What is investment appraisal? ~~ Evaluating the financial return of an investment project.
What is payback period? ~~ The time it takes for an investment to generate enough net cash inflows to cover its initial cost.
What is the advantage of payback? ~~ It is simple and focuses on liquidity and risk.
What is the disadvantage of payback? ~~ It ignores cash flows after payback and the time value of money.
What is the ARR formula? ~~ (Average annual profit ÷ initial investment) × 100.
What is the advantage of ARR? ~~ It considers all cash flows and gives a percentage return that can be compared with interest rates.
What is the disadvantage of ARR? ~~ It ignores the timing of cash flows and the time value of money.
What is net present value? ~~ The sum of discounted future cash flows minus the initial investment.
What is the advantage of NPV? ~~ It takes into account the time value of money.
What is the disadvantage of NPV? ~~ It is complex, and the choice of discount rate affects the result.
What is a decision tree? ~~ A diagram showing the possible outcomes of decisions with probabilities and financial values.
What is expected value? ~~ The sum of each outcome's probability multiplied by its value.
What are the limitations of decision trees? ~~ Probabilities and values are estimates, and they ignore qualitative factors.
What is critical path analysis? ~~ A technique that identifies the sequence of tasks that determines the minimum project time.
What is the critical path? ~~ The sequence of activities with zero float that determines the project duration.
What is the earliest start time? ~~ The earliest time an activity can begin.
What is the latest finish time? ~~ The latest time an activity can finish without delaying the whole project.
What is total float? ~~ Latest finish time − duration − earliest start time.
What are the benefits of critical path analysis? ~~ It identifies critical activities, helps allocate resources, and aids planning.

## 3.4
What is corporate culture? ~~ The values, attitudes and beliefs shared by people in an organisation.
What is a power culture (Handy)? ~~ Power concentrated in one person or a small group at the centre.
What is a role culture (Handy)? ~~ Power based on job roles and rules, as in bureaucracies.
What is a task culture (Handy)? ~~ A focus on completing projects through teamwork.
What is a person culture (Handy)? ~~ Individuals see themselves as more important than the organisation, e.g. in law firms.
What is a strong culture? ~~ When shared values are widely held and influence behaviour throughout the business.
What is a stakeholder? ~~ An individual or group with an interest in a business.
What are the objectives of shareholders? ~~ High dividends and rising share prices.
What are the objectives of employees? ~~ Good pay, job security and working conditions.
What are the objectives of customers? ~~ Quality products at fair prices.
What are the objectives of the local community? ~~ Jobs and minimal pollution or disruption.
What is stakeholder mapping? ~~ Plotting stakeholders by their power and interest to decide how to manage them.
What is the shareholder approach? ~~ The business focuses on maximising returns for shareholders.
What is the stakeholder approach? ~~ The business considers the interests of all stakeholders in its decisions.
What is corporate social responsibility? ~~ A business's commitment to act ethically and contribute to society and the environment beyond legal requirements.
What is business ethics? ~~ Moral principles that guide business behaviour.
What is an ethical trade-off? ~~ Choosing between acting ethically and maximising profit, e.g. paying fair wages versus reducing costs.
What is pay dispute ethics? ~~ The ethical issue of executive pay compared with that of ordinary workers.
What is whistleblowing? ~~ An employee reporting wrongdoing within a business.
What is the benefit of CSR? ~~ Improved reputation, customer loyalty, employee motivation and reduced risk.
What is short-termism? ~~ Focusing on immediate profits at the expense of long-term growth.
What is evidence-based decision-making? ~~ Making decisions based on data and analysis rather than intuition.

## 3.5
What is a statement of comprehensive income? ~~ A financial statement showing revenue, costs and profit over a period.
What is a statement of financial position? ~~ A financial statement showing assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time.
What is a non-current asset? ~~ An asset held for more than a year, e.g. property and machinery.
What is a current asset? ~~ An asset likely to be turned into cash within a year, e.g. inventory, receivables and cash.
What is a current liability? ~~ A debt to be paid within a year, e.g. payables and overdrafts.
What is a non-current liability? ~~ A debt due after more than a year, e.g. long-term loans.
What is the gearing formula? ~~ (Non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed) × 100.
What does high gearing mean? ~~ Over 50%: the business relies heavily on borrowing, increasing risk from interest rate rises.
What is capital employed? ~~ Total equity + non-current liabilities.
What is the ROCE formula? ~~ (Operating profit ÷ capital employed) × 100.
What does ROCE show? ~~ How efficiently the business uses its capital to generate profit.
What is labour turnover? ~~ (Number of staff leaving ÷ average number of staff) × 100.
What does high labour turnover indicate? ~~ Poor motivation, low pay or poor management, leading to high recruitment and training costs.
What is the retention rate? ~~ (Number of staff staying for a period ÷ number of staff at the start) × 100.
What is the absenteeism rate? ~~ (Number of staff absent ÷ total staff) × 100, or days lost as a percentage.
What is labour productivity? ~~ Output per worker over a period.
What is the limitation of financial ratios? ~~ They use historical data, don't include qualitative factors, and may be manipulated.
What is window dressing? ~~ Presenting financial statements in a way that makes the business appear more favourable.
What is a competitive advantage? ~~ A feature that allows a business to outperform its competitors.
How can HR strategies improve retention? ~~ Better pay, training opportunities, flexible working and improved management.
What is an employee engagement? ~~ The level of enthusiasm and commitment employees have to the business.
How can a business reduce absenteeism? ~~ Improve working conditions, offer incentives for attendance, and address causes such as stress.

## 3.6
What is internal change? ~~ Change from within, e.g. a new leader, new technology or restructuring.
What is external change? ~~ Change from outside, e.g. economic, legal, social or technological factors.
What is incremental change? ~~ Gradual, small changes over time.
What is disruptive change? ~~ Rapid, significant change that transforms an industry.
What is transformational leadership? ~~ A leader who inspires and motivates staff to achieve significant change.
What is Kotter's 8-step model? ~~ A model for managing change: create urgency, build a coalition, form a vision, communicate, empower, create short-term wins, build on change, anchor it in culture.
What is Lewin's force field analysis? ~~ A technique that identifies driving and restraining forces for change.
What is resistance to change? ~~ Opposition from employees or stakeholders to changes in the business.
Why do employees resist change? ~~ Fear of the unknown, job losses, loss of status, and a lack of understanding.
How can resistance to change be overcome? ~~ Communication, consultation, training, support and incentives.
What is a contingency plan? ~~ A plan for dealing with unexpected events.
What is scenario planning? ~~ Preparing for different possible future situations.
What is risk assessment? ~~ Identifying potential risks, their likelihood and impact, and how to manage them.
What is business continuity planning? ~~ Plans to keep the business operating during and after a crisis.
What is succession planning? ~~ Preparing for the replacement of key staff, especially senior leaders.
What is the effect of size on managing change? ~~ Large firms may find change slower due to bureaucracy; small firms may be more flexible.
What is the effect of culture on managing change? ~~ A strong, rigid culture may resist change; an open culture may adapt more easily.
Why does speed of change matter? ~~ Businesses must adapt quickly to technological and market changes to stay competitive.
What is organisational flexibility? ~~ The ability to adapt structure, staff and processes quickly to change.
What is a flexible workforce? ~~ A workforce that can adapt to changing demands through part-time, temporary and multi-skilled staff.
What is knowledge management? ~~ Capturing, sharing and using knowledge within an organisation.
What is restructuring? ~~ Changing the organisational structure, e.g. delayering, to improve efficiency.

## 4.1
What is globalisation? ~~ The growing integration and interdependence of economies around the world.
What factors have increased globalisation? ~~ Trade liberalisation, improved transport and communication, and the growth of MNCs.
What is an emerging economy? ~~ A country with rapid economic growth and industrialisation, e.g. China, India and Brazil.
Why are emerging economies attractive to businesses? ~~ Growing markets, rising incomes and low production costs.
What is GDP per capita? ~~ GDP divided by population.
What is the Human Development Index? ~~ A measure of development based on income, education and life expectancy.
What is literacy rate? ~~ The percentage of the adult population that can read and write.
What is specialisation in international trade? ~~ Countries concentrating on producing goods where they have a comparative advantage.
What is comparative advantage? ~~ A country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
What is foreign direct investment? ~~ Investment by a firm in one country in business operations in another country.
What are the benefits of FDI to the host country? ~~ Jobs, technology transfer, tax revenue and skills.
What is protectionism? ~~ Government policies that restrict imports to protect domestic industries.
What is a tariff? ~~ A tax on imported goods.
What is a quota? ~~ A limit on the quantity of imports.
What is an embargo? ~~ A complete ban on trade with a particular country or of a particular good.
What is a non-tariff barrier? ~~ A restriction on trade other than tariffs, e.g. regulations, standards and paperwork.
What is a trading bloc? ~~ A group of countries that reduce or eliminate trade barriers between themselves.
What are examples of trading blocs? ~~ EU, USMCA, ASEAN and Mercosur.
What is the effect of trading blocs on businesses inside the bloc? ~~ Access to larger markets, lower costs, and increased competition.
What is the effect of trading blocs on businesses outside the bloc? ~~ Face tariffs and barriers when exporting into the bloc.
What is the World Trade Organization? ~~ The international body that promotes free trade and resolves trade disputes.
What is deglobalisation? ~~ A slowdown or reversal of global integration, e.g. more protectionism and reshoring.

## 4.2
What is a push factor for international trade? ~~ A factor that encourages a business to leave its domestic market, e.g. saturation or strong competition.
What is a pull factor for international trade? ~~ A factor that attracts a business to foreign markets, e.g. growth opportunities and economies of scale.
What is offshoring? ~~ Moving business activities to another country.
What is outsourcing? ~~ Contracting out business activities to another company, which may be abroad.
What is reshoring? ~~ Bringing previously offshored activities back to the home country.
What factors are considered when assessing a market? ~~ Income levels, ease of doing business, infrastructure, political stability and exchange rates.
What factors are considered when choosing a production location? ~~ Costs of production, skills, infrastructure, government incentives and access to markets.
What is the ease of doing business? ~~ How simple it is to start and run a business in a country, considering regulations and bureaucracy.
What is political stability? ~~ The likelihood of a government remaining in power without conflict, which reduces risk.
What is a global merger? ~~ A merger between businesses in different countries.
What is a joint venture in international business? ~~ Two firms from different countries working together on a specific project, sharing risks and local knowledge.
What are the benefits of global joint ventures? ~~ Access to local knowledge, shared costs and risks, and overcoming legal barriers.
How do exchange rates affect competitiveness? ~~ A weaker currency makes exports cheaper and imports more expensive.
What is global competitiveness? ~~ The ability of a firm to compete in international markets.
What is cost competitiveness? ~~ Competing by keeping costs low.
What is differentiation in global markets? ~~ Offering unique products that stand out internationally.
What is a skills shortage's effect on competitiveness? ~~ It raises costs and limits productivity and growth.
What is economies of scale's role in global competitiveness? ~~ Larger output lowers average costs, allowing lower prices.
What is extending the product life cycle through international markets? ~~ Selling a product in new markets when sales decline in the home market.
What is a cultural barrier to international trade? ~~ Differences in language, customs and values that affect how products are marketed.
What are the risks of entering international markets? ~~ Currency risk, political risk, cultural misunderstandings and higher costs.
What is licensing in international business? ~~ Allowing a foreign firm to use a brand, patent or technology in return for a fee.

## 4.3
What is a global marketing strategy? ~~ Using the same marketing mix across all countries (standardisation).
What is glocalisation? ~~ Adapting a global product or marketing mix to suit local markets.
What is an ethnocentric approach? ~~ Treating overseas markets the same as the home market, with little adaptation.
What is a polycentric approach? ~~ Adapting products and marketing to suit each local market.
What is a geocentric approach? ~~ A blend of standardisation and adaptation, treating the world as one market with local variations.
What are the benefits of standardisation? ~~ Economies of scale, a consistent brand image and lower costs.
What are the benefits of adaptation? ~~ Better fit with local tastes, higher sales and customer loyalty.
How might product be adapted for global markets? ~~ Changes to size, ingredients, packaging and features to suit local tastes and regulations.
How might price be adapted for global markets? ~~ Setting prices according to local incomes, competition and costs.
How might promotion be adapted for global markets? ~~ Using different languages, images and media to reflect local cultures.
How might place be adapted for global markets? ~~ Using local distributors and different channels to reach customers.
What is a global niche market? ~~ A small, specialised market segment across several countries.
What is a cultural factor in global marketing? ~~ Religion, language, values and customs that affect consumer behaviour.
What is a social factor in global marketing? ~~ Changing lifestyles, demographics and consumer trends.
What is an example of a translation mistake in marketing? ~~ Brand names or slogans that have unintended or offensive meanings in another language.
What is the risk of ignoring cultural differences? ~~ Offending customers, poor sales and damage to the brand.
What is the role of market research in global marketing? ~~ Understanding local customer needs, competition and cultural factors.
What is a global brand? ~~ A brand recognised worldwide, e.g. Coca-Cola, Apple and McDonald's.
What is international market segmentation? ~~ Dividing global markets into groups with similar characteristics across countries.
What is the role of digital marketing in global markets? ~~ Allows businesses to reach global customers at low cost through websites and social media.
What is e-commerce's role in international trade? ~~ It enables small firms to sell globally without a physical presence.
What is a global marketing mix? ~~ The combination of product, price, place and promotion used in international markets.

## 4.4
What is a multinational corporation? ~~ A business with its headquarters in one country and operations in several others.
How can MNCs affect local labour? ~~ They create jobs and training but may pay low wages or cause job losses in local firms.
How can MNCs affect local businesses? ~~ They may create opportunities for local suppliers, or force out local competitors.
How can MNCs affect the local environment? ~~ Pollution, resource depletion and habitat destruction, although some bring cleaner technology.
How do MNCs affect the national economy? ~~ FDI increases GDP, tax revenue and exports, but profits may be repatriated.
What is profit repatriation? ~~ Sending profits made abroad back to the MNC's home country.
How do MNCs affect the balance of payments? ~~ FDI inflows and exports improve it; profit repatriation and imports worsen it.
What is technology transfer? ~~ MNCs bringing new technology and skills to host countries.
What is the global minimum corporate tax rate agreement? ~~ An OECD-led deal (2021) for large MNCs to pay at least 15% tax on profits in each country, reducing profit shifting to tax havens.
What is tax avoidance by MNCs? ~~ Legal methods of reducing tax, e.g. registering profits in low-tax countries.
What is tax evasion? ~~ Illegally avoiding paying tax.
What are ethical issues in MNC supply chains? ~~ Child labour, poor working conditions, low wages and unsafe factories.
What is ethical sourcing? ~~ Ensuring products are produced in ways that respect workers' rights and the environment.
What is the role of pressure groups in controlling MNCs? ~~ Campaigning, raising awareness and organising boycotts to change MNC behaviour.
What is the role of social media in controlling MNCs? ~~ It exposes unethical behaviour quickly, damaging reputations.
What is the role of legal control of MNCs? ~~ Governments regulate MNCs through laws on tax, employment, the environment and competition.
What is political influence on MNCs? ~~ Governments may offer incentives or impose restrictions to influence MNC behaviour.
What is environmental ethics for MNCs? ~~ Reducing pollution, carbon emissions and waste, and using sustainable resources.
What is the role of the OECD in MNC regulation? ~~ It sets guidelines for responsible business conduct and works on international tax rules.
What is a stakeholder conflict for MNCs? ~~ Balancing shareholder profits with the interests of workers, communities and the environment.
What are the benefits of MNCs to consumers? ~~ More choice, lower prices and access to global brands.
What is the negative impact of MNCs on local culture? ~~ Cultural homogenisation, as global brands replace local products and traditions.
