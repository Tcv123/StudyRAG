## B1
What is resolution of an instrument? ~~ The smallest change in the measured quantity that the instrument can detect.
What is meant by the "spread" of a set of readings? ~~ The range of the readings; half the spread (±) is used as an estimate of the uncertainty in the mean.
How are uncertainties shown on a graph? ~~ With error bars extending ± the uncertainty from each plotted point.
What is systematic error and how can it be detected? ~~ An error that shifts all readings by the same amount. It can be detected by a non-zero intercept on a graph expected to pass through the origin.
What is the difference between sensitivity and resolution of a sensor? ~~ Sensitivity is the change in output per unit change in input; resolution is the smallest input change that gives a detectable output change.
What is response time of an instrument? ~~ The time taken for the instrument to respond to a change in the quantity it is measuring.
What does a "zero error" mean? ~~ The instrument does not read zero when the measured quantity is zero. It gives a systematic error, corrected by subtracting it.
Why should the dependent variable be plotted on the y-axis? ~~ By convention, so the gradient and intercept can be interpreted directly and relationships are easy to read.
What is the purpose of repeating measurements? ~~ To identify anomalies and to reduce the effect of random error by averaging.
What is meant by a "fair test"? ~~ Only the independent variable changes; all other variables that could affect the outcome are kept constant.
How should you choose the range of an independent variable? ~~ As wide as practical and safe, with evenly spaced values, so the whole relationship can be seen.
How can the precision of a timing measurement be improved? ~~ Use electronic timing (light gates or data loggers) and time multiple events.
What is a calibration curve? ~~ A graph of instrument output against known values of the input, used to convert readings into measured values.
Why is it important to state the uncertainty of a result? ~~ It shows the range within which the true value is likely to lie, so results can be compared with accepted values or other experiments.
What makes a conclusion valid? ~~ It is supported by data from a fair, well-controlled experiment that measures what it claims to measure.

## B2
How do you linearise T = 2π√(m/k) to find k? ~~ Plot T^2 against m: gradient = 4π^2/k, so k = 4π^2/gradient.
How can a straight-line graph through the origin show direct proportion? ~~ If y ∝ x, the graph of y against x is a straight line that passes through the origin.
What is the "worst acceptable line"? ~~ The steepest or shallowest straight line that still passes through all the error bars, used to estimate the uncertainty in the gradient.
How is percentage uncertainty in a gradient calculated? ~~ (|best gradient − worst gradient| ÷ best gradient) × 100%.
Why take logs of both sides of y = kx^n? ~~ log y = n log x + log k is the equation of a straight line, so plotting log y against log x gives gradient n and intercept log k.
For N = N0 e^(−λt), what graph gives a straight line? ~~ ln N against t: the gradient is −λ and the intercept is ln N0.
What does the gradient of a distance–time graph represent? ~~ Speed.
What is the relationship between successive values in an exponential decay? ~~ The ratio of successive values over equal time intervals is constant.
How do you use a constant-ratio test to check for exponential behaviour? ~~ Take readings at equal time intervals and divide each by the previous one. If the ratios are equal, the change is exponential.
What does it mean if points on a graph show a curve when a straight line was predicted? ~~ The proposed relationship is incorrect, or a systematic effect such as heating or friction becomes significant at extreme values.
How is the mean of a set of values calculated, and what is its uncertainty? ~~ The sum divided by the number of values; the uncertainty is roughly half the range.
Why use a spreadsheet to model data? ~~ It lets you calculate derived quantities quickly, test different models and plot graphs to compare with experimental results.
What is an order-of-magnitude calculation? ~~ An estimate to the nearest power of ten, used to check whether results or predictions are reasonable.
How is the percentage difference between an experimental value and an accepted value calculated? ~~ (|experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted) × 100%.
When should a result be quoted to fewer significant figures? ~~ When the uncertainty is large, the number of significant figures should reflect the uncertainty (e.g. 9.8 ± 0.3 m s^-2, not 9.8127).

## B3
What is signal-to-noise ratio? ~~ The ratio of the strength of the wanted signal to the unwanted noise. A higher ratio gives a clearer signal.
What is the minimum sampling rate for a signal? ~~ At least twice the highest frequency present in the signal (the Nyquist condition).
What is bandwidth? ~~ The range of frequencies a signal occupies or a channel can transmit. A higher bandwidth allows a higher bit rate.
State the relationship between bit rate, samples per second and bits per sample. ~~ Bit rate = samples per second × bits per sample.
How much information is carried by an image of N pixels, each with b bits? ~~ Total information = N × b bits.
What is the magnification of a lens? ~~ Image size ÷ object size, which also equals image distance ÷ object distance (v/u).
State the lens equation using the Cartesian convention. ~~ 1/v = 1/u + 1/f, with distances measured from the lens (u is negative for a real object).
Define the power of a lens. ~~ Power = 1/f, with f in metres; unit dioptre (D).
How does a potential divider make a sensing circuit? ~~ A sensor such as a thermistor or LDR is paired with a fixed resistor. As the sensor's resistance changes, the output pd changes.
What is conductance, and how does it combine? ~~ G = 1/R, with unit siemens (S). Conductances in parallel add: G = G1 + G2.
What is the relationship between EMF, terminal pd and internal resistance? ~~ V = ε − Ir.
What is the difference between a strong and a stiff material? ~~ Strong: high breaking stress. Stiff: high Young modulus (small strain for a given stress).
How does a metal deform plastically at the microscopic level? ~~ Dislocations in the crystal lattice move under stress, allowing planes of atoms to slip past each other.
Why are composite materials such as reinforced concrete useful? ~~ They combine the properties of materials: concrete is strong in compression, steel is strong in tension.
What is noise in an image, and how can it be reduced? ~~ Random variation in pixel values. It can be reduced by replacing each pixel with the median of its neighbours (median filter).

## B4
What is a phasor? ~~ A rotating arrow whose length is the amplitude and whose angle is the phase, used to add waves with different phases.
How do you find the resultant amplitude from phasors? ~~ Add the phasors tip to tail; the resultant from start to end gives the amplitude and phase of the combined wave.
What is the condition for a maximum in a two-slit pattern using phasors? ~~ The phasors from each slit line up (path difference = nλ), giving a large resultant.
What is the condition for a minimum using path difference? ~~ Path difference = (n + ½)λ, so the phasors point in opposite directions and cancel.
State the fringe-spacing equation for a double slit. ~~ λ = xd/L, where x is fringe spacing, d slit separation and L the distance to the screen.
What is the photon energy of green light of wavelength 500 nm? ~~ E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10^-34 × 3.00 × 10^8)/(500 × 10^-9) ≈ 4.0 × 10^-19 J (about 2.5 eV).
What evidence shows electrons behave as waves? ~~ Electron diffraction: electrons passed through thin graphite produce rings, a diffraction pattern.
How does the quantum "explore all paths" idea explain light travelling in straight lines? ~~ Phasors for paths far from the straight line curl up and cancel. Only paths near the straight (least-time) route add, so light appears to travel in a straight line.
What is the difference between speed and velocity? ~~ Speed is a scalar (rate of change of distance); velocity is a vector (rate of change of displacement).
How do you add two perpendicular velocities? ~~ Magnitude = √(v1^2 + v2^2); direction θ = tan^-1(v2/v1).
How is the motion of a projectile analysed? ~~ Horizontal velocity is constant; vertical motion has constant acceleration g. They are treated independently and linked by time.
Define work done by a force. ~~ Work = force × displacement in the direction of the force: W = Fs cos θ.
State the principle of conservation of momentum. ~~ The total momentum of a system is constant unless an external resultant force acts.
How can iterative (step-by-step) modelling predict motion? ~~ Using small time steps: new v = v + aΔt and new s = s + vΔt, with a recalculated each step from the forces.
What is the relationship between power and velocity for a constant force? ~~ P = Fv.

## B5
What does the Boltzmann factor e^(−E/kT) represent? ~~ The ratio of the number of particles in a state of energy E to those in the ground state; it estimates the fraction of particles with at least energy E.
Why does a small rise in temperature greatly increase a reaction rate? ~~ The Boltzmann factor depends exponentially on 1/T, so the fraction of particles with enough activation energy rises sharply.
How is capacitor discharge modelled iteratively? ~~ ΔQ = −(Q/RC)Δt: the change in charge each small time step is proportional to the charge remaining.
State the capacitor discharge equation. ~~ Q = Q0 e^(−t/RC).
What is the time constant and what fraction remains after one time constant? ~~ τ = RC; after one time constant about 37% (1/e) of the charge remains.
What is the radioactive decay equation in terms of activity? ~~ A = A0 e^(−λt), and A = λN.
Compare the models for radioactive decay and capacitor discharge. ~~ Both are exponential: the rate of decrease is proportional to the amount remaining (dN/dt = −λN; dQ/dt = −Q/RC).
What is the gravitational potential energy of two masses? ~~ E = −GMm/r (zero at infinity).
State Kepler's third law for circular orbits. ~~ T^2 = (4π^2/GM)r^3: T^2 is proportional to r^3.
What is cosmological redshift and how is it calculated? ~~ The stretching of light from distant galaxies by the expansion of space: z = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c.
State Hubble's law. ~~ v = H0 d: the recession speed of a galaxy is proportional to its distance.
What evidence supports the Big Bang? ~~ The redshift of distant galaxies (expansion) and the cosmic microwave background radiation.
What is the cosmic microwave background? ~~ Radiation left from the early hot universe, now redshifted to microwave wavelengths, corresponding to about 2.7 K.
Why does an ideal gas obey pV = NkT? ~~ Molecules move randomly with negligible volume and no intermolecular forces except in collisions, so pressure comes only from collisions with the walls.
State the relationship between mean molecular kinetic energy and temperature. ~~ (1/2)m(c_rms)^2 = (3/2)kT.

## B6
What is the relationship between field strength and potential gradient? ~~ E = −dV/dr: field strength is minus the potential gradient.
What are equipotential surfaces? ~~ Surfaces joining points of equal potential. They are perpendicular to field lines, and no work is done moving along them.
Describe the motion of an electron in a uniform electric field between plates. ~~ It accelerates towards the positive plate; if it enters at right angles to the field it follows a parabolic path.
What is the electronvolt? ~~ The energy gained by one electron accelerated through 1 V: 1.6 × 10^-19 J.
What is flux linkage and how is induced EMF related to it? ~~ Flux linkage = NΦ. The induced EMF = −d(NΦ)/dt.
How do eddy currents reduce transformer efficiency, and how are they reduced? ~~ Induced currents in the iron core heat it, wasting energy. Laminating the core reduces them.
What is magnetic flux density? ~~ B = F/(IL): the force per unit current per unit length on a wire at right angles to the field, measured in tesla.
What did Rutherford scattering show? ~~ The atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus, since a few α particles were scattered through large angles.
Why do high-energy electrons probe nuclear structure? ~~ Their de Broglie wavelength is comparable to nuclear size (about 10^-15 m), and they are unaffected by the strong force.
What is the de Broglie wavelength of a particle with kinetic energy E? ~~ λ = h/p, where p = √(2mE) at non-relativistic speeds.
Why are particle accelerators so large? ~~ Particles need very high energies for small wavelengths and to create massive particles (E = mc^2), which needs long accelerating distances or large rings.
What is the relativistic factor γ? ~~ γ = 1/√(1 − v^2/c^2). Total energy E = γmc^2 increases without limit as v approaches c.
Describe pair production and annihilation. ~~ Pair production: a photon of at least 2mc^2 creates a particle and its antiparticle. Annihilation: a particle and its antiparticle meet and turn into photons.
What is absorbed dose and its unit? ~~ Energy absorbed per unit mass of tissue, measured in gray (Gy = J kg^-1).
How is effective dose calculated? ~~ Effective dose (Sv) = absorbed dose × radiation weighting factor (quality factor), accounting for the harm caused by different radiations.
