## 1
Define magnification. ~~ How many times bigger an image is than the real size of the object.
Define resolution. ~~ The smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate; higher resolution means more detail.
Why do electron microscopes have a higher resolution than light microscopes? ~~ Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than visible light, so smaller structures (e.g. ribosomes, organelle detail) can be distinguished.
State the magnification equation. ~~ Magnification = image size ÷ real size (both in the same units).
Calculate the magnification if image size is 12 mm and real size is 0.04 mm. ~~ Magnification = image ÷ real = 12 ÷ 0.04 = ×300.
A cell is 25 µm long. Its image is 50 mm. What is the magnification? ~~ Convert: 50 mm = 50 000 µm. Magnification = 50 000 ÷ 25 = ×2000.
How many micrometres are in 1 mm, and nanometres in 1 µm? ~~ 1 mm = 1000 µm; 1 µm = 1000 nm.
Write 0.000 05 m in standard form and in micrometres. ~~ 5 × 10⁻⁵ m = 50 µm.
How is total magnification of a light microscope calculated? ~~ Eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification (e.g. ×10 × ×40 = ×400).
Core practical: how do you prepare an onion cell slide? ~~ Peel thin epidermis, lay flat on slide in a drop of water, add iodine stain, lower coverslip at an angle with a mounting needle to avoid air bubbles.
Core practical: how should you focus a light microscope? ~~ Start with lowest-power objective, raise stage close using coarse focus while watching from the side, then focus moving away; refine with fine focus, then switch to higher power.
What are the rules for a scientific drawing of cells? ~~ Sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading, label lines with a ruler not crossing, title and magnification stated.
Name the subcellular structures found in animal cells. ~~ Nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes.
Which three extra structures do plant cells have? ~~ Cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a permanent vacuole filled with cell sap.
What is the function of the mitochondria? ~~ Site of aerobic respiration, releasing energy for the cell.
What is the function of ribosomes? ~~ Site of protein synthesis.
Name the structures found in a bacterial cell. ~~ Cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, chromosomal DNA (single loop), plasmid DNA and flagella.
Difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells? ~~ Prokaryotes (bacteria) lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, are much smaller and have a loop of DNA plus plasmids; eukaryotes have a nucleus.
How is a sperm cell adapted? ~~ Acrosome with digestive enzymes to penetrate egg, haploid nucleus, many mitochondria for energy, tail (flagellum) to swim.
How is an egg cell adapted? ~~ Nutrients in cytoplasm, haploid nucleus, cell membrane changes after fertilisation so only one sperm enters.
How are ciliated epithelial cells adapted? ~~ Cilia on surface beat to move mucus (and trapped particles) up the airways or eggs along the oviduct.
Explain the lock and key model. ~~ The substrate has a complementary shape to the enzyme's active site, so it fits to form an enzyme-substrate complex; products are then released.
What does denatured mean for an enzyme? ~~ High temperature or extreme pH changes the shape of the active site so the substrate no longer fits; this is irreversible.
Common exam trap: do enzymes die at high temperatures? ~~ No. Enzymes are not alive; they denature because the active site changes shape.
Core practical: how is the effect of pH on amylase measured? ~~ Mix amylase, starch and buffer at set pH in water bath; every 10 s sample onto iodine in spotting tile; record time until iodine stays orange-brown.
Starch was fully broken down in 40 s. Calculate the rate. ~~ Rate = 1000 ÷ time = 1000 ÷ 40 = 25 s⁻¹ (or 1/40 = 0.025 s⁻¹).
Name the enzymes that break down carbohydrates, proteins and lipids. ~~ Carbohydrases (e.g. amylase) to simple sugars; proteases to amino acids; lipases to fatty acids and glycerol.
Food tests: what are the tests for starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid? ~~ (Biology only) Iodine turns blue-black; Benedict's heated turns brick-red; biuret turns lilac/purple; ethanol emulsion turns cloudy white.
Define diffusion, osmosis and active transport. ~~ Diffusion: net movement of particles high to low concentration. Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution. Active transport: low to high, needs energy from respiration.
Core practical: potato chip mass rose from 2.0 g to 2.3 g in water. Percentage change? ~~ (2.3 − 2.0) ÷ 2.0 × 100 = +15%. Water entered by osmosis because the solution was more dilute than the cell contents.

## 2
What is the cell cycle? ~~ Interphase (cell grows, organelles and DNA replicated), mitosis (chromosomes separate), cytokinesis (cytoplasm and membrane divide).
State the stages of mitosis in order. ~~ Prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, then cytokinesis.
What happens in prophase? ~~ Chromosomes condense and become visible, nuclear membrane breaks down.
What happens in metaphase? ~~ Chromosomes line up along the middle (equator) of the cell.
What happens in anaphase? ~~ Spindle fibres pull chromatids of each chromosome to opposite poles of the cell.
What happens in telophase and cytokinesis? ~~ Nuclear membranes form around each set of chromosomes; the cytoplasm and cell membrane then divide, forming two cells.
What are the products of mitosis? ~~ Two genetically identical diploid daughter cells.
Give three uses of mitosis. ~~ Growth, repair of damaged tissue and asexual reproduction.
How does cancer form? ~~ Changes (mutations) in cells lead to uncontrolled cell division by mitosis, forming a tumour.
Define differentiation. ~~ The process by which cells become specialised for a particular function.
How do plants grow? ~~ Cell division by mitosis in meristems (shoot and root tips), cell elongation, and cell differentiation.
How do animals grow? ~~ Cell division and differentiation; most animal cells differentiate early and lose the ability to divide.
What are percentile charts used for? ~~ Monitoring growth of babies/children (e.g. mass, head circumference) against the population to spot problems early.
A baby is on the 75th percentile for mass. What does that mean? ~~ 75% of babies of the same age weigh less than this baby, and 25% weigh more.
Calculate percentage growth if a seedling grows from 4.0 cm to 5.0 cm. ~~ (5.0 − 4.0) ÷ 4.0 × 100 = 25% increase.
What is a stem cell? ~~ An undifferentiated cell that can divide to produce more stem cells and can differentiate into specialised cells.
Compare embryonic and adult stem cells. ~~ Embryonic can differentiate into any cell type; adult (e.g. bone marrow) can only form a limited range of cells.
Where are plant stem cells found and what can they do? ~~ In meristems; they can differentiate into any type of plant cell throughout the plant's life.
Give a benefit and two risks of using stem cells in medicine. ~~ Benefit: replace damaged cells (e.g. Parkinson's, spinal injury). Risks: rejection by immune system, could become cancerous or carry viruses; embryonic use raises ethical issues.
Name the parts of the brain and their functions. ~~ (Biology only) Cerebrum: thought, memory, language; cerebellum: balance and muscle coordination; medulla oblongata: unconscious actions like heart rate and breathing.
How can brain damage be investigated? ~~ (Biology only) CT scans (X-rays) show structure; PET scans (radioactive tracer) show active areas; treatment is difficult as the brain is complex and delicate.
What makes up the central nervous system? ~~ The brain and spinal cord.
Describe the structure of a sensory neurone. ~~ Long dendron carrying impulses from receptor to cell body, short axon to the CNS; myelin sheath and dendrites.
What is the function of the myelin sheath? ~~ Insulates the axon so impulses jump between gaps, speeding up transmission.
Describe how an impulse crosses a synapse. ~~ Impulse arrives, neurotransmitter released, diffuses across the gap, binds to receptors on next neurone, triggering a new impulse.
State the path of a reflex arc. ~~ Stimulus, receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone (in spinal cord), motor neurone, effector, response.
Why are reflexes important? ~~ They are rapid and automatic, protecting the body from harm; they bypass the conscious brain.
How does the eye focus on a near object? ~~ (Biology only) Ciliary muscles contract, suspensory ligaments slacken, lens becomes fatter (more convex) and refracts light more.
What causes short-sightedness and how is it corrected? ~~ (Biology only) Light focused in front of the retina (eyeball too long or lens too thick); corrected with a concave (diverging) lens.
What are rods and cones? ~~ (Biology only) Retina receptor cells: rods detect dim light (black and white); cones detect colour in bright light. Colour blindness involves faulty cones.

## 3
Compare sexual and asexual reproduction. ~~ Sexual: two parents, gametes fuse, variation in offspring. Asexual: one parent, mitosis, genetically identical clones, quicker.
Give one advantage of sexual reproduction. ~~ (Biology only) Produces variation, so a population is more likely to survive environmental change or disease.
Give one advantage of asexual reproduction. ~~ (Biology only) Only one parent needed, faster, and many offspring produced when conditions are favourable.
What are gametes? ~~ Sex cells (sperm and egg in animals; pollen and egg in plants) with half the normal number of chromosomes (haploid).
What does meiosis produce? ~~ Four genetically different haploid gametes from one diploid cell, after two divisions.
Why is meiosis needed for sexual reproduction? ~~ Halving chromosome number means the diploid number (46 in humans) is restored at fertilisation.
Describe the structure of DNA. ~~ A polymer made of two strands of nucleotides coiled into a double helix, held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases.
What is a nucleotide made of? ~~ A sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate group and one of four bases (A, T, C, G).
State the complementary base pairs. ~~ Adenine pairs with thymine (A–T); cytosine pairs with guanine (C–G).
DNA has 30% adenine. What percentage is guanine? ~~ T = 30%, so A + T = 60%; C + G = 40%, so G = 20%.
Practical: how do you extract DNA from fruit? ~~ Mash fruit with detergent (breaks membranes) and salt (clumps DNA), filter, then pour ice-cold ethanol down the side; DNA precipitates as white strands.
Define gene and genome. ~~ Gene: a section of DNA coding for a specific protein. Genome: the entire genetic material of an organism.
How does a gene code for a protein? ~~ A sequence of three bases (triplet) codes for one amino acid; the order of bases determines the order of amino acids.
Describe transcription. ~~ (Biology only, Higher) RNA polymerase binds to non-coding DNA before the gene; DNA unzips; complementary mRNA is made from the template strand and leaves the nucleus.
Describe translation. ~~ (Biology only, Higher) mRNA attaches to a ribosome; tRNA molecules bring specific amino acids, their anticodons pairing with mRNA codons; amino acids join to form a polypeptide.
How can a mutation in non-coding DNA affect a phenotype? ~~ (Biology only, Higher) It can affect where RNA polymerase binds, changing how much protein (mRNA) is made.
What did Mendel discover? ~~ (Biology only) Using pea plants he showed inherited characteristics are passed on as separate units (now genes), with some dominant and some recessive.
Define allele, dominant and recessive. ~~ Allele: a different version of a gene. Dominant: expressed if one copy present. Recessive: only expressed if two copies present.
Define genotype and phenotype. ~~ Genotype: the alleles an organism has (e.g. Bb). Phenotype: the observable characteristic (e.g. brown eyes).
Define homozygous and heterozygous. ~~ Homozygous: two identical alleles (BB or bb). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Bb).
Two heterozygous parents (Ff) for cystic fibrosis have a child. Probability child has CF? ~~ Punnett square gives FF, Ff, Ff, ff: 1 in 4, 25% (ratio 3:1 unaffected to affected).
How is sex determined in humans? ~~ Females are XX, males XY; the father's sperm carries X or Y, giving a 50% chance of each sex.
How are ABO blood groups inherited? ~~ (Biology only) Multiple alleles: IA and IB are codominant, IO is recessive; IAIB gives group AB, IOIO gives group O.
Why are sex-linked disorders more common in males? ~~ (Biology only) The allele is recessive on the X chromosome; males have only one X, so one faulty allele causes the disorder (e.g. colour blindness, haemophilia).
What is a family pedigree used for? ~~ Tracing how a characteristic is inherited through generations to work out genotypes and probabilities.
What causes variation? ~~ Genetic variation (alleles inherited, mutation) and environmental variation (e.g. diet, climate); most characteristics are influenced by both.
What is a mutation? ~~ A change in the DNA base sequence; most have no effect, some affect the phenotype a little, rarely significantly.
What was the Human Genome Project? ~~ An international project that mapped the full sequence of bases in human DNA, completed in 2003.
Give uses of knowing the human genome. ~~ Identify genes linked to disease, personalised medicine tailored to a person's alleles, better understanding of inherited disorders and human migration.
Exam trap: are most characteristics controlled by one gene? ~~ No. Most are controlled by many genes interacting with the environment; single-gene examples include cystic fibrosis and polydactyly.

## 4
Who proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection? ~~ Charles Darwin; Alfred Russel Wallace independently came to similar conclusions.
Describe evolution by natural selection. ~~ Variation from mutation; competition for resources; individuals with advantageous alleles survive and reproduce; they pass on alleles, so the allele becomes more frequent over generations.
Explain how antibiotic resistance develops in bacteria. ~~ A mutation gives resistance; antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria; resistant ones survive and reproduce rapidly, passing on the allele; resistant population grows.
Why does antibiotic resistance provide evidence for evolution? ~~ Bacteria reproduce rapidly, so the change in allele frequency through natural selection can be observed over a short time.
How can antibiotic resistance be reduced? ~~ Only prescribe antibiotics when needed (not for viruses), complete the course, and limit use in farming.
What is speciation? ~~ Formation of a new species when populations become so different they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
What does the hominid fossil Ardi show? ~~ Ardipithecus ramidus, 4.4 million years old: mix of ape and human features, grasping big toe for climbing, but walked upright; small brain (~350 cm³).
What does the fossil Lucy show? ~~ Australopithecus afarensis, 3.2 million years old: walked upright more efficiently than Ardi, arched feet, brain about 400 cm³.
What did Leakey's discoveries show? ~~ Turkana Boy (Homo erectus, 1.6 million years): more human-like, taller, larger brain (~880 cm³); Leakey also found Homo habilis fossils and stone tools.
How do stone tools show human evolution? ~~ Tools became more complex over time (e.g. simple pebble tools to shaped hand axes), suggesting brain size and skill increased; dated by stratigraphy.
How can stone tools and fossils be dated? ~~ By the rock layer they are found in (deeper layers are older) and by carbon-14 dating of nearby material.
What is the pentadactyl limb? ~~ A five-digit limb structure found in many vertebrates (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians), evidence they share a common ancestor.
Why is the fossil record incomplete? ~~ Soft tissue decays, conditions for fossilisation are rare, and many fossils are destroyed by geological activity or not yet found.
What is the three-domain system? ~~ Carl Woese's classification based on RNA/DNA sequences: Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.
List the classification levels in order. ~~ Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
Why has classification changed over time? ~~ Genetic analysis (DNA sequencing) revealed relationships that physical features could not, e.g. archaea differ from bacteria.
What are the five kingdoms? ~~ Animals, plants, fungi, protists and prokaryotes.
Define selective breeding. ~~ Humans choosing parents with desired characteristics to breed together, over many generations, to produce offspring with those characteristics.
Give two risks of selective breeding. ~~ Reduced gene pool (inbreeding) increases risk of inherited disorders, and less variation makes populations vulnerable to new diseases.
What is tissue culture? ~~ (Biology only) Growing new plants from a small piece of tissue on sterile nutrient medium with hormones; produces many identical clones quickly.
What is genetic engineering? ~~ Changing an organism's genome by inserting a gene from another organism to give a desired characteristic.
Describe the stages of genetic engineering. ~~ (Higher) Restriction enzymes cut out gene leaving sticky ends; same enzyme cuts plasmid vector; ligase joins gene into plasmid; plasmid inserted into host cell.
What is a vector in genetic engineering? ~~ Something used to carry the gene into the host cell, e.g. a bacterial plasmid or virus.
Give an example of genetic engineering in medicine. ~~ Bacteria engineered with the human insulin gene produce insulin for people with diabetes.
How are crops engineered to resist insects? ~~ (Biology only) The Bt gene from a bacterium is inserted so the plants produce a toxin that kills insect larvae, reducing crop damage and pesticide use.
Give benefits and risks of GM crops. ~~ Benefits: higher yields, pest/herbicide resistance, extra nutrients (e.g. Golden Rice). Risks: genes transfer to wild plants, harm to insects, long-term effects unknown, seed cost.
How do fertilisers and biological control increase food production? ~~ (Biology only) Fertilisers add minerals (nitrates) for growth; biological control uses a predator or parasite to reduce pests without chemicals.
Give a risk of biological control. ~~ (Biology only) The introduced species may become a pest itself or harm native species.
Exam trap: does an individual organism evolve during its lifetime? ~~ No. Populations evolve over generations; individuals do not change their alleles in response to the environment.
Define species. ~~ A group of similar organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.

## 5
How does the WHO define health? ~~ A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease.
Define communicable and non-communicable disease. ~~ Communicable: caused by a pathogen and can spread between individuals. Non-communicable: cannot spread, e.g. heart disease, cancer.
How can one disease increase the risk of another? ~~ e.g. HIV weakens the immune system, increasing the chance of TB; malnutrition or infection can make someone more susceptible.
Name the four types of pathogen with an example of each. ~~ Bacteria (cholera, TB), viruses (HIV, Ebola), fungi (Chalara ash dieback), protists (malaria).
Describe the lytic cycle of a virus. ~~ (Biology only) Virus attaches and injects genetic material; host replicates viral material and proteins; new viruses assemble; cell bursts (lyses) releasing them.
How does the lysogenic cycle differ? ~~ (Biology only) Viral DNA inserts into host genome and is copied each division without harm until triggered to enter the lytic cycle.
Cholera: cause, spread, symptoms, prevention. ~~ Bacterium Vibrio cholerae; spread by contaminated water; causes diarrhoea and dehydration; prevented by clean water supplies.
Tuberculosis: cause, spread, prevention. ~~ Bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis; airborne droplets; lung damage; reduced by isolating patients, good ventilation, completing antibiotic course, BCG vaccine.
Chalara ash dieback: cause, spread, prevention. ~~ Fungus; spores carried by wind; leaf loss and bark lesions in ash trees; controlled by removing infected trees and restricting import of young trees.
Malaria: cause, spread, prevention. ~~ Protist (Plasmodium) carried by mosquito vectors; recurring fever; prevented by nets, insecticides, removing standing water, antimalarial drugs.
HIV: cause, spread, prevention. ~~ Virus spread by bodily fluids (sex, sharing needles); destroys white blood cells leading to AIDS; prevented by condoms, not sharing needles; antiretroviral drugs slow it.
Stomach ulcers: cause, spread, treatment. ~~ Bacterium Helicobacter pylori; spread by oral transmission (contaminated food/water); stomach pain; treated with antibiotics, reduced by clean water and hygiene.
Ebola: cause, spread, prevention. ~~ Virus spread via bodily fluids; haemorrhagic fever; controlled by isolating patients, wearing protective clothing, safe burial.
Name two sexually transmitted infections and how to reduce their spread. ~~ Chlamydia (bacterium) and HIV (virus); use condoms, screening and treatment, and not sharing needles for HIV.
Name the human body's physical and chemical barriers to infection. ~~ Skin, mucus and cilia in airways; lysozyme in tears; hydrochloric acid in the stomach.
How do plants defend against pathogens? ~~ (Biology only) Physical: waxy cuticle, cellulose cell walls. Chemical: antiseptics and antimicrobial substances; some produce poisons to deter herbivores.
How are plant diseases identified? ~~ (Biology only) Observing symptoms, distribution analysis, identifying pathogens in a lab, and testing kits using monoclonal antibodies.
Describe the specific immune response. ~~ Lymphocytes with complementary antibodies to a pathogen's antigens divide; plasma cells secrete antibodies; antibodies bind antigens, destroying pathogens; memory lymphocytes remain.
Explain how vaccination works. ~~ Inactive or dead pathogen (antigens) injected; lymphocytes produce antibodies and memory cells; on re-infection a faster, larger secondary response destroys the pathogen.
What is herd immunity? ~~ When a large proportion of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen is less likely to spread, protecting unvaccinated people.
How are monoclonal antibodies produced? ~~ (Biology only, Higher) Mouse lymphocytes making a specific antibody are fused with tumour cells to form hybridomas that divide and make identical antibodies.
Give uses of monoclonal antibodies. ~~ (Biology only, Higher) Pregnancy tests (detect hCG), locating blood clots or cancer cells, and delivering drugs to cancer cells.
How do antibiotics work, and why can't they treat viral infections? ~~ They kill bacteria or stop their growth by damaging bacterial cells; viruses live inside host cells, so antibiotics cannot reach them without harming body cells.
Core practical: how do you test the effect of antiseptics on bacteria? ~~ (Biology only) Spread bacteria on agar plate aseptically, add discs soaked in antiseptics/antibiotics, incubate at 25°C, measure clear zones of inhibition.
Clear zone diameter is 14 mm. Calculate its area. ~~ (Biology only) r = 7 mm; area = πr² = 3.14 × 7² ≈ 154 mm².
Why are cultures incubated at no more than 25°C in schools? ~~ (Biology only) Reduces the chance of growing harmful human pathogens that thrive at 37°C.
Describe the stages of drug development. ~~ Discovery; preclinical testing on cells, tissues and live animals; clinical trials on healthy volunteers (low dose for safety) then patients (efficacy, optimum dose).
What is a double-blind trial? ~~ Neither patients nor doctors know who receives the drug or placebo, preventing bias.
Give lifestyle risk factors for cardiovascular disease and treatments. ~~ Smoking, poor diet, obesity, lack of exercise. Treatments: lifestyle change, statins/antihypertensives, stents, bypass surgery or heart transplant.
Calculate waist:hip ratio for waist 90 cm and hip 100 cm. ~~ 90 ÷ 100 = 0.9; a high ratio (abdominal obesity) increases cardiovascular disease risk.

## 6
State the word equation for photosynthesis. ~~ Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll).
State the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. ~~ 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
Why is photosynthesis an endothermic reaction? ~~ Energy is transferred from the environment (light) to the chloroplasts.
Why are plants described as producers? ~~ They make biomass (glucose) by photosynthesis, which is the source of energy for food chains.
Name the factors that limit the rate of photosynthesis. ~~ Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature.
Explain the shape of a rate vs light intensity graph. ~~ Rate rises as light increases, then plateaus because another factor (CO₂ or temperature) becomes limiting.
Why does photosynthesis rate fall at high temperatures? ~~ Enzymes involved are denatured, so the rate drops sharply.
State the inverse square law for light intensity. ~~ (Higher) Light intensity ∝ 1 ÷ distance²; doubling the distance reduces intensity to a quarter.
A lamp moves from 10 cm to 20 cm. By how much does light intensity change? ~~ (Higher) Distance doubles, so intensity becomes 1/2² = 1/4 of the original.
Core practical: how is the effect of light intensity on photosynthesis investigated? ~~ Pondweed in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution; lamp at set distances; count oxygen bubbles or measure gas volume per minute.
Core practical: name the variables in the pondweed investigation. ~~ Independent: distance from lamp (light intensity). Dependent: bubbles/gas per minute. Control: CO₂ (hydrogencarbonate), temperature, same pondweed, acclimatisation time.
Why is a beaker of water placed between lamp and pondweed? ~~ Acts as a heat shield so temperature stays constant and only light intensity varies.
How do farmers use limiting factors in greenhouses? ~~ Artificial lighting, paraffin heaters (raise temperature and CO₂) to increase yield; must balance extra cost against extra profit.
Give five uses of glucose in plants. ~~ Respiration, starch for storage, cellulose for cell walls, proteins (with nitrates), and lipids/oils for storage in seeds.
How are root hair cells adapted? ~~ Long projections give large surface area for absorbing water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport; many mitochondria.
Why do root hair cells need active transport? ~~ Mineral ions are more concentrated inside root cells than in soil, so they must move against the concentration gradient using energy.
Describe the structure of xylem. ~~ Dead cells joined end to end with no end walls, forming hollow tubes strengthened by lignin; carry water and minerals upward.
Describe the structure of phloem. ~~ Living sieve tube cells with sieve plates (perforated end walls) and companion cells that provide energy; transport sucrose.
What is translocation? ~~ Movement of sucrose through phloem from sources (leaves) to sinks (roots, growing tissues, storage organs) in both directions, needing energy.
What is transpiration? ~~ Loss of water vapour from leaves by evaporation and diffusion out through stomata, pulling water up the xylem.
How do guard cells control stomata? ~~ When turgid, guard cells curve, opening the stoma; when flaccid, they close it, reducing water loss.
Explain how leaves are adapted for photosynthesis and gas exchange. ~~ Large flat surface, thin for short diffusion distance, palisade cells with many chloroplasts, air spaces in spongy mesophyll, stomata for CO₂ entry.
How does temperature affect transpiration rate? ~~ Higher temperature increases evaporation and diffusion of water vapour, increasing transpiration rate.
How do humidity and wind speed affect transpiration? ~~ High humidity reduces the concentration gradient so rate falls; wind removes water vapour near leaves, increasing rate.
How is transpiration rate measured? ~~ Using a potometer: record distance the air bubble moves in a capillary tube over time; rate = water uptake ÷ time.
An air bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. What is the rate of water uptake? ~~ 30 ÷ 5 = 6 mm per minute.
How do auxins cause phototropism? ~~ (Biology only) Auxin moves to the shaded side of the shoot, causing cells there to elongate more, so the shoot bends towards light.
How do roots respond to gravity? ~~ (Biology only) Auxin collects on the lower side and inhibits cell elongation in roots, so the root bends downwards (positive gravitropism).
Give commercial uses of plant hormones. ~~ (Biology only) Auxins: weedkillers, rooting powder. Gibberellins: seed germination, fruit size, flowering. Ethene: ripening fruit.
How are plants adapted to dry conditions? ~~ (Biology only) Thick waxy cuticle, fewer or sunken stomata, small or spiny leaves, extensive roots, water storage tissue.

## 7
What is a hormone? ~~ A chemical messenger secreted by an endocrine gland into the blood, carried to a target organ where it has an effect.
Compare nervous and hormonal responses. ~~ Nervous: electrical impulses, very fast, short-lived, precise. Hormonal: chemicals in blood, slower, longer lasting, more widespread.
Name the hormones made by the pituitary, thyroid and adrenal glands. ~~ Pituitary: FSH, LH, TSH, ADH, growth hormone. Thyroid: thyroxine. Adrenal: adrenaline.
Name the hormones made by the pancreas, ovaries and testes. ~~ Pancreas: insulin and glucagon. Ovaries: oestrogen and progesterone. Testes: testosterone.
What are the effects of adrenaline? ~~ (Higher) Increases heart rate and blood pressure, more blood to muscles, liver converts glycogen to glucose; prepares the body for fight or flight.
How does negative feedback control thyroxine? ~~ (Higher) Low thyroxine triggers TSH release from the pituitary, stimulating thyroid to release thyroxine; high levels inhibit TSH release.
What is the role of thyroxine? ~~ (Higher) Regulates the body's metabolic rate.
What are the stages of the menstrual cycle? ~~ Days 1–4 menstruation; uterus lining rebuilds; ovulation about day 14; lining maintained; if no fertilisation, lining breaks down.
What do oestrogen and progesterone do in the menstrual cycle? ~~ Oestrogen builds the uterus lining (and inhibits FSH); progesterone maintains the lining and inhibits FSH and LH.
How do FSH and LH control the menstrual cycle? ~~ (Higher) FSH causes egg to mature in a follicle and stimulates oestrogen; LH surge triggers ovulation and corpus luteum forms, which releases progesterone.
How does the combined pill work? ~~ Oestrogen and progesterone inhibit FSH and LH, so eggs do not mature or get released.
Compare hormonal and barrier contraception. ~~ Hormonal (pill, implant): more effective but side effects, no STI protection. Barrier (condoms): no hormones, protect against STIs, less reliable if misused.
How does clomifene therapy treat infertility? ~~ (Higher) It increases FSH and LH release, stimulating egg maturation and ovulation.
Describe IVF. ~~ (Higher) FSH and LH given to mature several eggs; eggs collected and fertilised by sperm in a lab; embryos grown then one or two implanted in the uterus.
Define homeostasis. ~~ Maintaining a constant internal environment (e.g. temperature, blood glucose, water) despite external changes.
What happens when blood glucose concentration is too high? ~~ Pancreas releases insulin; liver and muscle cells take up glucose and convert it to glycogen; glucose level falls.
What happens when blood glucose concentration is too low? ~~ (Higher) Pancreas releases glucagon; liver converts glycogen back to glucose, releasing it into the blood.
Exam trap: glycogen, glucagon or glucose? ~~ Glucose is the sugar; glycogen is the storage carbohydrate; glucagon is the hormone that converts glycogen to glucose.
Compare type 1 and type 2 diabetes. ~~ Type 1: pancreas makes no insulin, treated with insulin injections. Type 2: cells stop responding to insulin, controlled by diet, exercise and drugs; linked to obesity.
Calculate BMI for 95 kg and 1.80 m. ~~ BMI = 95 ÷ 1.80² = 95 ÷ 3.24 ≈ 29.3 kg/m², overweight (obese is 30 or above).
How does the body respond when too hot? ~~ (Biology only) Sweating (evaporation cools skin), vasodilation of skin arterioles so more heat lost by radiation, hairs lie flat.
How does the body respond when too cold? ~~ (Biology only) Shivering (respiration releases heat), vasoconstriction reduces blood to skin, hairs raised to trap air, sweating stops.
Where is body temperature monitored? ~~ (Biology only) Thermoregulatory centre in the hypothalamus detects blood temperature; skin receptors also send impulses.
Describe the structure of a nephron. ~~ (Biology only) Bowman's capsule (glomerulus), proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct.
What happens during ultrafiltration and selective reabsorption? ~~ (Biology only) High pressure forces small molecules from glomerulus into Bowman's capsule; all glucose and needed ions and water are reabsorbed in the tubule.
How does ADH control water content of blood? ~~ (Biology only) When blood is too concentrated, pituitary releases more ADH, making collecting ducts more permeable so more water is reabsorbed; urine is concentrated.
How is urea formed? ~~ (Biology only) Excess amino acids are deaminated in the liver, forming ammonia, which is converted into urea and excreted by the kidneys.
Compare dialysis and kidney transplant. ~~ (Biology only) Dialysis: regular, time-consuming, dialysis fluid allows diffusion of urea out. Transplant: long-term, but risk of rejection, needs immunosuppressants and a donor.
How does testosterone affect males at puberty? ~~ Stimulates sperm production and secondary sexual characteristics such as facial hair and deeper voice.
Why must a hormone affect only certain organs? ~~ Only target cells have specific receptors complementary to the hormone.

## 8
Why do larger organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems? ~~ They have a smaller surface area to volume ratio and longer diffusion distances, so diffusion alone is too slow.
Calculate the SA:V ratio of a 2 cm cube. ~~ SA = 6 × 2² = 24 cm²; volume = 2³ = 8 cm³; ratio = 24 : 8 = 3 : 1.
What substances must be transported into and out of the body? ~~ In: oxygen, nutrients, water. Out: carbon dioxide and urea.
How are alveoli adapted for gas exchange? ~~ Large surface area, walls one cell thick (short diffusion distance), rich capillary network and ventilation maintain a steep concentration gradient.
State Fick's law. ~~ (Biology only) Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × concentration difference) ÷ thickness of membrane.
List the components of blood and their roles. ~~ Red blood cells carry oxygen; white blood cells fight pathogens; platelets clot blood; plasma transports dissolved CO₂, glucose, urea, hormones.
How are red blood cells adapted? ~~ Biconcave shape for large surface area, no nucleus so more room for haemoglobin, which binds oxygen.
Name two types of white blood cell and their roles. ~~ Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens; lymphocytes produce antibodies.
Describe the structure of arteries. ~~ Thick muscular and elastic walls and small lumen to withstand and maintain high pressure from the heart.
Describe the structure of veins. ~~ Thin walls, large lumen, valves to prevent backflow of low-pressure blood returning to the heart.
How are capillaries adapted? ~~ Walls one cell thick for short diffusion distance; narrow and highly branched for a large surface area.
Trace blood through the heart. ~~ Vena cava, right atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary artery, lungs, pulmonary vein, left atrium, left ventricle, aorta.
Why is the left ventricle wall thicker than the right? ~~ It pumps blood at higher pressure around the whole body, whereas the right pumps only to the lungs.
Why is the human circulatory system called a double system? ~~ Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: once to the lungs and once to the body.
What is the function of valves in the heart? ~~ Prevent backflow of blood, ensuring it flows in one direction.
State the equation for cardiac output. ~~ Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.
Heart rate 70 bpm, stroke volume 75 cm³. Cardiac output? ~~ 75 × 70 = 5250 cm³ per minute (5.25 dm³/min).
Cardiac output is 6000 cm³/min and heart rate is 80 bpm. Find stroke volume. ~~ Stroke volume = 6000 ÷ 80 = 75 cm³.
State the word and symbol equation for aerobic respiration. ~~ Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O.
State the equation for anaerobic respiration in muscles. ~~ Glucose → lactic acid.
State the equation for anaerobic respiration in yeast and plants. ~~ Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (fermentation).
Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration. ~~ Aerobic needs oxygen, releases much more energy, products CO₂ and water. Anaerobic needs no oxygen, releases less energy, incomplete breakdown of glucose.
What is an oxygen debt? ~~ Extra oxygen needed after exercise to break down lactic acid that built up during anaerobic respiration.
Why is respiration exothermic? ~~ It transfers energy to the environment; it is a continuous process in all living cells.
Give uses of energy from respiration. ~~ Muscle contraction, building larger molecules (e.g. proteins), active transport, maintaining body temperature in mammals and birds.
Core practical: how does a respirometer measure respiration rate? ~~ Organisms (e.g. woodlice or seeds) in a sealed tube with soda lime to absorb CO₂; oxygen uptake moves coloured liquid along a capillary; distance per minute.
Core practical: why is soda lime used and what is the control? ~~ Soda lime absorbs CO₂ so liquid movement shows only oxygen use. Control: same tube with glass beads of equal volume instead of organisms.
Core practical: how is temperature varied in the respirometer practical? ~~ Place tubes in water baths at different temperatures, leave to equilibrate, then measure distance moved by liquid in a set time.
A liquid moves 18 mm in 6 minutes. What is the rate of respiration? ~~ 18 ÷ 6 = 3 mm per minute (of oxygen consumption).
Exam trap: do plants respire only at night? ~~ No. Plants respire all the time; in light, photosynthesis may exceed respiration so net gas exchange is CO₂ uptake.

## 9
Define population, community and ecosystem. ~~ Population: all organisms of one species in an area. Community: populations of different species. Ecosystem: community plus abiotic factors interacting.
Give examples of abiotic factors. ~~ Light intensity, temperature, water availability, pollutants, CO₂ and oxygen levels, soil pH and minerals.
Give examples of biotic factors. ~~ Competition, predation, disease, food availability.
What do animals and plants compete for? ~~ Animals: food, mates, territory, water. Plants: light, water, space, mineral ions.
Define interdependence. ~~ The way species in a community depend on each other for food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal; a change in one affects others.
Define parasitism and mutualism. ~~ Parasitism: parasite benefits, host harmed (e.g. tapeworm, fleas). Mutualism: both benefit (e.g. oxpeckers on buffalo, nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legumes).
Explain predator–prey cycles. ~~ Prey increase, so predators increase; more predators eat more prey, so prey fall; predators then fall. Predator peaks lag behind prey peaks.
Core practical: how do you estimate population size with quadrats? ~~ Place quadrats randomly (random coordinates), count organisms in each, find mean per quadrat, then multiply up to total area.
Mean 4 daisies per 0.25 m² quadrat; field is 100 m². Estimate population. ~~ 4 ÷ 0.25 = 16 per m²; 16 × 100 = 1600 daisies.
Core practical: when and how is a belt transect used? ~~ To study how distribution changes across a gradient (e.g. from shade to light); place quadrats at regular intervals along a tape and measure abiotic factors too.
Why must quadrats be placed randomly? ~~ To avoid bias and get a representative sample; a larger number of quadrats improves reliability.
Why does energy decrease along a food chain? ~~ Energy is lost through respiration (heat), movement, excretion and undigested material in faeces, so only about 10% is transferred.
Calculate efficiency of energy transfer if 2000 kJ goes to 150 kJ. ~~ (Biology only) Efficiency = energy transferred ÷ energy available × 100 = 150 ÷ 2000 × 100 = 7.5%.
Why is a pyramid of biomass usually more useful than a pyramid of numbers? ~~ It always has a pyramid shape and shows the mass of living material at each level, whereas numbers ignore organism size.
Define biodiversity. ~~ The variety of different species of organisms on Earth or within an ecosystem.
What is eutrophication? ~~ Fertiliser runoff adds nitrates/phosphates to water; algae grow and block light; plants die; bacteria decompose them using oxygen; fish and other organisms die.
How can fish farming affect biodiversity? ~~ Waste and food cause eutrophication; diseases and parasites spread to wild fish; escaped fish compete with or breed with wild populations.
How do non-indigenous species affect ecosystems? ~~ They may outcompete native species for resources, carry disease, or lack natural predators (e.g. grey squirrels vs red squirrels).
How do conservation and reforestation increase biodiversity? ~~ Protect habitats and endangered species, breeding programmes; replanting trees restores habitats and food sources for many species.
Describe the carbon cycle. ~~ Photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration by organisms and decomposers and combustion release CO₂; carbon passes along food chains by feeding; some forms fossil fuels.
What role do decomposers play in material cycles? ~~ Bacteria and fungi break down dead matter and waste, releasing CO₂ by respiration and returning mineral ions to the soil.
Describe the water cycle. ~~ Evaporation and transpiration, condensation forming clouds, precipitation, run-off and percolation back to rivers and seas.
How is potable water produced in areas of drought? ~~ Desalination by distillation or reverse osmosis to remove salt from seawater.
Describe the nitrogen cycle. ~~ Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert N₂ to ammonia/nitrates; decomposers turn proteins into ammonia; nitrifying bacteria convert ammonia to nitrates; denitrifying bacteria return N₂ to air.
How do farmers increase soil nitrates? ~~ Crop rotation with legumes (nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules), adding manure or chemical fertilisers.
What are indicator species? ~~ (Biology only) Species whose presence or absence shows pollution levels, e.g. lichens (air quality, SO₂), bloodworms and sludgeworms (polluted water), stonefly larvae (clean water).
Practical: how is temperature's effect on decomposition investigated? ~~ (Biology only) Milk, lipase and phenolphthalein/sodium carbonate in water baths; time for pink to disappear as fatty acids lower pH; repeat at different temperatures.
What factors affect the rate of decay? ~~ (Biology only) Temperature, water content and oxygen availability; warm, moist, aerobic conditions speed up decomposition.
Calculate rate of decay if 120 g of compost loses 30 g in 10 days. ~~ (Biology only) Rate = mass lost ÷ time = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 g per day.
Give biological factors affecting food security. ~~ (Biology only) Rising population, changing diets, new pests and pathogens, environmental change, cost of agricultural inputs, conflict.
