# OCR Gateway Science Chemistry A (J248) content, mapped to this board's topics C1–C6.

## C1
What are the three states of matter? ~~ Solid, liquid and gas.
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a solid. ~~ Closely packed in a regular arrangement, vibrating about fixed positions.
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a liquid. ~~ Close together but randomly arranged, able to move past each other.
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a gas. ~~ Far apart and randomly arranged, moving quickly in all directions.
What is the name for the change from liquid to gas at the boiling point? ~~ Boiling (evaporation also happens at the surface below the boiling point).
What is the name for the change directly from solid to gas? ~~ Sublimation.
What is the name for the change from gas to liquid? ~~ Condensation.
What is the name for the change from liquid to solid? ~~ Freezing.
What happens to the particles when a solid melts? ~~ They gain energy, vibrate more and overcome enough of the forces between them to move past each other.
What is the difference between a physical change and a chemical change? ~~ A physical change (e.g. change of state) forms no new substance and is easily reversed; a chemical change forms new substances.
In a physical change, what happens to the particles themselves? ~~ They stay the same; only their arrangement, movement and energy change.
What is one limitation of the particle model that shows particles as solid spheres? ~~ It ignores the forces between particles, the space inside particles and the fact that particles are not solid or inelastic.
Who proposed that atoms are tiny solid spheres that cannot be divided? ~~ John Dalton.
What was Thomson's model of the atom? ~~ The plum pudding model: a ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it.
What did Geiger and Marsden's alpha-scattering experiment show? ~~ Most alpha particles passed straight through gold foil, but a few were deflected, showing a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
Whose model replaced the plum pudding model after the alpha-scattering experiment? ~~ Rutherford's nuclear model.
What did Bohr add to the model of the atom? ~~ Electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels (shells).
Why has the model of the atom changed over time? ~~ New experimental evidence could not be explained by the old model.
Describe the structure of an atom. ~~ A small, positively charged nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons in shells.
How does the radius of the nucleus compare with the radius of the atom? ~~ The nucleus is much smaller, about 1/100 000 of the atom's radius.
Where is most of the mass of an atom? ~~ In the nucleus.
What is the typical order of magnitude of the radius of an atom? ~~ About 10⁻¹⁰ m.
What are the relative charges of a proton, a neutron and an electron? ~~ Proton +1, neutron 0, electron −1.
What are the relative masses of a proton, a neutron and an electron? ~~ Proton 1, neutron 1, electron about 1/2000 (negligible).
Why is an atom neutral overall? ~~ It has the same number of protons as electrons.
What does the atomic number tell you? ~~ The number of protons in the nucleus.
What does the mass number tell you? ~~ The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in an atom of ³⁵Cl (atomic number 17)? ~~ 17 protons, 18 neutrons, 17 electrons.
What are isotopes? ~~ Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
How many electrons are in a Mg²⁺ ion (atomic number 12)? ~~ 10 — the atom has lost 2 electrons.

## C2
What does 'pure' mean in chemistry? ~~ A single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance.
How does the everyday meaning of 'pure' differ from the scientific one? ~~ In everyday use it means natural or with nothing added (e.g. pure orange juice), even though it is a mixture.
How can melting point be used to test purity? ~~ A pure substance melts at a sharp, fixed temperature; an impure one melts over a range, usually lower.
What is a formulation? ~~ A mixture designed as a useful product, with each component in a measured quantity, e.g. an alloy, paint or medicine.
How is relative formula mass (Mr) calculated? ~~ Add up the relative atomic masses (Ar) of all the atoms in the formula.
What is the Mr of CO₂? (C = 12, O = 16) ~~ 12 + 16 + 16 = 44.
What is an empirical formula? ~~ The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
What is the empirical formula of C₆H₁₂O₆? ~~ CH₂O.
Which technique separates an insoluble solid from a liquid? ~~ Filtration.
How do you obtain crystals of a soluble salt from its solution? ~~ Heat to evaporate some of the water, leave to cool so crystals form, then filter and dry them (crystallisation).
Which technique separates a solvent from a solution, e.g. water from salty water? ~~ Simple distillation.
When is fractional distillation used? ~~ To separate a mixture of liquids with similar boiling points, e.g. ethanol and water or crude oil.
What are the two phases in chromatography? ~~ A stationary phase (paper or TLC plate) and a mobile phase (the solvent).
What does separation in chromatography depend on? ~~ How each substance distributes between the stationary and mobile phases.
What is the formula for an Rf value? ~~ Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front.
How does a chromatogram show a pure substance? ~~ It gives a single spot in every solvent.
Why is the start line in paper chromatography drawn in pencil? ~~ Pencil is insoluble, so it will not run with the solvent.
How are ions formed? ~~ Atoms lose or gain electrons — metals lose electrons to form positive ions, non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions.
What is an ionic bond? ~~ The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
What is a covalent bond? ~~ A shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms.
What is metallic bonding? ~~ The attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
What is the electronic structure of sodium (atomic number 11)? ~~ 2.8.1
How does the group number relate to electronic structure? ~~ The group number equals the number of electrons in the outer shell (for Groups 1–7).
How did Mendeleev arrange the elements, and how was this refined? ~~ By atomic mass, leaving gaps for undiscovered elements; the modern table is arranged by atomic number.
Why does diamond have a very high melting point and not conduct electricity? ~~ Each carbon forms four strong covalent bonds in a giant structure, and there are no free electrons.
Why does graphite conduct electricity? ~~ Each carbon forms three bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that can move between the layers.
What is graphene? ~~ A single layer of graphite: one atom thick, strong and a good conductor.
Why do simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points? ~~ Only weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome, not the covalent bonds.
Why do ionic compounds conduct electricity when molten or dissolved but not when solid? ~~ The ions are free to move when molten or dissolved; in a solid they are held in fixed positions.
How big are nanoparticles? ~~ Between 1 nm and 100 nm across (a few hundred atoms or fewer).

## C3
What is the formula of sulfuric acid? ~~ H₂SO₄
What is the formula of the ammonium ion? ~~ NH₄⁺
What is the formula of calcium hydroxide? ~~ Ca(OH)₂
What do the state symbols (s), (l), (g) and (aq) mean? ~~ Solid, liquid, gas, and aqueous (dissolved in water).
What is the law of conservation of mass? ~~ No atoms are made or lost in a reaction, so the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants.
Why might mass appear to decrease when a reaction is done in an open flask? ~~ A gas product escapes into the air.
What is a mole? ~~ The amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant).
What is the equation linking mass, moles and Mr? ~~ Amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ Mr.
How many moles are in 9 g of water? (Mr = 18) ~~ 9 ÷ 18 = 0.5 mol.
What is a limiting reactant? ~~ The reactant that is completely used up, so it limits the amount of product formed.
What is an exothermic reaction? ~~ A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings rises.
What is an endothermic reaction? ~~ A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings falls.
What is activation energy? ~~ The minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur.
On a reaction profile for an exothermic reaction, where are the products? ~~ Lower in energy than the reactants.
Is bond breaking exothermic or endothermic? ~~ Endothermic — energy is taken in to break bonds.
How is the overall energy change found from bond energies? ~~ Energy taken in to break bonds − energy released making bonds (negative = exothermic).
What is oxidation in terms of oxygen? ~~ Gain of oxygen.
What is oxidation in terms of electrons? ~~ Loss of electrons (OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain).
What ions do acids produce in water? ~~ Hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq).
What ions do alkalis produce in water? ~~ Hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq).
What is the ionic equation for neutralisation? ~~ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
What are the products when a metal carbonate reacts with an acid? ~~ A salt, water and carbon dioxide.
What are the products when a metal reacts with an acid? ~~ A salt and hydrogen.
What is the difference between a strong acid and a weak acid? ~~ A strong acid is fully ionised in water; a weak acid is only partially ionised.
What is the difference between a dilute acid and a concentrated acid? ~~ A dilute acid has a small amount of acid per unit volume; a concentrated acid has a large amount.
If H⁺ concentration increases by a factor of 10, what happens to the pH? ~~ It decreases by 1.
How can pH be measured? ~~ With universal indicator (compare the colour with a chart) or a pH meter/probe.
In electrolysis with inert electrodes, where are metals or hydrogen formed? ~~ At the cathode (negative electrode).
What are the products of electrolysis of molten sodium chloride? ~~ Sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode.
What are the products of electrolysis of aqueous copper sulfate with inert electrodes? ~~ Copper at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.

## C4
What is the trend in reactivity down Group 1? ~~ Reactivity increases.
Why does Group 1 reactivity increase down the group? ~~ The outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily.
What forms when sodium reacts with water? ~~ Sodium hydroxide and hydrogen: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
What is the trend in reactivity down Group 7? ~~ Reactivity decreases.
Why does Group 7 reactivity decrease down the group? ~~ The outer shell is further from the nucleus, so an extra electron is gained less easily.
What is the trend in melting and boiling points down Group 7? ~~ They increase.
What is the state and colour of chlorine at room temperature? ~~ A pale green gas.
What is the state and colour of bromine at room temperature? ~~ A red-brown liquid.
What is a displacement reaction of halogens? ~~ A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution, e.g. Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂.
What is seen when chlorine water is added to potassium iodide solution? ~~ The solution turns brown as iodine forms.
Why are the noble gases unreactive? ~~ They have a full outer shell of electrons.
What is the trend in boiling point down Group 0? ~~ Boiling point increases.
Give three typical properties of transition metals compared with Group 1 metals. ~~ Higher melting points, higher densities, less reactive (also form coloured compounds and ions with different charges).
Why are transition metals useful in industry? ~~ They and their compounds are often good catalysts, e.g. iron in the Haber process.
How is the reactivity of a metal related to the ions it forms? ~~ The more easily a metal forms its positive ion, the more reactive it is.
How can an order of reactivity of metals be found experimentally? ~~ Compare their reactions with water or dilute acid, or use displacement reactions with metal salt solutions.
What is seen when magnesium is added to copper sulfate solution? ~~ A brown coating of copper forms and the blue colour fades — magnesium displaces copper.
What is the test for hydrogen? ~~ A lit splint gives a squeaky pop.
What is the test for oxygen? ~~ It relights a glowing splint.
What is the test for carbon dioxide? ~~ It turns limewater milky (cloudy).
What is the test for chlorine? ~~ It bleaches damp litmus paper white.
What colour precipitate does Cu²⁺ form with sodium hydroxide solution? ~~ Blue.
What colour precipitates do Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ form with sodium hydroxide solution? ~~ Fe²⁺ gives green; Fe³⁺ gives orange-brown.
Which two cations give a white precipitate with sodium hydroxide, and how are they told apart? ~~ Ca²⁺ and Zn²⁺; the zinc hydroxide precipitate dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide does not.
What is the test for carbonate ions? ~~ Add dilute acid — it fizzes and the gas turns limewater milky.
What colours do chloride, bromide and iodide give with acidified silver nitrate? ~~ Chloride white, bromide cream, iodide yellow precipitate.
What is the test for sulfate ions? ~~ Add acidified barium chloride (or barium nitrate) — a white precipitate forms.
What are the flame test colours for Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cu²⁺? ~~ Li⁺ crimson, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Cu²⁺ green.
How is a flame test carried out? ~~ Dip a clean nichrome wire loop in the sample and hold it in a hot (blue) Bunsen flame.
What are the advantages of instrumental methods of analysis? ~~ They are more sensitive, more accurate and faster.

## C5
What is the unit of concentration in moles? ~~ mol/dm³
How is concentration in mol/dm³ calculated? ~~ Concentration = amount (mol) ÷ volume (dm³).
How do you convert cm³ to dm³? ~~ Divide by 1000.
What is the concentration of 0.2 mol of solute in 500 cm³ of solution? ~~ 0.2 ÷ 0.5 = 0.4 mol/dm³.
Which apparatus measures out exactly 25.0 cm³ of alkali for a titration? ~~ A volumetric pipette (with a pipette filler).
What is the end point of a titration? ~~ The point at which the indicator changes colour, showing the acid and alkali have exactly reacted.
What are concordant titres? ~~ Titres within 0.10 cm³ of each other; these are used to calculate the mean.
Why is the conical flask swirled during a titration? ~~ To mix the reactants so the indicator colour change is seen at the right moment.
What volume does 1 mole of any gas occupy at room temperature and pressure? ~~ 24 dm³
What volume does 0.25 mol of oxygen occupy at RTP? ~~ 0.25 × 24 = 6 dm³.
What is percentage yield? ~~ (Actual mass of product ÷ theoretical mass) × 100.
Give two reasons why the actual yield is less than the theoretical yield. ~~ The reaction may be reversible, product is lost when separating it, or side reactions occur.
What is atom economy? ~~ (Mr of desired product ÷ total Mr of all reactants) × 100.
Why is a high atom economy desirable? ~~ Less waste is produced, so the process is more sustainable and often cheaper.
Name three ways to follow the rate of a reaction. ~~ Measure gas volume over time, mass loss over time, or time for a colour change or precipitate to appear.
What does the gradient of a volume of gas–time graph show? ~~ The rate of reaction.
In the disappearing cross experiment, how is rate related to the time taken? ~~ Rate is proportional to 1/time.
What is the effect of increasing temperature on rate, and why? ~~ The rate increases — particles move faster, so they collide more often and more collisions have energy ≥ the activation energy.
Why does increasing concentration increase the rate? ~~ There are more particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent.
Why does increasing the pressure of a gas increase the rate? ~~ Particles are closer together, so collisions are more frequent.
Why does powder react faster than lumps of the same mass? ~~ It has a larger surface area to volume ratio, so there are more frequent collisions.
What is a catalyst? ~~ A substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up.
How does a catalyst increase the rate? ~~ It provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
What are enzymes? ~~ Biological catalysts.
Which black powder catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide? ~~ Manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂.
What is a reversible reaction? ~~ A reaction in which the products can react to re-form the reactants (shown by ⇌).
What is dynamic equilibrium? ~~ In a closed system, the forward and reverse reactions happen at the same rate, so concentrations stay constant.
What happens to the equilibrium position if the temperature is increased? ~~ It shifts in the endothermic direction.
What happens to the equilibrium position if the pressure is increased? ~~ It shifts towards the side with fewer moles of gas.
What happens to the equilibrium position if the concentration of a reactant is increased? ~~ It shifts to the right, making more product.

## C6
How are metals less reactive than carbon extracted from their ores? ~~ By reduction with carbon, e.g. heating the oxide with carbon (coke).
Why is aluminium extracted by electrolysis rather than with carbon? ~~ Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot reduce its oxide.
Why is aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite for electrolysis? ~~ It lowers the operating temperature, saving energy.
What are phytoextraction and bioleaching? ~~ Biological metal extraction: plants absorb metal compounds and are burned to ash; bacteria produce leachate solutions containing metal compounds.
What is the equation for the Haber process? ~~ N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
What conditions are used in the Haber process? ~~ About 450 °C, 200 atmospheres and an iron catalyst.
Why is a compromise temperature used in the Haber process? ~~ A lower temperature gives a higher yield but too slow a rate; 450 °C gives a reasonable yield quickly.
Which three elements do fertilisers mainly supply? ~~ Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium (NPK).
What is a life-cycle assessment? ~~ An assessment of the environmental impact of a product at each stage: raw materials, manufacture, use and disposal.
What is the composition of brass? ~~ Copper and zinc.
What is the composition of bronze? ~~ Copper and tin.
What two substances are needed for iron to rust? ~~ Oxygen and water.
How does sacrificial protection prevent rusting? ~~ A more reactive metal (e.g. zinc) is attached and corrodes instead of the iron.
What is galvanising? ~~ Coating iron or steel with zinc, which acts as a barrier and gives sacrificial protection.
What is a homologous series? ~~ A family of compounds with the same functional group and general formula, differing by CH₂.
What is the general formula of the alkanes? ~~ CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
What is the functional group of the alkenes? ~~ The C=C double bond.
What is the test for an alkene? ~~ Bromine water turns from orange to colourless.
What is the functional group of the alcohols? ~~ –OH
What is the functional group of the carboxylic acids? ~~ –COOH
What is addition polymerisation? ~~ Many small alkene monomers join together to form a long polymer chain, with no other product.
What is the difference between addition and condensation polymerisation? ~~ Condensation polymerisation joins monomers with two functional groups and releases a small molecule such as water.
Name three naturally occurring polymers. ~~ DNA (from nucleotides), proteins (from amino acids) and starch or cellulose (from sugars).
How is crude oil separated into fractions? ~~ Fractional distillation: it is vaporised and the fractions condense at different heights in a column, depending on their boiling points.
Why do larger hydrocarbon molecules have higher boiling points? ~~ They have stronger intermolecular forces, which need more energy to overcome.
What is cracking? ~~ Breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes, using a catalyst and high temperature.
What is the only product of a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell? ~~ Water.
How did the early atmosphere become rich in oxygen? ~~ Photosynthesis by algae and plants removed carbon dioxide and released oxygen.
How do greenhouse gases warm the Earth? ~~ They absorb infrared radiation emitted from the Earth's surface and re-emit it, warming the atmosphere.
Name a source and an effect of sulfur dioxide pollution. ~~ Burning fuels containing sulfur impurities; it causes acid rain.
