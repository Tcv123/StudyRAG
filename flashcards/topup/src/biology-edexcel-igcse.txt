## 1.1
List the eight characteristics shared by living organisms. ~~ Nutrition, respiration, excretion, response to surroundings, movement, control of internal conditions, reproduction, growth and development.
What is meant by excretion as a characteristic of life? ~~ Removal of metabolic waste products (e.g. CO2, urea) made inside the organism's cells.
Why is egestion not the same as excretion? ~~ Egestion removes undigested food (faeces) that was never part of the body's metabolism; excretion removes waste made by metabolic reactions.
Name the five groups of organisms studied in 4BI1. ~~ Plants, animals, fungi, protoctists, bacteria (plus viruses, which are not living organisms).
Give three features of plants. ~~ Multicellular; cells contain chloroplasts and photosynthesise; cellulose cell walls; store carbohydrate as starch or sucrose.
Give two named examples of plants. ~~ Flowering plants such as a cereal (e.g. maize) and a herbaceous legume (e.g. peas or beans).
Give three features of animals. ~~ Multicellular; no chloroplasts so cannot photosynthesise; no cell walls; usually have nervous coordination and can move; store carbohydrate as glycogen.
Give two named examples of animals. ~~ A mammal (e.g. human) and an insect (e.g. housefly or mosquito).
How do animals store carbohydrate? ~~ As glycogen.
Give the key features of fungi. ~~ Cannot photosynthesise; body of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium; cell walls of chitin; feed by saprotrophic nutrition; may store glycogen.
What is saprotrophic nutrition? ~~ Secreting extracellular digestive enzymes onto dead or decaying organic matter and absorbing the soluble products.
Name two examples of fungi. ~~ Mucor (multicellular, hyphae) and yeast (single-celled).
What are hyphae and a mycelium? ~~ Hyphae are thread-like structures making up a fungus; a mycelium is the network of many hyphae.
What material makes up fungal cell walls? ~~ Chitin.
Give the key features of bacteria. ~~ Microscopic single cells; no nucleus, with a circular chromosome of DNA; may have plasmids; cell wall, cell membrane and cytoplasm; some photosynthesise but most feed on other organisms.
What is a plasmid? ~~ A small circular ring of DNA found in bacterial cells, separate from the main chromosome.
Name two example bacteria and their significance. ~~ Lactobacillus bulgaricus (rod-shaped, used to make yoghurt) and Pneumococcus (spherical, causes pneumonia).
Give the key features of protoctists. ~~ Microscopic single-celled organisms with a nucleus; some are plant-like with chloroplasts, others animal-like.
Name two examples of protoctists. ~~ Amoeba (animal-like, lives in pond water) and Chlorella (plant-like, has chloroplasts); Plasmodium causes malaria.
Give the key features of viruses. ~~ Small particles, smaller than bacteria; parasitic and can only reproduce inside living cells; protein coat around DNA or RNA; no cellular structure.
Why are viruses not considered living organisms? ~~ They are not cells and can only reproduce inside a host's living cells; they show no independent nutrition, respiration or excretion.
Name three examples of viruses. ~~ Tobacco mosaic virus (stops chloroplast formation, discoloured leaves), influenza virus (flu) and HIV (causes AIDS).
What is a pathogen? ~~ A microorganism that causes disease.
Give one example of a pathogen from each of fungi, bacteria, protoctists and viruses. ~~ Fungi: athlete's foot fungus; bacteria: Pneumococcus; protoctists: Plasmodium; viruses: HIV or influenza.
Which group has cells with a nucleus but is mostly unicellular? ~~ Protoctists.
Exam trap: do all bacteria lack a cell wall? ~~ No. Bacteria have a cell wall (not made of cellulose); they lack a nucleus, not a wall.
How does the cell wall differ between plants, fungi and bacteria? ~~ Plants: cellulose; fungi: chitin; bacteria: a wall made of different polymers (not cellulose or chitin).
Which organisms store carbohydrate as starch and which as glycogen? ~~ Plants store starch (or sucrose); animals and some fungi store glycogen.
How is Plasmodium transmitted to humans? ~~ By the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, which acts as a vector.
Why is yeast classified as a fungus despite being single-celled? ~~ It has a nucleus, a cell wall of chitin, cannot photosynthesise and feeds saprotrophically like other fungi.

## 2.1
State the levels of organisation from smallest to largest. ~~ Organelles, cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, organism.
Define a tissue. ~~ A group of similar cells working together to perform a particular function, e.g. muscle tissue.
Define an organ. ~~ A structure made of several different tissues working together to perform a specific function, e.g. the heart or leaf.
Define an organ system. ~~ A group of organs working together to carry out a function, e.g. the digestive system.
What is the function of the nucleus? ~~ Contains genetic material (chromosomes of DNA) that controls the activities of the cell.
What is the function of the cytoplasm? ~~ Site of most chemical reactions in the cell; contains enzymes and organelles.
What is the function of the cell membrane? ~~ Controls which substances enter and leave the cell; partially permeable.
What is the function of the mitochondria? ~~ Site of aerobic respiration, releasing energy for the cell.
What is the function of ribosomes? ~~ Site of protein synthesis.
What is the function of chloroplasts? ~~ Contain chlorophyll which absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.
What is the function of the cell wall in plant cells? ~~ Made of cellulose; strengthens and supports the cell and stops it bursting when it takes in water.
What is the function of the permanent vacuole? ~~ Contains cell sap; keeps the cell turgid to support the plant.
Which structures are found in plant cells but not animal cells? ~~ Cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole containing cell sap.
Which organelles are found in both plant and animal cells? ~~ Nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes.
Exam trap: do all plant cells contain chloroplasts? ~~ No. Root hair cells and other cells not exposed to light lack chloroplasts.
State the magnification formula. ~~ Magnification = image size ÷ actual (real) size, with both in the same units.
Calculate the magnification if image size is 12 mm and real size is 0.04 mm. ~~ Magnification = image ÷ real = 12 ÷ 0.04 = ×300.
A cell is 50 µm long and appears 25 mm long in a drawing. Calculate the magnification. ~~ Convert 25 mm to 25 000 µm. Magnification = 25 000 ÷ 50 = ×500.
An image is 6 mm wide at ×400 magnification. What is the actual width in µm? ~~ Actual = image ÷ magnification = 6 ÷ 400 = 0.015 mm = 15 µm.
How many micrometres are in one millimetre? ~~ 1000 µm = 1 mm (and 1000 nm = 1 µm).
What is cell differentiation? ~~ The process by which a cell becomes specialised, developing structures suited to a particular function.
Why is cell differentiation important? ~~ It produces specialised cells that form tissues and organs, allowing a multicellular organism to carry out many different functions efficiently.
What is a stem cell? ~~ An undifferentiated cell that can divide to produce more stem cells and can differentiate into many types of specialised cell.
Where are stem cells found? ~~ Embryos (embryonic stem cells), adult tissues such as bone marrow, and plant meristems at root and shoot tips.
Give two advantages of using stem cells in medicine. ~~ Could replace damaged or diseased cells (e.g. diabetes, paralysis); may grow tissues or organs for transplant, reducing waiting lists.
Give two disadvantages or concerns about using stem cells in medicine. ~~ Ethical objections to destroying embryos; risk of rejection, infection from contaminated cells, or uncontrolled division causing tumours.
How is a palisade cell adapted for its function? ~~ Packed with many chloroplasts near the top surface of the leaf to absorb maximum light for photosynthesis.
How is a red blood cell adapted to carry oxygen? ~~ Contains haemoglobin; no nucleus for more haemoglobin; biconcave shape gives a large surface area for diffusion.
How does a root hair cell's structure suit its function? ~~ Long projection increases surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions; many mitochondria for active transport.
Why must a cell contain both a nucleus and ribosomes to make enzymes? ~~ The nucleus holds the DNA code for the enzyme; ribosomes use that code to assemble the protein.

## 2.2
Which elements are found in carbohydrates? ~~ Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
Which elements are found in proteins? ~~ Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen (sometimes sulfur).
Which elements are found in lipids? ~~ Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (with much less oxygen than carbohydrates).
What are the building blocks of starch and glycogen? ~~ Glucose molecules (simple sugars) joined together.
What are the building blocks of proteins? ~~ Amino acids.
What are the building blocks of lipids? ~~ Fatty acids and glycerol (three fatty acids per glycerol).
Describe the test for starch. ~~ Add iodine solution; colour changes from orange-brown to blue-black if starch is present.
Describe the test for glucose (reducing sugar). ~~ Add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath (about 80 °C); blue turns green, yellow, orange or brick-red if present.
Describe the test for protein. ~~ Add biuret reagent (or sodium hydroxide then copper sulfate); blue turns purple/lilac if protein is present.
Describe the test for lipids. ~~ Shake sample with ethanol, then add to water; a cloudy white emulsion forms if lipid is present.
Exam trap: what must you do for the Benedict's test that is not needed for the others? ~~ Heat the mixture in a hot water bath; without heating, no colour change occurs.
What is an enzyme? ~~ A protein that acts as a biological catalyst, speeding up chemical reactions without being used up.
What is a biological catalyst? ~~ A substance in living organisms that increases the rate of a reaction without itself being changed.
What is the active site of an enzyme? ~~ The region of the enzyme with a specific shape where the substrate binds.
Why is each enzyme specific to one substrate? ~~ Only a substrate with a complementary shape fits the active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex (lock and key).
Describe the effect of increasing temperature up to the optimum on enzyme activity. ~~ Molecules gain kinetic energy, move faster and collide more often, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and the rate increases.
What happens to an enzyme above its optimum temperature? ~~ Bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits; the enzyme is denatured.
What does denatured mean? ~~ The enzyme's active site has permanently changed shape so it can no longer bind its substrate.
Exam trap: are enzymes killed at high temperatures? ~~ No. Enzymes are not alive; they are denatured, not killed.
What is the optimum temperature for most human enzymes? ~~ About 37 °C (body temperature).
How does pH affect enzyme activity? ~~ Each enzyme has an optimum pH; pH far from this alters the shape of the active site, denaturing the enzyme.
Give the optimum pH of pepsin and of amylase. ~~ Pepsin about pH 2 (stomach); salivary amylase about pH 7.
Describe how to investigate the effect of temperature on amylase activity. ~~ Mix starch and amylase at a set temperature in a water bath; sample every 30 s onto iodine on a spotting tile; time until iodine stays orange-brown. Repeat at other temperatures.
In the amylase experiment, what are the independent, dependent and control variables? ~~ Independent: temperature (or pH); dependent: time for starch to disappear; controls: volume and concentration of starch and amylase, pH (or temperature).
Why are the starch and amylase solutions left in the water bath before mixing? ~~ So both reach the required temperature before the reaction starts, giving an accurate result.
How is the rate calculated from the time taken for starch to disappear? ~~ Rate = 1 ÷ time. E.g. 120 s gives rate = 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹.
In the amylase practical, what is the role of buffer solution? ~~ Keeps the pH constant (or sets the pH being tested) so pH does not affect the results.
Why is a spotting tile with iodine used in the amylase practical? ~~ To test samples for starch at intervals; when iodine stays orange-brown, all starch has been broken down.
What enzyme breaks down starch, and into what? ~~ Amylase breaks starch into maltose (and then maltase converts maltose to glucose).
Why do enzymes not work well at very low temperatures, even though they are not denatured? ~~ Molecules have little kinetic energy, so there are fewer successful collisions between enzyme and substrate.

## 2.3
Define diffusion. ~~ The net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient. It is passive.
Define osmosis. ~~ The net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane.
Define active transport. ~~ Movement of substances from a lower to a higher concentration, against a concentration gradient, using energy from respiration.
Why does active transport require mitochondria? ~~ It needs energy released by respiration, which mainly occurs in mitochondria.
Give an example of active transport in plants. ~~ Root hair cells absorbing mineral ions (e.g. nitrate) from soil where they are less concentrated than in the cell.
Give an example of active transport in humans. ~~ Uptake of glucose by cells of the small intestine villi (and glucose reabsorption in the kidney).
List the factors that affect the rate of movement of substances into and out of cells. ~~ Surface area to volume ratio, distance (diffusion path), temperature and concentration gradient.
How does surface area to volume ratio affect diffusion? ~~ A larger ratio means more membrane relative to volume, so substances diffuse in faster relative to the cell's needs.
Calculate the SA:V ratio of a 2 cm cube. ~~ SA = 6 × 2 × 2 = 24 cm²; V = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 cm³; ratio = 24 : 8 = 3 : 1.
Calculate the SA:V ratio of a 1 cm cube. ~~ SA = 6 cm²; V = 1 cm³; ratio = 6 : 1, larger than bigger cubes.
Why do large multicellular organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems? ~~ Their small SA:V ratio and long diffusion distances mean diffusion alone across the body surface is too slow to supply all cells.
How does temperature affect the rate of diffusion? ~~ Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and diffusion is quicker.
How does a steeper concentration gradient affect diffusion? ~~ It increases the rate of diffusion.
What happens to an animal cell placed in pure water? ~~ Water enters by osmosis; the cell swells and may burst (lysis) because it has no cell wall.
What happens to a plant cell placed in pure water? ~~ Water enters by osmosis; vacuole swells and cell becomes turgid; the cell wall prevents bursting.
What is plasmolysis? ~~ When a plant cell loses water by osmosis in a concentrated solution, the cytoplasm and membrane shrink and pull away from the cell wall.
What does turgid mean? ~~ A plant cell that is full of water, with cytoplasm pushing against the cell wall, giving support.
What does flaccid mean? ~~ A plant cell that has lost water and is soft; the cytoplasm no longer presses against the wall.
Describe the potato cylinder osmosis practical. ~~ Cut equal cylinders, blot and weigh; place in different sucrose concentrations for a set time (e.g. 30 min); blot dry and reweigh; calculate percentage change in mass.
Why calculate percentage change in mass rather than change in mass? ~~ The cylinders may start at different masses, so percentage change allows fair comparison.
A potato piece goes from 2.50 g to 2.20 g. Calculate the percentage change in mass. ~~ Change = 2.20 − 2.50 = −0.30 g; % change = −0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −12%.
How do you find the concentration of potato cell sap from an osmosis graph? ~~ Plot % change in mass against concentration; where the line crosses zero, the solution has the same water potential as the cells.
Why are potato cylinders blotted before weighing? ~~ To remove surface solution so only water absorbed into the tissue is measured.
Describe a practical to investigate diffusion using agar cubes. ~~ Cut different-sized cubes of agar containing indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein); place in acid; time how long colour takes to disappear completely. Smaller cubes change faster.
What is the dependent variable in the agar cube diffusion experiment? ~~ Time for the colour to disappear completely (the cube to change colour throughout).
Describe a simple practical to show osmosis using visking tubing. ~~ Fill visking tubing with sugar solution, attach a capillary tube, place in water; the liquid rises up the tube as water enters by osmosis.
Exam trap: in osmosis answers, why should you avoid saying water moves 'from high concentration to low concentration'? ~~ It is ambiguous; say water moves from a dilute to a more concentrated solution (higher to lower water potential) across a partially permeable membrane.
What is a partially permeable membrane? ~~ A membrane that allows small molecules such as water through but not larger molecules such as sucrose.
Which processes require energy from respiration: diffusion, osmosis or active transport? ~~ Only active transport. Diffusion and osmosis are passive.
How does a shorter diffusion distance affect the rate of diffusion? ~~ It increases the rate, so exchange surfaces are often only one cell thick.

## 2.4
Write the word equation for photosynthesis. ~~ Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (in the presence of light and chlorophyll).
Write the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. ~~ 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2.
What is the role of chlorophyll in photosynthesis? ~~ Absorbs light energy and converts it to chemical energy stored in glucose.
Name the limiting factors of photosynthesis. ~~ Carbon dioxide concentration, light intensity and temperature.
What is a limiting factor? ~~ The factor in shortest supply that limits the rate of a reaction; increasing it increases the rate.
Explain why the rate of photosynthesis levels off as light intensity increases. ~~ Another factor, such as CO2 concentration or temperature, becomes limiting.
How is a leaf adapted for photosynthesis? ~~ Large surface area, thin for short diffusion distance, chloroplasts in palisade layer, stomata for gas exchange, air spaces in spongy mesophyll, veins (xylem) supply water.
Why do plants need magnesium ions? ~~ To make chlorophyll; deficiency causes yellow leaves (chlorosis).
Why do plants need nitrate ions? ~~ To make amino acids and proteins; deficiency causes stunted growth.
Describe how to test a leaf for starch. ~~ Boil leaf in water (kills cells), boil in ethanol in a water bath (removes chlorophyll), rinse in warm water to soften, add iodine; blue-black shows starch.
Why must ethanol be heated in a water bath, not over a flame? ~~ Ethanol is flammable.
How do you show that chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis? ~~ Destarch a variegated plant, expose to light, then test a leaf for starch; only the green areas turn blue-black.
How do you show that light is needed for photosynthesis? ~~ Destarch the plant in the dark, cover part of a leaf with foil, expose to light, then starch test; covered part stays orange-brown.
How do you show that CO2 is needed for photosynthesis? ~~ Destarch plant, enclose a leaf with soda lime (absorbs CO2) in a sealed bag, expose to light; starch test shows no starch.
What does destarching a plant mean and why is it done? ~~ Keeping it in the dark for 48 hours so stored starch is used up; any starch found later must have been made during the experiment.
Describe how to investigate the effect of light intensity on photosynthesis using pondweed. ~~ Place pondweed in water with sodium hydrogencarbonate at measured distances from a lamp; count bubbles or measure gas volume per minute at each distance.
Why is a heat shield or water bath used in the pondweed experiment? ~~ Keeps temperature constant so only light intensity changes.
Name the components of a balanced diet. ~~ Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, vitamins (A, C, D), minerals (calcium, iron), water and dietary fibre.
State the functions of vitamin C and its deficiency disease. ~~ Needed to make collagen for skin and connective tissue; deficiency causes scurvy.
State the function of iron and its deficiency disease. ~~ Needed to make haemoglobin in red blood cells; deficiency causes anaemia.
State the functions of calcium and vitamin D. ~~ Both needed for strong bones and teeth; vitamin D helps calcium absorption. Deficiency causes rickets.
Why is dietary fibre important? ~~ Adds bulk to food, helping peristalsis move food through the gut and preventing constipation.
How do energy requirements vary in humans? ~~ Increase with activity level and age to adulthood; males usually need more; pregnant women need more energy.
Name the enzyme, substrate and product for carbohydrate digestion. ~~ Amylase digests starch to maltose; maltase digests maltose to glucose.
Name the enzyme, substrate and products for protein digestion. ~~ Proteases (e.g. pepsin, trypsin) digest proteins into amino acids.
Name the enzyme, substrate and products for lipid digestion. ~~ Lipase digests lipids into fatty acids and glycerol.
What are the roles of bile? ~~ Made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder; neutralises stomach acid and emulsifies fats, increasing surface area for lipase.
Describe the order of the alimentary canal. ~~ Mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum then ileum), large intestine (colon then rectum), anus.
What is peristalsis? ~~ Waves of contraction of circular and longitudinal muscles in the gut wall that push food along the alimentary canal.
How are villi adapted for absorption? ~~ Large surface area (microvilli), wall one cell thick, good blood supply maintains a concentration gradient, lacteal absorbs fatty acids and glycerol.

## 2.5
Why do living organisms respire? ~~ To release energy from glucose for metabolic processes such as muscle contraction, active transport, growth and keeping warm.
Write the word equation for aerobic respiration. ~~ Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (energy released).
Write the balanced symbol equation for aerobic respiration. ~~ C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O.
Where does aerobic respiration take place? ~~ Mainly in the mitochondria.
Exam trap: does respiration produce energy? ~~ No. Energy cannot be made; respiration releases energy stored in glucose. Never write 'produces energy'.
What is ATP? ~~ The molecule that provides energy for cells; respiration releases energy used to make ATP.
Write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in animals. ~~ Glucose → lactic acid (small amount of energy released).
Write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in yeast and plants. ~~ Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (small amount of energy released).
Compare the energy released by aerobic and anaerobic respiration. ~~ Aerobic releases much more energy per glucose molecule because glucose is fully broken down; anaerobic breakdown is incomplete.
When does anaerobic respiration occur in human muscles? ~~ During vigorous exercise when oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough for aerobic respiration.
What is oxygen debt? ~~ The extra oxygen needed after exercise to break down lactic acid that built up during anaerobic respiration.
Why does heavy breathing continue after exercise has stopped? ~~ To repay the oxygen debt, supplying oxygen to break down lactic acid in the liver.
What is fermentation by yeast used for? ~~ Making bread (CO2 makes dough rise) and alcoholic drinks (ethanol).
Give two differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration. ~~ Aerobic needs oxygen and anaerobic does not; aerobic releases more energy; aerobic makes CO2 and water, anaerobic makes lactic acid (or ethanol and CO2).
Describe a practical to investigate CO2 production by germinating seeds. ~~ Seal germinating seeds in a tube with hydrogencarbonate indicator (not touching); indicator changes from red to yellow as CO2 is released. Use boiled seeds as a control.
Why are boiled seeds used as a control in respiration experiments? ~~ Boiling kills the seeds and denatures enzymes so they cannot respire, showing changes are due to respiration.
Describe how to show that respiring organisms release heat. ~~ Place germinating seeds in a vacuum flask with a thermometer, and boiled (disinfected) seeds in another; the live seeds' temperature rises.
Why are the boiled seeds disinfected in the heat release experiment? ~~ To kill microorganisms that would respire on the dead seeds and release heat, which would affect the result.
Why are vacuum flasks used in the seed respiration experiment? ~~ To insulate the seeds and reduce heat loss, so the temperature rise can be measured.
Why is the vacuum flask plugged with cotton wool and placed upside down? ~~ Cotton wool lets gases exchange so seeds can respire aerobically; heat rises, so inverting reduces heat loss through the opening.
What colour is hydrogencarbonate indicator at atmospheric, high and low CO2? ~~ Orange-red at atmospheric CO2; yellow at high CO2; purple at low CO2.
What is the role of limewater in respiration experiments? ~~ Detects CO2: limewater turns cloudy (milky) when CO2 is bubbled through it.
How could you investigate the effect of temperature on respiration in yeast? ~~ Mix yeast and glucose solution at different water bath temperatures; measure CO2 released (gas volume or bubbles per minute).
Name a control variable when investigating temperature and yeast respiration. ~~ Concentration and volume of glucose, mass of yeast, or the time allowed for gas collection.
Do plants respire at night only? ~~ No. Plants respire all the time, day and night; photosynthesis only happens in the light.
Name the waste product of anaerobic respiration in muscles and its effect. ~~ Lactic acid; builds up causing muscle fatigue and cramp.
Why is anaerobic respiration useful despite releasing less energy? ~~ It releases energy quickly when oxygen is in short supply, allowing muscles to keep working for a short time.
Why are respiration and combustion similar? ~~ Both break down glucose (fuel) using oxygen to release energy and produce CO2 and water.
How does a respirometer measure the rate of respiration? ~~ Organisms absorb O2; soda lime absorbs the CO2 released; volume decreases, so a coloured liquid moves along a capillary tube.
In a respirometer, what is the role of soda lime? ~~ It absorbs carbon dioxide, so any change in volume is due to oxygen uptake.

## 2.6
Why do plants need gas exchange? ~~ To take in CO2 and release O2 for photosynthesis, and take in O2 and release CO2 for respiration.
How does gas exchange in a leaf depend on light intensity? ~~ In bright light photosynthesis exceeds respiration so net CO2 uptake and O2 release; in dark only respiration so net CO2 release.
What is the compensation point? ~~ The light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, so there is no net gas exchange.
How is a leaf adapted for gas exchange? ~~ Large surface area, thin, stomata allow gases in and out, air spaces in spongy mesophyll give large internal surface area for diffusion.
What controls the opening and closing of stomata? ~~ Guard cells; when turgid the stoma opens, when flaccid it closes.
Describe the hydrogencarbonate indicator experiment on leaf gas exchange. ~~ Tubes with leaves and indicator: one in light, one covered in foil, one gauze-shaded, plus a no-leaf control. Light: purple; dark: yellow; shaded: stays orange-red.
Why is a tube with no leaf used in the hydrogencarbonate indicator experiment? ~~ It is a control, showing the colour change is caused by the leaf and not by light alone.
Describe the structure of the human gas exchange system in order of air flow. ~~ Nose/mouth, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli; protected by ribs and intercostal muscles, with the diaphragm below.
What is the role of the intercostal muscles and diaphragm in breathing in? ~~ External intercostals contract, ribs move up and out; diaphragm contracts and flattens; volume of thorax increases, pressure falls, and air enters.
What happens during breathing out at rest? ~~ Intercostal muscles and diaphragm relax; ribs move down and in, diaphragm domes upwards; thorax volume decreases, pressure rises and air is forced out.
How are alveoli adapted for gas exchange? ~~ Huge total surface area; walls one cell thick for short diffusion distance; moist lining; rich capillary network and ventilation maintain steep concentration gradients.
Why does a good blood supply help gas exchange in the alveoli? ~~ Blood constantly removes O2 and brings CO2, keeping a steep concentration gradient for diffusion.
What is the role of the ribs? ~~ Protect the lungs and heart and, moved by intercostal muscles, help change thorax volume.
What is the role of cartilage rings in the trachea? ~~ Keep the trachea open so air can flow freely, even when pressure inside falls.
What do goblet cells and ciliated cells do in the airways? ~~ Goblet cells make mucus that traps dust and pathogens; cilia beat to sweep the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed.
What is the role of the pleural membranes? ~~ They line the lungs and thorax; pleural fluid reduces friction during breathing.
How does tar in cigarette smoke affect health? ~~ Tar contains carcinogens that cause lung cancer; it also damages cilia and builds up mucus.
How does carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke affect the body? ~~ Combines irreversibly with haemoglobin, reducing the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
How does smoking cause coronary heart disease? ~~ Chemicals (e.g. nicotine) damage artery walls and raise blood pressure, leading to fatty deposits and narrowed coronary arteries.
How does smoking lead to bronchitis and emphysema? ~~ Damaged cilia let mucus build up causing infection (bronchitis); alveolar walls break down, reducing surface area (emphysema).
What is the effect of nicotine? ~~ Addictive; narrows blood vessels and increases heart rate and blood pressure.
Describe a practical to investigate the effect of exercise on breathing rate. ~~ Count breaths per minute at rest, then after exercise for set times (e.g. 1, 2, 3 min of stepping); repeat and calculate means.
Why does breathing rate increase during exercise? ~~ Muscles respire faster so need more O2 and produce more CO2; faster, deeper breathing increases gas exchange.
Give two control variables for a breathing-rate exercise investigation. ~~ Type and intensity of exercise, duration of exercise, same person or similar age and fitness.
Compare the composition of inhaled and exhaled air. ~~ Inhaled: about 21% O2, 0.04% CO2. Exhaled: about 16% O2, 4% CO2, more water vapour and warmer.
A person takes 15 breaths per minute with 0.5 dm³ per breath. Calculate ventilation rate. ~~ Ventilation = 15 × 0.5 = 7.5 dm³ per minute.
Why is the lung surface moist? ~~ Gases dissolve in the moisture before diffusing across the alveolar wall.
In which direction do O2 and CO2 diffuse at the alveolus? ~~ O2 diffuses from the alveolar air into the blood; CO2 diffuses from the blood into the alveolar air.
Why are fish and leaves not suited to gas exchange through their whole body surface like single cells? ~~ They are too large; their SA:V ratio is small, so specialised exchange surfaces (gills, spongy mesophyll) are needed.
Exam trap: does the diaphragm contract during exhalation at rest? ~~ No. At rest the diaphragm relaxes during exhalation; it contracts (flattens) during inhalation.

## 2.7
Why do multicellular organisms need a transport system? ~~ Their small SA:V ratio and large size mean diffusion is too slow to supply all cells with substances and remove waste.
What does xylem transport? ~~ Water and mineral ions from the roots to the rest of the plant, in one direction (upwards).
What does phloem transport? ~~ Sucrose and amino acids from the leaves to other parts of the plant, in both directions (translocation).
What is translocation? ~~ The movement of sucrose and amino acids through the phloem from where they are made to where they are used or stored.
How is water absorbed by root hair cells? ~~ By osmosis, from the dilute soil water into the more concentrated cell sap; the root hair gives a large surface area.
Define transpiration. ~~ The evaporation of water from the surface of mesophyll cells and its diffusion as vapour out of the leaf through the stomata.
What is the transpiration stream? ~~ The continuous flow of water from roots up the xylem to the leaves, pulled by water loss from the leaves.
How do light intensity, temperature, humidity and wind affect transpiration? ~~ Light and temperature increase it; humidity decreases it (smaller gradient); wind increases it (removes moist air near the leaf).
Why does light intensity increase the rate of transpiration? ~~ More light opens more stomata, so more water vapour can diffuse out.
Describe how a potometer is used to measure transpiration. ~~ Set up under water to avoid air bubbles; seal the shoot; measure the distance an air bubble moves in a capillary tube in a set time.
Exam trap: what does a potometer actually measure? ~~ The rate of water uptake by the shoot, which is assumed to approximately equal the rate of transpiration.
A bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake. ~~ Rate = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 mm per minute.
What are the four main components of blood? ~~ Plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells (phagocytes and lymphocytes) and platelets.
What is the role of plasma? ~~ Transports CO2, digested food (glucose, amino acids), urea, hormones and heat around the body.
How do phagocytes defend the body? ~~ They engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis.
How do lymphocytes defend the body? ~~ They produce antibodies that bind to specific antigens on pathogens, helping to destroy them.
How does vaccination give immunity? ~~ A vaccine contains harmless antigens; lymphocytes make antibodies and memory cells, so a later infection triggers a faster, larger antibody response.
What is the role of platelets in blood clotting? ~~ Platelets trigger conversion of soluble fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin, forming a mesh that traps red blood cells to make a clot.
Why is blood clotting important? ~~ It prevents blood loss and stops pathogens entering through the wound.
Describe the route of blood through the heart from the vena cava. ~~ Vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary artery, lungs, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, aorta.
Why is the left ventricle wall thicker than the right? ~~ It must pump blood at high pressure around the whole body, whereas the right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs.
What is the function of valves in the heart and veins? ~~ They prevent the backflow of blood, keeping it flowing in one direction.
How does heart rate change during exercise and why? ~~ It increases to deliver more O2 and glucose to respiring muscles and remove CO2 faster.
How does adrenaline affect heart rate? ~~ Adrenaline increases heart rate, preparing the body for 'fight or flight'.
Name the risk factors for coronary heart disease. ~~ Smoking, high-fat diet (high cholesterol), high blood pressure, lack of exercise, obesity, stress and genetic factors.
How are arteries adapted to their function? ~~ Thick, muscular, elastic walls withstand and maintain high pressure; narrow lumen.
How are veins adapted to their function? ~~ Thin walls, wide lumen and valves to keep low-pressure blood flowing back to the heart.
How are capillaries adapted to their function? ~~ Walls one cell thick for short diffusion distance; very narrow and highly branched for large surface area.
Name the blood vessels to and from the kidneys and liver. ~~ Kidneys: renal artery and renal vein. Liver: hepatic artery and hepatic vein; hepatic portal vein brings blood from the gut.
Why is the human circulatory system described as double? ~~ Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: pulmonary circuit to the lungs and systemic circuit to the body.

## 2.8
Name the waste products excreted by plants. ~~ Oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration.
Name the excretory organs in humans. ~~ Lungs (CO2), kidneys (urea, excess water and ions) and skin (sweat containing water, salts, a little urea).
What are the two main functions of the kidneys? ~~ Excretion of urea and osmoregulation (controlling the water content of the blood).
Where is urea made? ~~ In the liver, from the breakdown of excess amino acids (deamination).
Describe the structure of the urinary system. ~~ Two kidneys, each with a renal artery and vein; ureters carry urine to the bladder; urethra carries urine out of the body.
What is a nephron? ~~ The functional unit of the kidney; a tiny tubule where blood is filtered and urine is formed.
Name the parts of the nephron in order. ~~ Bowman's capsule (around glomerulus), proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct.
What is ultrafiltration? ~~ Small molecules (water, glucose, urea, salts) are forced out of the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule under high pressure.
Why is blood pressure high in the glomerulus? ~~ The afferent arteriole bringing blood in is wider than the efferent arteriole taking blood away.
Why are proteins and blood cells not found in the glomerular filtrate? ~~ They are too large to pass through the capillary walls and basement membrane.
Where does selective reabsorption of glucose occur? ~~ In the proximal convoluted tubule, by active transport back into the blood.
Why is there no glucose in the urine of a healthy person? ~~ All glucose is selectively reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule.
What is the role of the loop of Henle? ~~ Creates a high salt concentration in the medulla so water can be reabsorbed from the collecting duct by osmosis.
Where is water reabsorbed in the nephron? ~~ Mainly in the collecting duct (and proximal tubule), by osmosis into the surrounding tissue and blood.
Which hormone controls the water content of the blood? ~~ ADH (antidiuretic hormone), released by the pituitary gland.
What happens when the blood is too concentrated (dehydration)? ~~ Pituitary releases more ADH; collecting ducts become more permeable; more water reabsorbed; small volume of concentrated urine.
What happens when the blood is too dilute? ~~ Less ADH released; collecting ducts less permeable; less water reabsorbed; large volume of dilute urine.
Which part of the brain detects blood water content? ~~ The hypothalamus, which signals the pituitary gland to release ADH.
Name the substances in urine of a healthy person. ~~ Water, urea and salts (ions).
Why is ADH an example of negative feedback? ~~ A change in blood water content triggers a response that reverses the change, returning it to normal.
What is osmoregulation? ~~ Control of the water and salt balance of the body fluids.
Why must urea be excreted? ~~ It is toxic and would damage cells if it built up in the blood.
Compare blood in the renal artery and renal vein. ~~ Renal vein blood has less urea, less oxygen, more CO2 and regulated water and salt levels.
A filtrate flows at 125 cm³/min and urine at 1 cm³/min. What percentage of water is reabsorbed? ~~ Reabsorbed = 124 cm³; 124 ÷ 125 × 100 = 99.2%.
Exam trap: is urine stored in the kidney? ~~ No. Urine is stored in the bladder; ureters carry it from kidneys to bladder.
Exam trap: does ADH cause more urine to be produced? ~~ No. ADH increases water reabsorption, producing less, more concentrated urine.
What is the difference between the ureter and the urethra? ~~ Ureters carry urine from each kidney to the bladder; the single urethra carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
Why do people produce more urine on a cold day? ~~ Less sweating, so more water stays in the blood; less ADH released and more dilute urine is made.
Why might glucose appear in the urine of a person with untreated diabetes? ~~ Blood glucose is so high that not all glucose can be reabsorbed from the filtrate.
What is the role of the skin in excretion? ~~ Sweat glands release sweat containing water, salts and small amounts of urea.

## 2.9
What is homeostasis? ~~ The maintenance of a constant internal environment, e.g. body temperature, blood glucose and water content.
What is a stimulus? ~~ A change in the environment of an organism that can be detected.
Why do organisms respond to stimuli? ~~ To increase their chances of survival, e.g. finding food or avoiding danger.
What is a tropism? ~~ A growth response of a plant towards or away from a directional stimulus.
What is phototropism? ~~ Plant growth in response to light; shoots grow towards light (positive phototropism).
What is geotropism (gravitropism)? ~~ Plant growth in response to gravity; roots grow down (positive), shoots grow up (negative).
Explain the role of auxin in the phototropic response of shoots. ~~ Auxin moves to the shaded side of the shoot tip and stimulates cell elongation there, so the shoot bends towards the light.
Compare the nervous and hormonal systems. ~~ Nervous: electrical impulses, fast, short-lived, precise. Hormonal: chemicals in blood, slower, longer-lasting, widespread targets.
What makes up the central nervous system? ~~ The brain and the spinal cord.
Describe the pathway of a reflex arc. ~~ Stimulus, receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone (in spinal cord), motor neurone, effector, response.
What is a synapse? ~~ A gap between two neurones; a neurotransmitter diffuses across it to trigger an impulse in the next neurone.
Why are reflex actions important? ~~ They are rapid and automatic, protecting the body from harm without conscious thought.
What is an effector? ~~ A muscle or gland that brings about a response.
How does the eye focus on a near object? ~~ Ciliary muscles contract, suspensory ligaments slacken, lens becomes fatter (more convex), refracting light more.
How does the eye focus on a distant object? ~~ Ciliary muscles relax, suspensory ligaments tighten, lens is pulled thinner, refracting light less.
How does the iris respond to bright light? ~~ Circular muscles contract and radial muscles relax, so the pupil becomes smaller and less light enters.
How does the iris respond to dim light? ~~ Radial muscles contract and circular muscles relax, so the pupil widens and more light enters.
State the functions of the cornea, retina and optic nerve. ~~ Cornea refracts light; retina contains light receptors (rods and cones); optic nerve carries impulses to the brain.
How does the skin respond when body temperature rises? ~~ Sweating (evaporation removes heat), vasodilation of skin arterioles increases heat loss, hairs lie flat.
How does the skin respond when body temperature falls? ~~ Vasoconstriction reduces blood flow to skin, sweating stops, shivering releases heat, hairs stand up to trap air.
Exam trap: do blood vessels move up and down in the skin during vasodilation? ~~ No. Arterioles widen (vasodilation) or narrow (vasoconstriction); capillaries do not move.
Which gland secretes adrenaline and what does it do? ~~ Adrenal glands; increases heart rate, breathing rate and blood glucose to prepare for fight or flight.
Which gland secretes insulin and what does it do? ~~ Pancreas; lowers blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glucose to glycogen.
What is the role of glucagon? ~~ Secreted by the pancreas when blood glucose is low; causes the liver to convert glycogen to glucose.
Name the source and role of testosterone. ~~ Testes; controls male secondary sexual characteristics.
Name the source and roles of oestrogen and progesterone. ~~ Ovaries; oestrogen develops female secondary sexual characteristics and repairs the uterus lining; progesterone maintains the uterus lining.
Name the source and role of FSH and LH. ~~ Pituitary gland; FSH causes eggs to mature and oestrogen release; LH stimulates ovulation.
Name the source and role of ADH. ~~ Pituitary gland; increases water reabsorption in the kidney collecting ducts.
What is a hormone? ~~ A chemical messenger secreted by an endocrine gland into the blood, which carries it to target organs.
Describe a practical to investigate reaction time. ~~ Partner drops a ruler between your thumb and finger without warning; record the distance before catching; repeat and convert distance to time using a table.

## 3.1
State three differences between sexual and asexual reproduction. ~~ Sexual: two parents, gametes fuse at fertilisation, offspring genetically varied. Asexual: one parent, no gametes or fusion, offspring genetically identical clones.
What is fertilisation? ~~ The fusion of a male gamete nucleus with a female gamete nucleus to form a diploid zygote.
Give one advantage of sexual reproduction. ~~ It produces genetic variation, so some offspring may survive environmental change or disease, allowing adaptation by natural selection.
Give two advantages of asexual reproduction. ~~ Only one parent needed (no mate), and it is fast, producing many identical offspring suited to a stable environment.
What is the function of the anther? ~~ Produces pollen grains, which contain the male gametes.
What is the function of the stigma? ~~ Receives pollen grains during pollination.
What is the function of the ovary in a flower? ~~ Contains the ovules (female gametes); develops into the fruit after fertilisation.
What is the function of the sepals? ~~ Protect the flower while it is a bud.
Define pollination. ~~ The transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.
Compare the petals of insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated flowers. ~~ Insect-pollinated: large, brightly coloured, scented, with nectar to attract insects. Wind-pollinated: small, dull or absent, no scent or nectar.
Compare the pollen of insect- and wind-pollinated flowers. ~~ Insect-pollinated: larger, sticky or spiky, smaller amounts. Wind-pollinated: small, smooth, light, produced in huge amounts.
Describe the stigmas and anthers of a wind-pollinated flower. ~~ Large, feathery stigmas hang outside the flower to trap pollen; anthers hang outside on long filaments so wind blows pollen away.
Describe how fertilisation occurs in a flowering plant after pollination. ~~ Pollen tube grows from the pollen grain down through the style to the ovule; the male nucleus travels down it and fuses with the female nucleus in the ovule.
What do the ovule and ovary develop into after fertilisation? ~~ Ovule becomes the seed; ovary becomes the fruit.
What three conditions are needed for seed germination? ~~ Water, oxygen and a suitable (warm) temperature. Light is not needed.
How does a germinating seed get energy before it can photosynthesise? ~~ Food stores (starch, lipids, protein) in the cotyledons or endosperm are digested by enzymes and used in respiration for growth.
Describe natural asexual reproduction in strawberry plants. ~~ Runners (horizontal stems) grow along the ground, take root at nodes and form new plants genetically identical to the parent.
State the functions of the testes and sperm duct. ~~ Testes produce sperm and testosterone; sperm duct carries sperm from the testes to the urethra.
Where does fertilisation occur in humans, and where does the embryo implant? ~~ Fertilisation occurs in the oviduct; the embryo implants in the uterus lining.
How is a sperm cell adapted for its function? ~~ Tail for swimming, many mitochondria to release energy, acrosome containing enzymes to digest into the egg, haploid nucleus.
How is an egg cell adapted for its function? ~~ Large cytoplasm with food reserves for the early embryo, jelly coat that changes after fertilisation to stop more sperm entering, haploid nucleus.
Describe the role of the placenta. ~~ Exchanges substances between mother and fetus: oxygen, glucose, amino acids and antibodies to the fetus; carbon dioxide and urea to the mother. Blood does not mix.
What is the role of the amniotic fluid? ~~ Surrounds and cushions the fetus, protecting it from physical damage and supporting it.
Why are pregnant women advised to avoid alcohol and smoking? ~~ Alcohol, nicotine, other drugs and some viruses (e.g. rubella) can cross the placenta and harm fetal development.
Describe the roles of oestrogen and progesterone in the menstrual cycle. ~~ Oestrogen repairs and thickens the uterus lining. Progesterone maintains the lining; when progesterone falls, the lining breaks down (menstruation).
Describe the roles of FSH and LH in the menstrual cycle. ~~ FSH (pituitary) causes an egg follicle to mature and stimulates oestrogen release. LH (pituitary) triggers ovulation around day 14.
What happens to the corpus luteum if fertilisation does not occur? ~~ It breaks down, progesterone levels fall, the uterus lining is shed and a new cycle begins.
Name three male secondary sexual characteristics and the hormone responsible. ~~ Testosterone: growth of facial and body hair, deepening of voice, muscle development, sperm production.
Name three female secondary sexual characteristics and the hormone responsible. ~~ Oestrogen: breast development, widening of hips, growth of pubic and underarm hair, start of the menstrual cycle.
State the order of structures sperm pass through during ejaculation. ~~ Testis, sperm duct (glands add seminal fluid), urethra, out through the penis.

## 3.2
What is a gene? ~~ A section of a DNA molecule that codes for a specific protein.
What is an allele? ~~ An alternative form of the same gene, e.g. the allele for brown eyes or blue eyes.
Define genome. ~~ The entire DNA of an organism.
Describe the structure of DNA. ~~ Two strands coiled into a double helix, made of nucleotides; the strands are joined by complementary base pairs: A with T, C with G.
What are the complementary base pairs in DNA? ~~ Adenine pairs with thymine; cytosine pairs with guanine.
Describe transcription in protein synthesis. ~~ In the nucleus, the DNA of a gene is copied into a complementary mRNA molecule, which leaves the nucleus through a pore.
Describe translation in protein synthesis. ~~ At a ribosome, mRNA codons (triplets of bases) are read; tRNA brings matching amino acids, which are joined in order to make a polypeptide.
How many bases code for one amino acid? ~~ Three bases (a triplet or codon) code for one amino acid.
Define genotype and phenotype. ~~ Genotype: the alleles an organism has (e.g. Bb). Phenotype: the observable characteristics, resulting from genotype and environment.
Define homozygous and heterozygous. ~~ Homozygous: two identical alleles of a gene (BB or bb). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Bb).
Define dominant and recessive alleles. ~~ Dominant: expressed when one copy is present. Recessive: only expressed when two copies are present (no dominant allele).
What is codominance? ~~ Both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote, e.g. red and white snapdragons producing pink, or IA and IB giving blood group AB.
Give the expected phenotype ratio from crossing two heterozygotes (Bb × Bb). ~~ 3 dominant : 1 recessive (genotypes 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb).
Give the expected ratio from crossing a heterozygote with a homozygous recessive (Bb × bb). ~~ 1 dominant : 1 recessive phenotype (1 Bb : 1 bb).
How can a test cross show whether a dominant-phenotype organism is BB or Bb? ~~ Cross it with a homozygous recessive (bb). All dominant offspring suggests BB; any recessive offspring shows it is Bb.
How do you identify a recessive condition in a pedigree diagram? ~~ Two unaffected parents have an affected child, so both parents must be heterozygous carriers.
How is sex determined in humans? ~~ Females are XX, males XY. Eggs all carry X; sperm carry X or Y, so there is a 50% chance of each sex.
How many chromosomes are in human body cells and gametes? ~~ Body cells are diploid with 46 (23 pairs); gametes are haploid with 23.
What is mitosis and what is it used for? ~~ Cell division producing two genetically identical diploid cells; used for growth, repair, cloning and asexual reproduction.
What is meiosis and where does it occur? ~~ Cell division producing four genetically different haploid gametes; occurs in testes and ovaries (anthers and ovules in plants).
Why is meiosis important for sexual reproduction? ~~ It halves the chromosome number so the diploid number is restored at fertilisation, and it creates genetic variation.
State the two causes of variation. ~~ Genetic variation (different alleles inherited) and environmental variation (e.g. diet, climate); most features result from both.
What is a mutation? ~~ A rare, random change in the base sequence of DNA. Most have no effect; a few are harmful or beneficial.
What increases the rate of mutation? ~~ Ionising radiation (gamma, X-rays, UV) and chemical mutagens such as those in tobacco smoke.
How can a mutation affect a protein? ~~ It changes the base sequence, which may change the amino acid sequence, altering the protein's shape and function, e.g. an enzyme's active site.
Describe Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection. ~~ Variation exists; more offspring are produced than survive; those with advantageous alleles survive and reproduce, passing alleles on; over generations the allele becomes more common.
Explain how bacteria become resistant to antibiotics. ~~ A random mutation gives resistance; antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria; resistant ones survive, reproduce and pass on the allele, so the resistant population grows.
In a cross Rr × Rr, what is the probability of an rr offspring? ~~ 1 in 4, i.e. 25% or 0.25.
What are continuous and discontinuous variation? Give examples. ~~ Continuous: any value in a range, e.g. height, mass (many genes plus environment). Discontinuous: distinct categories, e.g. blood group, tongue rolling.
What is a chromosome? ~~ A long thread of DNA, carrying many genes, found in the nucleus.

## 4.1
Define population. ~~ All the organisms of one species living in the same area at the same time.
Define community. ~~ All the populations of different species living in the same habitat at the same time.
Define habitat. ~~ The place where an organism lives.
Define ecosystem. ~~ A community of organisms and the non-living (abiotic) environment they interact with.
Give four abiotic factors that affect populations. ~~ Temperature, light intensity, water availability, soil pH and minerals, oxygen or carbon dioxide levels, wind.
Give three biotic factors that affect populations. ~~ Availability of food, predators, competition (for food, mates, space), disease and pathogens.
How is a quadrat used to estimate population size? ~~ Place quadrats randomly, count individuals in each, find the mean per quadrat, then multiply by (total area ÷ quadrat area).
Why must quadrats be placed randomly? ~~ To avoid bias, so the sample is representative of the whole area.
How are random positions for quadrats generated? ~~ Lay two tape measures at right angles as axes and use a random number generator to choose coordinates.
How can you make quadrat results more reliable? ~~ Use a larger number of quadrats (e.g. 10+) and calculate a mean.
Calculate the population if the mean is 6 daisies per 0.25 m² quadrat in a 100 m² field. ~~ Number of quadrats fitting = 100 ÷ 0.25 = 400. Population = 6 × 400 = 2400 daisies.
What is a transect and when is it used? ~~ A line (tape) across a habitat with quadrats at regular intervals; used to study how distribution changes with a changing factor, e.g. from shade to light.
Define biodiversity. ~~ The variety of different species in an area, including how many species and the evenness of their numbers.
Why is high biodiversity valuable? ~~ More stable ecosystems, less dependence on one species, source of foods and medicines, and genetic resources.
What is percentage cover and when is it used? ~~ The percentage of the quadrat area covered by a species; used when individuals are hard to count, such as grass or moss.
Why does light intensity affect plant distribution in a woodland? ~~ Plants need light for photosynthesis; shade-tolerant species grow under the canopy while others grow only in open, brighter areas.
What abiotic factor might you measure along a transect, and with what? ~~ Light intensity with a light meter, soil moisture with a moisture meter, soil pH with a pH probe or indicator, temperature with a thermometer.
Why should the same quadrat size be used when comparing two areas? ~~ So the comparison is fair; quadrat size is a control variable.
Why might a population estimate from quadrats be inaccurate? ~~ Too few samples, non-random placement, organisms miscounted or misidentified, or uneven distribution across the area.
How does competition affect population size? ~~ Organisms compete for limited resources (food, light, space, water); losers have fewer offspring, so population growth is limited.
How does predation affect the population of prey? ~~ More predators reduce prey numbers; with less food, predators then decline, allowing prey to recover, giving cycles.
How can the number of species in an area be compared between two habitats? ~~ Count species richness in the same number of random quadrats of the same size in each habitat, then compare.
What is a species? ~~ A group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
Why are organisms not evenly distributed in a habitat? ~~ Abiotic and biotic factors vary across the habitat, so organisms are found where conditions suit their adaptations.
Explain how water availability can limit plant populations. ~~ Water is needed for photosynthesis, support (turgor) and transport; in dry areas fewer plants survive except drought-adapted species.
How does temperature affect organisms in an ecosystem? ~~ It affects enzyme activity, so growth, reproduction and survival rates; extremes limit which species can live there.
What is an independent variable when comparing populations in a shaded and open area? ~~ The area (level of shade or light intensity); the dependent variable is the number of the chosen species.
How could disease be a biotic factor reducing a population? ~~ Pathogens spread quickly in dense populations, killing or weakening individuals and reducing reproduction.
Define abiotic and biotic. ~~ Abiotic: non-living physical or chemical factors. Biotic: living factors, i.e. other organisms.
Why do ecologists sample rather than count every organism? ~~ Counting every organism in large areas is impractical and time-consuming; a representative sample gives a good estimate.

## 4.2
What is a producer? ~~ An organism, usually a green plant or alga, that makes its own food by photosynthesis.
Define primary consumer. ~~ An herbivore that feeds on producers.
Define secondary and tertiary consumers. ~~ Secondary consumers eat primary consumers; tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers. Both are carnivores.
What is a decomposer? ~~ An organism (bacteria, fungi) that breaks down dead organisms and waste, releasing nutrients back into the environment.
What is a trophic level? ~~ The position an organism occupies in a food chain, e.g. producer is trophic level 1.
What do the arrows in a food chain show? ~~ The direction of energy transfer, from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it.
What is a food web? ~~ A network of interlinked food chains showing all feeding relationships in an ecosystem.
What is a pyramid of numbers and why can it be an odd shape? ~~ Bar diagram of numbers at each trophic level; can be inverted, e.g. one oak tree supporting thousands of caterpillars.
What is a pyramid of biomass? ~~ Shows the dry mass of living material at each trophic level; almost always pyramid shaped.
What is a pyramid of energy? ~~ Shows energy transferred through each trophic level per unit area per year; always pyramid shaped.
Why is only about 10% of energy transferred between trophic levels? ~~ Energy is lost through respiration (heat), movement, excretion, egestion (faeces) and parts not eaten or digested.
Calculate efficiency if grass contains 20 000 kJ and rabbits gain 2000 kJ. ~~ Efficiency = (energy transferred ÷ energy available) × 100 = 2000 ÷ 20 000 × 100 = 10%.
Why are food chains usually no longer than four or five trophic levels? ~~ So much energy is lost at each level that too little remains to support further levels.
Where does the energy in most food chains originally come from? ~~ The Sun; light energy is captured by producers in photosynthesis.
Why is most energy from the Sun not captured by plants? ~~ Light is reflected, passes through leaves, misses leaves, or is the wrong wavelength; only a small percentage is used in photosynthesis.
Explain why eating plants is more energy-efficient for humans than eating meat. ~~ Fewer trophic levels so less energy lost; more energy from the producer reaches humans, so more people can be fed per area.
Predict the effect on a food web if a predator is removed. ~~ Its prey increases, which may reduce the prey's food source; other predators may also increase. Effects ripple through the web.
Why are food webs more stable than single food chains? ~~ Consumers have several food sources, so loss of one species has a smaller effect.
What is biomass? ~~ The mass of living material, usually measured as dry mass.
Why is dry mass used in biomass pyramids? ~~ Water content varies greatly, so dry mass gives a more accurate, comparable measure of organic material.
What are herbivores, carnivores and omnivores? ~~ Herbivores eat plants; carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat both plants and animals.
How is energy lost as heat in a food chain? ~~ Respiration releases energy, much of which is lost to the surroundings as heat, especially in mammals and birds maintaining body temperature.
Why do warm-blooded animals transfer less energy to the next trophic level? ~~ They use much energy in respiration to maintain a constant body temperature, so less is stored in biomass.
In a food chain grass → rabbit → fox, identify the trophic levels. ~~ Grass: producer (level 1); rabbit: primary consumer (level 2); fox: secondary consumer (level 3).
What is a parasite? ~~ An organism living on or in a host, feeding from it and causing harm, e.g. tapeworm or flea.
Why might a pyramid of biomass be inverted in the sea? ~~ Phytoplankton reproduce very rapidly and are eaten quickly, so their biomass at any one time can be less than zooplankton.
How does energy enter and leave a food chain? ~~ Enters as light captured by producers; leaves as heat from respiration and in waste broken down by decomposers.
What is the role of decomposers in energy flow? ~~ They respire using energy in dead material and waste, releasing it as heat, and recycle nutrients.
Calculate the energy reaching level 3 if producers have 50 000 kJ and transfer is 10% each time. ~~ Level 2: 5000 kJ; level 3: 500 kJ.
Give three ways energy is lost from a cow's food. ~~ Faeces (undigested cellulose), urine, respiration as heat, movement, methane released.

## 4.3
Name the processes that remove carbon dioxide from the air. ~~ Photosynthesis by plants and algae (and dissolving in oceans).
Name the processes that add carbon dioxide to the air. ~~ Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, and combustion of fossil fuels and wood.
How does carbon pass from plants to animals? ~~ Through feeding; carbon in plant carbohydrates, proteins and fats is eaten and becomes part of animal tissues.
What is the role of decomposers in the carbon cycle? ~~ They break down dead organisms and waste, respiring and releasing carbon dioxide back to the air.
How are fossil fuels formed? ~~ Dead organisms buried and compressed over millions of years without decay, forming coal, oil and gas that store carbon.
Why has atmospheric carbon dioxide risen? ~~ Burning fossil fuels and deforestation (burning trees and fewer plants absorbing carbon dioxide by photosynthesis).
Why do plants need nitrogen? ~~ To make amino acids and proteins (and DNA) for growth.
Why can't plants use nitrogen gas directly? ~~ Nitrogen gas is very unreactive; plants absorb nitrogen only as nitrates (or ammonium ions) through roots.
What do nitrogen-fixing bacteria do? ~~ Convert nitrogen gas into ammonium compounds; found in soil and in root nodules of legumes such as peas, beans and clover.
What is nitrification? ~~ Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions into nitrites and then nitrates, in aerobic conditions.
What is denitrification? ~~ Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrates in soil into nitrogen gas, mainly in waterlogged, anaerobic soil.
What is the role of decomposers in the nitrogen cycle? ~~ They break down proteins and urea in dead organisms and waste into ammonium ions (ammonification).
How does lightning contribute to the nitrogen cycle? ~~ The high energy causes nitrogen and oxygen to react, forming nitrogen oxides that dissolve in rain to form nitrates.
Why do farmers plough and drain fields in terms of the nitrogen cycle? ~~ It aerates soil, favouring nitrifying bacteria and reducing anaerobic conditions where denitrifying bacteria remove nitrates.
Why is growing legumes beneficial in crop rotation? ~~ Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules increase soil nitrates, reducing the need for fertiliser.
What relationship exists between legumes and root-nodule bacteria? ~~ Mutualism: bacteria get sugars from the plant, and the plant gets ammonium compounds for making proteins.
Put the nitrogen cycle stages in order from dead organism to plant protein. ~~ Dead organism → decomposers produce ammonium → nitrifying bacteria make nitrites then nitrates → plant roots absorb nitrates → amino acids and proteins.
How does carbon become locked in carbonate rocks? ~~ Shells and skeletons of marine organisms containing calcium carbonate form sedimentary rocks such as limestone.
What conditions do decomposers work best in? ~~ Warm, moist conditions with plenty of oxygen.
Why is decomposition slow in waterlogged soil? ~~ There is little oxygen, so aerobic decomposers cannot respire efficiently.
In what forms is carbon stored in living organisms? ~~ Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and other organic compounds.
How is the carbon cycle balanced in a stable ecosystem? ~~ Carbon dioxide removed by photosynthesis roughly equals that released by respiration, so atmospheric levels stay steady.
Why is the nitrogen cycle important for ecosystems? ~~ It recycles nitrogen so plants can keep making proteins; without it, soil nitrates would run out.
Which bacteria in the nitrogen cycle reduce soil fertility? ~~ Denitrifying bacteria, because they turn nitrates into nitrogen gas, which plants cannot use.
How does the carbon cycle link to the energy flow in a food chain? ~~ Carbon compounds carry energy; photosynthesis fixes carbon and stores energy, respiration releases both carbon dioxide and energy.
What is the role of microorganisms in cycling nutrients? ~~ They decompose dead material and waste, releasing carbon dioxide and mineral ions back into the air and soil for reuse.
How does burning wood add carbon dioxide to the atmosphere? ~~ Combustion oxidises carbon compounds in wood, releasing carbon dioxide stored by photosynthesis.
How do animals obtain nitrogen? ~~ By eating plants or other animals; proteins are digested into amino acids and used to make their own proteins.
What happens to urea in the nitrogen cycle? ~~ It is broken down by decomposers into ammonium ions, which nitrifying bacteria convert into nitrates.
What is nitrogen fixation by the Haber process? ~~ Industrial conversion of nitrogen gas into ammonia, used to make artificial fertilisers that add nitrates to soil.

## 4.4
Name the greenhouse gases. ~~ Carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide and CFCs.
Explain the greenhouse effect. ~~ Short-wave radiation from the Sun warms Earth; Earth emits longer-wave infrared, which greenhouse gases absorb and re-radiate, keeping Earth warm.
What is the enhanced greenhouse effect? ~~ Increased greenhouse gas levels from human activity trap more heat, causing global warming.
Give sources of methane from human activity. ~~ Cattle (digestion), rice paddy fields, landfill sites and decomposing waste.
Give three consequences of global warming. ~~ Melting ice and sea-level rise, flooding, changes in climate and rainfall, species extinction or migration, reduced crop yields, more extreme weather.
Name the main source of sulfur dioxide pollution and its effect. ~~ Burning fossil fuels containing sulfur; forms acid rain, which damages trees, lowers pH of lakes (killing fish) and erodes buildings.
Why is carbon monoxide dangerous? ~~ It binds irreversibly to haemoglobin, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity; it comes from incomplete combustion such as car exhausts.
Describe the stages of eutrophication. ~~ Fertiliser or sewage adds nitrates and phosphates → algal bloom → light blocked, plants below die → bacteria decompose them, using oxygen → fish and other animals die.
Why does decomposition cause oxygen levels in water to fall during eutrophication? ~~ Decomposing bacteria increase in number and respire aerobically, using up dissolved oxygen.
How does untreated sewage pollute water? ~~ It adds nutrients causing eutrophication, increases bacterial respiration (oxygen depletion) and may contain pathogens.
Give four effects of deforestation. ~~ Reduced biodiversity, loss of habitats, soil erosion, more flooding, less carbon dioxide absorbed and more released when burned, disturbed water and carbon cycles.
Why does deforestation lead to soil erosion? ~~ Tree roots no longer hold soil, and there is no canopy to break rainfall, so soil is washed away and nutrients lost.
How does deforestation affect the water cycle? ~~ Less transpiration means less water vapour and rainfall; more run-off and flooding.
How does deforestation affect carbon dioxide levels? ~~ Fewer trees photosynthesise, and burning or decomposing timber releases carbon dioxide, so levels rise.
What is leaching and how does it link to eutrophication? ~~ Soluble fertilisers dissolve in rainwater and drain from soil into rivers and lakes, adding nutrients.
Why does overfishing threaten fish stocks? ~~ Fish are removed faster than they can breed, so populations fall and may collapse, disrupting food webs.
Give ways to reduce overfishing. ~~ Fishing quotas, larger mesh nets so young fish escape, closed seasons, protected areas, and fish farming.
How can acid rain be reduced? ~~ Remove sulfur from fuels, use scrubbers in power station chimneys, catalytic converters in cars, and use less fossil fuel.
What is a pollutant? ~~ A substance released into the environment, often by humans, that harms living organisms.
Give three ways to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. ~~ Use renewable energy, improve energy efficiency, reduce deforestation and replant trees, reduce meat consumption, use public transport.
Why does an algal bloom kill plants below the surface? ~~ Algae block light, so submerged plants cannot photosynthesise and die.
How can eutrophication be reduced? ~~ Apply fertiliser carefully (not before rain), leave buffer strips by rivers, treat sewage to remove nitrates and phosphates.
Which indicator species show clean or polluted water? ~~ Mayfly and stonefly larvae indicate clean, well-oxygenated water; bloodworms and sludgeworms indicate polluted, low-oxygen water.
How do lichens indicate air quality? ~~ Many lichen species are sensitive to sulfur dioxide; more lichen species indicates cleaner air.
Why is water vapour a greenhouse gas, and why is it less of a concern? ~~ It absorbs infrared radiation, but its levels are controlled mainly by natural evaporation rather than directly by human activity.
What is the effect of nitrous oxide and where does it come from? ~~ A potent greenhouse gas released from fertilised soils, vehicle exhausts and industry.
How do CFCs affect the environment? ~~ They are greenhouse gases and damage the ozone layer, increasing harmful UV reaching Earth; now largely banned.
Why does acid rain kill fish? ~~ Low pH in lakes damages gills and releases toxic aluminium ions from the soil into the water.
Why is biodiversity reduced in monoculture plantations after deforestation? ~~ A single crop species provides few habitats and food sources, so fewer species can live there.
How does burning fossil fuels contribute to climate change? ~~ Releases carbon dioxide that was locked away for millions of years, raising atmospheric levels and enhancing the greenhouse effect.

## 5.1
Why do glasshouses increase crop yield? ~~ They raise temperature, can add carbon dioxide and artificial light, and protect from pests and weather, increasing photosynthesis rate.
Why is carbon dioxide added to glasshouses? ~~ It is often a limiting factor for photosynthesis; more carbon dioxide increases the rate and yield. Paraffin burners also warm the air.
Why are polythene tunnels used? ~~ A cheaper version of glasshouses; trap heat and protect crops, extending the growing season.
Why are fertilisers used? ~~ They replace mineral ions (nitrate, phosphate, potassium) removed by crops, increasing growth and yield.
Why do plants need nitrates? ~~ To make amino acids and proteins for growth; deficiency causes stunted growth and yellow leaves.
Compare organic and artificial fertilisers. ~~ Organic (manure, compost): release minerals slowly, improve soil structure, cheap. Artificial: exact mineral content, fast-acting, easily leached.
What are pesticides and give examples? ~~ Chemicals that kill pests: insecticides (insects), herbicides (weeds), fungicides (fungi).
Give disadvantages of pesticides. ~~ Kill non-target species, can accumulate along food chains (bioaccumulation), pests may become resistant, must be reapplied.
What is biological control? ~~ Using a natural predator, parasite or pathogen to reduce a pest population, e.g. ladybirds eating aphids.
Give advantages and disadvantages of biological control. ~~ Advantages: specific, no chemicals, no resistance, self-sustaining. Disadvantages: slower, never removes all pests, the control organism may become a pest itself.
Write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in yeast. ~~ Glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.
How is yeast used in bread making? ~~ Yeast respires sugar, producing carbon dioxide that makes dough rise; ethanol evaporates during baking, which also kills the yeast.
Why is dough left in a warm place? ~~ Warmth increases enzyme activity in yeast, so respiration and carbon dioxide production are faster.
Which bacterium is used in yoghurt making? ~~ Lactobacillus.
Describe the stages of yoghurt making. ~~ Sterilise equipment, pasteurise milk, cool to about 40 °C, add Lactobacillus, incubate; bacteria convert lactose to lactic acid, which thickens milk and lowers pH.
Why does yoghurt have a sour taste and keep for a while? ~~ Lactic acid lowers pH, giving the sour taste and inhibiting growth of spoilage microorganisms.
Why are equipment and milk sterilised or pasteurised? ~~ To kill unwanted microorganisms that would compete with Lactobacillus or spoil the product.
What is an industrial fermenter used for? ~~ Growing microorganisms on a large scale in controlled conditions to make products such as penicillin, enzymes or mycoprotein.
Why is a fermenter sterilised with steam before use? ~~ To kill contaminating microorganisms that would compete with the culture or make harmful products.
Why does a fermenter have a water jacket? ~~ Respiration releases heat; cool water circulates to keep the temperature at the optimum for enzymes.
Why does a fermenter have paddles (stirrers)? ~~ To mix nutrients and microorganisms evenly, and spread heat and oxygen.
What conditions are monitored in an industrial fermenter? ~~ Temperature, pH, oxygen level, nutrient supply and asepsis (sterile conditions).
Why is air bubbled into fermenters? ~~ To supply oxygen for aerobic respiration of the microorganisms.
Why is pH monitored in a fermenter? ~~ Enzymes work best at an optimum pH; probes detect change and acid or alkali is added to correct it.
How is fish farming used to produce food? ~~ Fish are raised in enclosures, in controlled conditions, to give high-protein food efficiently.
How do fish farmers maintain water quality? ~~ Filter water, remove waste, and change or circulate water to keep oxygen high and prevent disease.
How do fish farmers control disease and parasites? ~~ Keep stocking densities low, use biological control (e.g. wrasse eating sea lice), and limited use of antibiotics or pesticides.
Why are different fish species and sizes kept separately in fish farms? ~~ To prevent predation (interspecific) and larger fish eating smaller ones (intraspecific).
How is feeding controlled in fish farms? ~~ High-protein food is given in controlled amounts several times a day; overfeeding is avoided because uneaten food pollutes water.
Why is selective breeding used in fish farming? ~~ To produce fast-growing, disease-resistant fish with good meat quality.

## 5.2
What is selective breeding? ~~ Artificial selection: humans choose individuals with desired characteristics to breed, so those features become more common over generations.
What is another name for selective breeding? ~~ Artificial selection.
Describe the steps of selective breeding. ~~ Choose parents with the desired trait; breed them; select offspring showing the trait most strongly; breed these; repeat over many generations.
Why must selective breeding be repeated over many generations? ~~ Each generation increases the trait only slightly; repeated selection makes the desired alleles common and the trait consistent.
Give an example of selective breeding in dairy cattle. ~~ Cows with the highest milk yield are bred with bulls from high-yielding mothers, increasing milk yield over generations.
Give an example of selective breeding in beef cattle. ~~ Cattle with high muscle mass and fast growth are bred to produce more meat.
Give characteristics wheat is selectively bred for. ~~ High grain yield, short, stiff stems that do not lodge (fall over), disease resistance, and grains ripening at the same time.
Why is wheat bred for short stems? ~~ Short stems are less likely to be blown over in wind and rain, and more energy goes into grain rather than stem.
Give characteristics crops are bred for, other than yield. ~~ Disease and pest resistance, drought or frost tolerance, better taste, larger fruit, longer shelf life.
Give examples of characteristics dogs are bred for. ~~ Appearance, gentle temperament, herding ability, hunting ability or sense of smell, guarding.
What is inbreeding? ~~ Breeding closely related individuals, which often happens in selective breeding.
Why does inbreeding cause health problems? ~~ It increases the chance of offspring inheriting two copies of harmful recessive alleles, so genetic disorders appear more often.
Give examples of health problems caused by selective breeding in dogs. ~~ Hip dysplasia in Labradors and German shepherds; breathing problems in bulldogs and pugs.
Why does selective breeding reduce genetic diversity? ~~ Only a few individuals with chosen alleles are bred, so other alleles are lost from the population.
Why is reduced genetic diversity a problem? ~~ If a new disease or environmental change occurs, few individuals may carry resistant alleles, so the whole population may be wiped out.
State three advantages of selective breeding. ~~ Increases yield and quality, produces disease-resistant varieties, uses natural reproduction without laboratory gene manipulation.
State three disadvantages of selective breeding. ~~ Slow, reduced genetic variation, inbreeding causes genetic disorders, unwanted traits can be inherited alongside desired ones.
How does selective breeding differ from natural selection? ~~ In selective breeding humans choose which individuals breed for useful traits; in natural selection the environment determines which individuals survive and reproduce.
Compare selective breeding and genetic modification. ~~ Selective breeding uses natural mating within a species over many generations; GM inserts a specific gene, often from another species, in one generation.
Why is GM faster than selective breeding? ~~ The desired gene is inserted directly, so the trait appears in one generation instead of many.
Can selective breeding produce a characteristic not present in the species? ~~ No; it only works with existing variation (alleles already present). GM can introduce genes from other species.
Why might animal welfare be an ethical concern with selective breeding? ~~ Animals may suffer, e.g. very high-yield cows with leg or udder problems, or fast-growing chickens unable to support their weight.
How can breeders reduce the problems of inbreeding? ~~ Keep records and avoid mating close relatives, or occasionally introduce unrelated individuals to increase genetic variation.
Why are wild varieties of crops conserved in seed banks? ~~ They keep alleles lost from selectively bred crops, which may be needed for future breeding, e.g. disease resistance.
How might selective breeding produce disease-resistant crops? ~~ Plants surviving infection are selected and bred, and the process is repeated so resistance becomes common.
Why do selectively bred cereal crops all ripen at the same time? ~~ Breeders selected for uniform ripening so the whole crop can be harvested in one go by machine.
Give an example of selective breeding in chickens. ~~ Chickens bred for high egg production (laying hens) or rapid growth and large breast muscle (broilers).
Why might genes linked to desired traits cause problems? ~~ Undesirable alleles can be inherited along with selected ones, so unwanted traits may also become common.
Describe how a farmer would breed sheep with finer wool. ~~ Select rams and ewes with the finest wool, breed them, choose the finest-wooled lambs to breed, and repeat for several generations.
Why is a population of selectively bred crops vulnerable to new pests? ~~ Plants are genetically very similar, so if one is susceptible, most are, and a pest can spread through the whole crop.

## 5.3
What is genetic modification? ~~ Changing an organism's genome, usually by inserting a gene from another organism, to give a desired characteristic.
What is a transgenic organism? ~~ An organism containing genetic material from a different species.
What do restriction enzymes do? ~~ Cut DNA at specific base sequences, often leaving sticky ends.
What does DNA ligase do? ~~ Joins pieces of DNA together, e.g. sealing a gene into a plasmid.
What is a vector in genetic engineering? ~~ A carrier that transfers the gene into the host cell, e.g. a plasmid or a virus.
What is a plasmid? ~~ A small circular ring of DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main chromosome, used as a vector.
What are sticky ends and why are they useful? ~~ Short unpaired base sequences left after cutting; complementary sticky ends on the gene and plasmid pair up so they can be joined.
Why is the same restriction enzyme used on the gene and the plasmid? ~~ It produces complementary sticky ends, so the gene fits into the opened plasmid.
What is recombinant DNA? ~~ DNA formed by combining DNA from two different sources, e.g. a human gene in a bacterial plasmid.
Describe the steps in producing human insulin using bacteria. ~~ Cut insulin gene from human DNA with restriction enzyme; cut plasmid with same enzyme; join with ligase; insert plasmid into bacteria; grow bacteria in fermenter; extract and purify insulin.
Why are bacteria used to make human insulin? ~~ They reproduce rapidly, are easy to grow in fermenters, and the genetic code is universal, so they make identical human insulin.
Give advantages of insulin from GM bacteria compared with animal insulin. ~~ Identical to human insulin (fewer allergic reactions), produced in large amounts cheaply, no ethical or religious issues about animal sources.
Why is the genetic code described as universal? ~~ The same base triplets code for the same amino acids in all organisms, so a gene from one species can be expressed in another.
What is Bt maize? ~~ GM maize containing a gene from Bacillus thuringiensis producing a toxin that kills insect pests that eat it.
What are the advantages of insect-resistant GM crops? ~~ Higher yields, less insecticide sprayed, so less harm to non-target insects and lower costs.
What are herbicide-resistant GM crops and why are they useful? ~~ Crops given a gene for herbicide resistance, so fields can be sprayed to kill weeds without harming the crop, increasing yield.
What is Golden Rice? ~~ GM rice containing genes to make beta-carotene (converted to vitamin A), to reduce vitamin A deficiency and blindness.
Give concerns about GM crops. ~~ Genes may spread to wild plants (superweeds), effects on non-target insects, reduced biodiversity, long-term health effects unknown, seed costs for farmers.
Give examples of GM crop traits that increase yield. ~~ Pest resistance, herbicide resistance, drought or salt tolerance, disease resistance.
How do you know which bacteria have taken up the recombinant plasmid? ~~ Plasmids include a marker gene (e.g. antibiotic resistance); bacteria grown on antibiotic plates survive only if they took up the plasmid.
Why might the insulin gene be inserted into a plasmid rather than directly into bacteria? ~~ Plasmids replicate in the bacterium and are copied when it divides, so the gene is passed to all offspring.
What happens after recombinant bacteria are placed in a fermenter? ~~ They multiply rapidly and express the gene, producing large quantities of the protein, which is then extracted and purified.
How can a virus be used as a vector? ~~ The desired gene is inserted into the virus, which then infects target cells and delivers the gene into their DNA.
What is gene therapy? ~~ Inserting normal alleles into a patient's cells to treat a genetic disorder such as cystic fibrosis.
Give an ethical concern about GM animals. ~~ Animal welfare, unknown long-term effects, and the view that crossing species barriers is unnatural.
How might GM crops help developing countries? ~~ Higher yields, crops resistant to drought or pests, and improved nutrition, e.g. Golden Rice, reduce food shortages.
Why might GM crops reduce biodiversity? ~~ Herbicide-resistant crops allow heavy herbicide use, killing wild plants and the insects that depend on them.
Give an example of a GM microorganism product other than insulin. ~~ Human growth hormone, enzymes such as chymosin for cheese making, or vaccines.
Why do restriction enzymes cut at specific sequences? ~~ Each enzyme has an active site complementary to one specific base sequence (recognition site).
Compare GM and selective breeding in terms of the genes involved. ~~ GM can transfer one specific gene between species; selective breeding mixes whole genomes within a species, so many genes change together.

## 5.4
What is a clone? ~~ An organism or cell genetically identical to its single parent, produced asexually.
Give natural examples of cloning. ~~ Bacterial binary fission, strawberry runners, potato tubers, bulbs, and identical twins.
What is micropropagation? ~~ Growing many genetically identical plants from small pieces of a parent plant (explants) on sterile nutrient agar.
Describe the steps of micropropagation. ~~ Take explants from the parent; sterilise; place on agar with nutrients and hormones; callus forms then plantlets; transfer plantlets to compost.
What is an explant? ~~ A small piece of tissue, often from a shoot tip, taken from the parent plant for micropropagation.
Why are explants sterilised in micropropagation? ~~ To kill bacteria and fungi that would grow quickly on the nutrient agar and kill the plant tissue.
Why are plant hormones added to the agar in micropropagation? ~~ Auxins and cytokinins stimulate cell division and growth of roots and shoots from the callus.
What is a callus? ~~ A mass of undifferentiated plant cells that forms from the explant and develops into plantlets.
Why are shoot tips used as explants? ~~ They contain actively dividing (meristem) cells and are usually free of viruses.
Give advantages of micropropagation. ~~ Many identical plants produced quickly and cheaply in a small space, all year round; virus-free stock; desirable traits kept; rare species preserved.
Give disadvantages of micropropagation. ~~ No genetic variation, so a single disease or environmental change can affect all plants; needs sterile labs and skilled workers; risk of contamination.
Give examples of plants commercially produced by micropropagation. ~~ Bananas, orchids, strawberries, potatoes and rare or endangered plants.
Why is cloning easier in plants than animals? ~~ Many plant cells remain totipotent (able to form any cell type), whereas most animal cells differentiate early and lose this ability.
What is the name of the technique used to clone Dolly the sheep? ~~ Somatic cell nuclear transfer (nuclear transfer).
Describe how Dolly the sheep was cloned. ~~ Udder cell nucleus from a Finn-Dorset ewe was inserted into an enucleated egg from a Scottish Blackface ewe, fused by electric shock, grown into an embryo and implanted into a surrogate.
What is enucleation? ~~ Removing the nucleus from an egg cell before a donor nucleus is inserted.
Why is an electric shock used in nuclear transfer? ~~ To fuse the donor nucleus with the egg cell and stimulate it to start dividing like a zygote.
Which sheep was Dolly genetically identical to? ~~ The Finn-Dorset ewe that donated the udder-cell nucleus, not the egg donor or surrogate.
Why did Dolly have a white face when the surrogate had a black face? ~~ Her nuclear DNA came from the white-faced Finn-Dorset donor, showing the nucleus controls the characteristics.
What is the role of the surrogate mother in animal cloning? ~~ She carries the embryo through pregnancy and gives birth; she contributes no nuclear genes.
What is embryo splitting (embryo transplantation)? ~~ Cells are separated from an early embryo of high-value parents, each grown and implanted into surrogates, producing identical offspring.
Give uses of cloned transgenic animals. ~~ Producing human proteins in milk (e.g. drugs such as antithrombin from goats), so many identical animals produce the medicine.
Why clone transgenic animals rather than breed them? ~~ Sexual reproduction might not pass the inserted gene to all offspring; cloning guarantees every offspring carries it.
Give advantages of animal cloning. ~~ Copies of high-value animals (high milk yield) produced; transgenic animals reproduced reliably; may help save endangered species.
Give disadvantages of animal cloning. ~~ Low success rate, expensive, clones may have health problems or age early, reduced genetic variation, ethical concerns.
Give ethical objections to cloning mammals. ~~ Animal suffering from failed attempts and health problems; fear it could lead to human cloning; seen as interfering with nature.
Compare plant micropropagation and animal nuclear transfer. ~~ Micropropagation is cheap, reliable and produces many plants; nuclear transfer is expensive, has a low success rate and produces few animals.
How is cloning different from selective breeding? ~~ Cloning copies one organism exactly with no variation; selective breeding uses sexual reproduction, so offspring vary and traits improve gradually.
Why might a farmer clone a prize cow rather than breed it? ~~ Sexual reproduction mixes alleles so offspring may not inherit all desirable traits; clones have exactly the same genes.
What is the role of the nucleus in cloning? ~~ It contains the DNA, so the clone inherits its characteristics from the nucleus donor.

## RP
In the food test practical, how do you test for starch? ~~ Add iodine solution; it turns from orange-brown to blue-black if starch is present.
How do you test a food for glucose (reducing sugar)? ~~ Add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath at about 80 °C for 5 minutes; blue turns green, yellow, orange or brick-red if present.
How do you test a food for protein? ~~ Add biuret reagent (potassium hydroxide then copper sulfate); blue turns purple if protein is present.
How do you test a food for lipids? ~~ Shake with ethanol, then add water; a cloudy white emulsion forms if lipid is present. Keep ethanol away from naked flames.
Describe how to investigate the effect of temperature on amylase activity. ~~ Equilibrate amylase and starch in water baths at different temperatures, mix, take drops every 30 s onto iodine on a spotting tile; time until iodine stays orange-brown.
How is rate calculated in the amylase practical? ~~ Rate = 1 ÷ time taken for starch to be fully digested (iodine no longer turns blue-black).
What results are expected for the amylase-temperature practical? ~~ Rate rises to an optimum around 37 °C, then falls sharply as the enzyme denatures and the active site changes shape.
Describe the pH and enzyme activity practical and its variables. ~~ IV: pH (buffers 3–11); DV: time for starch to disappear (iodine test); control temperature, volumes and concentrations of starch and amylase.
How do you investigate osmosis using potato cylinders? ~~ Cut equal cylinders with a cork borer, blot and weigh, place in different sucrose concentrations for the same time and temperature, blot and reweigh.
How is percentage change in mass calculated in the osmosis practical? ~~ % change = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100.
How do you find the concentration of potato cytoplasm from the osmosis results? ~~ Plot % change against sucrose concentration; where the line crosses zero change is the isotonic concentration.
Why are potato cylinders blotted before weighing? ~~ To remove surface solution, so only the mass of the tissue is measured and results are comparable.
How can Visking tubing show diffusion? ~~ Fill with starch and glucose, stand in water; Benedict's test outside is positive (glucose passed through) but iodine test is negative (starch too large).
How do you measure the effect of light intensity on photosynthesis using pondweed? ~~ Place pondweed in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution; vary lamp distance; count oxygen bubbles per minute; repeat at each distance.
Why is a beaker of water placed between the lamp and pondweed? ~~ It acts as a heat shield so temperature stays constant (control variable).
How is light intensity related to distance from the lamp? ~~ Light intensity is proportional to 1 ÷ distance² (inverse square law).
Describe how to test a leaf for starch. ~~ Boil in water to kill cells; heat in ethanol in a water bath to remove chlorophyll; rinse in warm water to soften; add iodine; blue-black shows starch.
Why are plants destarched before photosynthesis experiments? ~~ Keeping them in the dark for 24–48 hours uses up stored starch, so any starch detected was made during the experiment.
How can you show chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis? ~~ Destarch a variegated leaf, expose to light, then starch test; only the green areas turn blue-black.
How can you show carbon dioxide is needed for photosynthesis? ~~ Enclose a destarched leaf in a flask with soda lime (absorbs carbon dioxide); after light, starch test shows no starch, unlike a control leaf.
How do you measure the energy content of food? ~~ Burn a weighed food sample under a boiling tube of known water volume; record temperature rise. Energy (J) = mass of water × 4.2 × temperature rise.
Why is the measured energy content of food lower than the true value? ~~ Heat is lost to the air and glassware, the food may not burn completely, and some energy is used to relight it.
How do you show germinating seeds release heat? ~~ Put living and boiled (dead) peas, both rinsed in disinfectant, in vacuum flasks with thermometers; the living peas' temperature rises over several days.
Why are peas rinsed in disinfectant in the respiring seeds practical? ~~ To kill microorganisms, so heat comes only from pea respiration, not microbial respiration on dead peas.
Describe the results of the hydrogencarbonate indicator gas exchange practical. ~~ Dark: yellow (respiration only). Dim: red-orange (rates equal). Bright: purple (photosynthesis faster, carbon dioxide used).
How do you investigate the effect of exercise on breathing rate? ~~ Measure resting breaths per minute; do set exercise (e.g. 2 min step-ups); count breaths per minute immediately and each minute until back to resting.
How is a potometer used to measure transpiration rate? ~~ Cut a shoot underwater, set up airtight; measure distance an air bubble moves in the capillary per unit time; change one factor at a time.
How do you investigate the conditions needed for seed germination? ~~ Set up tubes with equal seeds each lacking one factor: dry (no water), boiled water with oil layer (no oxygen), fridge (cold), plus a complete control; count germinated seeds.
How do you estimate and compare population sizes in two areas with quadrats? ~~ Place 10+ quadrats randomly in each area, count organisms, find mean per quadrat, scale up to the total area; use the same method in both.
How do you investigate anaerobic respiration in yeast at different temperatures? ~~ Yeast and sugar solution under an oil layer in a water bath; count bubbles or volume of carbon dioxide per minute at each temperature; rate peaks around 35–40 °C.
