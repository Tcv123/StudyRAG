## T1
What is the resolution of an instrument? ~~ The smallest change in a quantity that gives a detectable change in the instrument's reading.
How are absolute uncertainties combined when adding or subtracting quantities? ~~ Add the absolute uncertainties.
How is the uncertainty in a mean estimated? ~~ Half the range of the repeated readings.
What is a zero error? ~~ A systematic error where an instrument reads a non-zero value when the true value is zero; it is corrected by subtracting it from all readings.
What is parallax error and how is it reduced? ~~ An error from reading a scale at an angle; it is reduced by viewing at eye level, perpendicular to the scale.
Give the SI prefixes from pico to tera. ~~ p 10^-12, n 10^-9, µ 10^-6, m 10^-3, k 10^3, M 10^6, G 10^9, T 10^12.
What is percentage difference and how is it used? ~~ |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100%. It is compared with the percentage uncertainty to judge whether a result agrees with the accepted value.
How do you find n in y = kx^n using a graph? ~~ Plot log y against log x. The gradient is n and the y-intercept is log k.
What is an order-of-magnitude estimate? ~~ An approximate value to the nearest power of ten, used to check whether answers are reasonable.
Express the watt in SI base units. ~~ W = J s^-1 = kg m^2 s^-3.
How are error bars used to judge a line of best fit? ~~ A valid line of best fit should pass through all (or nearly all) error bars.
How should results tables be headed? ~~ Quantity / unit (e.g. V / V), with the independent variable in the first column and readings to a consistent precision.
What is an anomalous result? ~~ A result that does not fit the pattern. It should be repeated or excluded from the mean and line of best fit.
Why use a set square or plumb line when measuring the height of a falling object? ~~ To make sure the ruler is vertical and the reading is taken level with the object, reducing systematic and parallax errors.
What is the difference between a derived unit and a base unit? ~~ Base units are defined independently (e.g. kg, m, s); derived units are combinations of base units (e.g. N = kg m s^-2).

## T2
What does the gradient of a velocity–time graph represent? ~~ Acceleration.
What does the area under a velocity–time graph represent? ~~ Displacement.
Write the four SUVAT equations. ~~ v = u + at; s = (u + v)t/2; s = ut + ½at^2; v^2 = u^2 + 2as.
What conditions are needed for the SUVAT equations to apply? ~~ The acceleration must be constant (uniform).
Describe how to determine g using a free-fall experiment. ~~ Drop a ball through a measured height and time the fall electronically (light gates or a trapdoor). Plot 2h against t^2, or use s = ½gt^2; the gradient gives g.
State Newton's first law. ~~ An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force.
State Newton's third law. ~~ If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force of the same type on A.
What is a free-body force diagram? ~~ A diagram showing a single object and all the forces acting on it, drawn as labelled arrows.
How do you find the component of a weight parallel to a slope? ~~ mg sin θ, where θ is the angle of the slope to the horizontal.
Define work done. ~~ Work = force × distance moved in the direction of the force: W = FΔs cos θ.
State the principle of conservation of energy. ~~ Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another; the total energy of a closed system is constant.
Define momentum. ~~ Momentum p = mv, a vector measured in kg m s^-1.
What does the gradient of a displacement–time graph represent? ~~ Velocity.
What is the principle of moments? ~~ For an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about that point.
Why does an object launched at 45° travel furthest on level ground? ~~ The range R = u^2 sin 2θ/g is greatest when sin 2θ = 1, i.e. θ = 45° (ignoring air resistance).
What are the energy changes for a ball thrown vertically upwards? ~~ Kinetic energy is transferred to gravitational potential energy as it rises, then back to kinetic energy as it falls (with some lost to air resistance).
What is the relationship between power and force and velocity? ~~ P = Fv for a constant force acting at constant velocity.
What is weight? ~~ The gravitational force on an object: W = mg.

## T3
Define potential difference. ~~ The energy transferred per unit charge between two points: V = W/Q.
Define resistance. ~~ The ratio of pd across a component to the current through it: R = V/I.
State the power equations for an electrical component. ~~ P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R.
State the equation for charge flowing in a current. ~~ ΔQ = IΔt.
Describe how to determine the resistivity of a wire. ~~ Measure the diameter with a micrometer at several points; measure R for different lengths l using an ammeter and voltmeter. Plot R against l; resistivity = gradient × cross-sectional area.
What is the I–V characteristic of a filament lamp and why? ~~ An S-shaped curve through the origin: as current increases, the filament heats up, so ion vibrations increase and resistance rises.
Why is the current the same at all points in a series circuit? ~~ Charge is conserved and there is only one path, so the rate of flow of charge is the same everywhere.
Why is pd the same across parallel branches? ~~ Energy is conserved; each coulomb transfers the same energy whichever branch it goes through.
What is the equation linking EMF, current, and resistances? ~~ ε = I(R + r).
Describe how to measure the EMF and internal resistance of a cell. ~~ Vary an external resistor and record V and I. Plot V against I: the y-intercept is ε and the gradient is −r.
Why does connecting more lamps in parallel to a battery reduce the brightness of each? ~~ The total current increases, so more pd is lost across the internal resistance and the terminal pd falls.
What is the effect of an LDR in a potential divider as light intensity increases? ~~ Its resistance falls, so the pd across it falls and the pd across the fixed resistor rises.
Give the equation for current in terms of drift velocity. ~~ I = nqvA.
How does n (charge carrier density) differ between metals, semiconductors and insulators? ~~ Metals have very high n (about 10^28 m^-3), semiconductors much lower and increasing with temperature, and insulators very low.
What is an ideal voltmeter and ammeter? ~~ An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance (draws no current); an ideal ammeter has zero resistance (no pd across it).
What are the units of resistivity? ~~ Ω m.
Why does a battery's terminal pd fall when current is drawn? ~~ Some energy per coulomb is transferred inside the battery because of its internal resistance (lost volts = Ir).
What is a potentiometer? ~~ A variable resistor used as a potential divider with a sliding contact, giving a continuously variable output pd.

## T4
What is upthrust? ~~ The upward force on an object in a fluid, equal to the weight of fluid displaced.
Why does an object float? ~~ The upthrust equals its weight, which happens when it displaces its own weight of fluid.
What is viscosity and how does it vary with temperature? ~~ A measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. For liquids, viscosity decreases as temperature increases.
Distinguish laminar and turbulent flow. ~~ Laminar: smooth, parallel layers with no mixing. Turbulent: chaotic flow with eddies and mixing.
Describe how to determine viscosity using a falling-ball method. ~~ Time a ball falling at terminal velocity between marks in a tube of liquid. Use W = U + F with Stokes' law F = 6πηrv to calculate η.
Define the elastic limit. ~~ The maximum stress (or force) a material can take and still return to its original shape when the load is removed.
Define the yield point. ~~ The point beyond which a material deforms plastically: large extension for little or no extra load.
What is the breaking stress? ~~ The stress at which a material fractures.
What does the gradient of a stress–strain graph show? ~~ The Young modulus (in the linear, elastic region).
What does the area under a force–extension graph represent? ~~ The work done in stretching the material (the elastic strain energy if within the elastic limit).
Describe how to determine the Young modulus of a wire. ~~ Hang masses from a long, thin wire, measure the extension with a vernier or travelling microscope, and measure the diameter with a micrometer. Plot stress against strain; the gradient is E.
Why is a long, thin wire used when measuring the Young modulus? ~~ It gives a larger extension (smaller percentage uncertainty) for a given force, and a smaller area gives a larger stress.
What is the formula for elastic strain energy? ~~ E = ½FΔx (= ½kΔx^2) for a material obeying Hooke's law.
What is the spring constant? ~~ Force per unit extension: k = F/Δx, in N m^-1.
What is meant by a stiff material? ~~ One with a high Young modulus; it needs a large stress to produce a given strain.
What is meant by a tough material? ~~ One that absorbs a lot of energy before breaking, so it resists crack propagation.
What is meant by a hard material? ~~ One that resists scratching and indentation at its surface.
What happens to a copper wire loaded beyond its elastic limit? ~~ It deforms plastically: it is permanently stretched and does not return to its original length.

## T5
What is the equation linking wave speed, frequency and wavelength? ~~ v = fλ.
Distinguish transverse and longitudinal waves. ~~ Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer.
What is the speed of a transverse wave on a string? ~~ v = √(T/µ), where T is tension and µ is mass per unit length.
How are nodes and antinodes produced on a stationary wave? ~~ By superposition of two waves of the same frequency travelling in opposite directions. Nodes have destructive interference; antinodes have constructive interference.
What is the separation between adjacent nodes? ~~ λ/2.
What is the critical angle? ~~ The angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is 90°: sin C = 1/n (for a boundary with air).
What is total internal reflection and when does it occur? ~~ All light is reflected at a boundary. It occurs when light goes from a denser to a less dense medium at an angle greater than the critical angle.
How does a lens form a real image? ~~ Refraction converges rays from each point of the object to a corresponding point on the other side of the lens.
State the thin lens equation and power of a lens. ~~ 1/u + 1/v = 1/f (real is positive), and power P = 1/f (in dioptres, f in metres).
What is plane polarisation? ~~ Oscillations of a transverse wave restricted to one plane.
Give an application of polarisation. ~~ Polaroid sunglasses reduce glare from reflected light; stress analysis in transparent materials.
How do you calculate photon energy in eV? ~~ E = hf in joules, divided by 1.6 × 10^-19 to convert to electronvolts.
What is the threshold frequency? ~~ The minimum frequency for photoelectric emission: f0 = φ/h.
How are energy level diagrams used to find emitted wavelengths? ~~ ΔE = hf = hc/λ, where ΔE is the difference between the two energy levels involved.
What is the Doppler effect for sound? ~~ The change in observed frequency when a source moves relative to an observer: higher when approaching, lower when receding.
What is pulse–echo used for, and how is distance calculated? ~~ Ultrasound imaging and sonar. Distance = (speed × time)/2, since the pulse travels there and back.
What limits the resolution of a pulse–echo system? ~~ The wavelength (smaller wavelength gives better resolution) and the pulse duration (shorter pulses give better resolution).
What is the difference between coherent and in-phase? ~~ Coherent: constant phase difference and the same frequency. In phase: phase difference of zero.

## T6
What is the equation for the centripetal force? ~~ F = mv^2/r = mω^2r.
Give the relationship between linear and angular velocity. ~~ v = ωr.
What is the period of rotation related to angular velocity? ~~ T = 2π/ω.
Define the radian. ~~ The angle subtended by an arc length equal to the radius; 2π rad = 360°.
Why is kinetic energy not conserved in inelastic collisions? ~~ Some kinetic energy is transferred to other forms such as heat, sound and deformation.
Give the kinetic energy in terms of momentum. ~~ E_k = p^2/(2m).
How is momentum conserved in an explosion from rest? ~~ The total momentum before is zero, so the fragments move off with equal and opposite momenta.
State the tension in a string at the top of a vertical circle. ~~ T = mv^2/r − mg.
What is the minimum speed at the top of a vertical circle for the string to stay taut? ~~ v = √(gr), when tension is zero and gravity alone provides the centripetal force.
Describe how to investigate a collision using light gates. ~~ Use trolleys on a friction-compensated track with interrupt cards. Light gates record speeds before and after, then compare total momentum before and after.
What is the resultant force on an object moving at constant speed in a circle? ~~ A centripetal force of mv^2/r directed towards the centre.
How is a 2D collision momentum problem solved? ~~ Resolve momenta into two perpendicular directions and apply conservation of momentum separately in each direction.
State Newton's second law in terms of momentum. ~~ F = Δp/Δt: the resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum.
Why does a gun recoil? ~~ The bullet gains forward momentum; by conservation of momentum the gun gains equal backward momentum.
What is the frequency of rotation of an object with ω = 4π rad s^-1? ~~ f = ω/2π = 2 Hz.
A 2 kg ball moving at 3 m s^-1 hits a wall and rebounds at 2 m s^-1. What is the change in momentum? ~~ Δp = 2 × (−2) − 2 × 3 = −10 kg m s^-1 (magnitude 10 kg m s^-1, directed away from the wall).
Why do passengers feel pushed outwards on a roundabout? ~~ Their inertia tends to keep them moving in a straight line; the seat or wall provides the inward centripetal force, which they feel as an outward push.
What is a conical pendulum? ~~ A mass on a string moving in a horizontal circle. The horizontal component of tension provides the centripetal force; the vertical component balances weight.

## T7
Define electric field strength. ~~ The force per unit positive charge: E = F/Q, in N C^-1 or V m^-1.
State the field strength of a radial field. ~~ E = Q/(4πε0r^2).
What is the field between parallel plates? ~~ E = V/d, uniform, from positive to negative.
What is electric potential at a point? ~~ The work done per unit charge bringing a positive test charge from infinity: V = Q/(4πε0r).
What are equipotentials? ~~ Lines or surfaces of equal potential. They are perpendicular to field lines, and no work is done moving a charge along them.
Give the energy stored in a capacitor in three forms. ~~ W = ½QV = ½CV^2 = ½Q^2/C.
Give the discharge equations for a capacitor through a resistor. ~~ Q = Q0 e^(−t/RC); V = V0 e^(−t/RC); I = I0 e^(−t/RC).
How can you find the time constant from a discharge graph? ~~ Read the time taken for the value to fall to 37% of its initial value, or plot ln V against t; the gradient is −1/RC.
Give the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field. ~~ F = BIl sin θ.
What is Fleming's left-hand rule used for? ~~ Finding the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor in a field: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion.
What is Lenz's law? ~~ The direction of an induced EMF (or current) opposes the change that produced it.
What is the induced EMF in a straight conductor moving through a field? ~~ ε = Blv, where l is length and v is speed perpendicular to the field.
Describe the output of an AC generator. ~~ A sinusoidal EMF: ε = BANω sin ωt. Its peak rises with field, area, number of turns and rotation speed.
How is a transformer designed to step up voltage? ~~ The secondary has more turns than the primary: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np.
Why are transformers used in the National Grid? ~~ Stepping up the voltage reduces the current for the same power, reducing I^2R losses in the cables.
What is the radius of the path of a charged particle in a magnetic field? ~~ r = p/(BQ) = mv/(BQ).
Why doesn't a magnetic field do work on a moving charge? ~~ The force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes direction but not speed or kinetic energy.
What is the unit of magnetic flux? ~~ The weber (Wb): 1 Wb = 1 T m^2.

## T8
What is a hadron? ~~ A particle made of quarks that feels the strong force: baryons (three quarks) and mesons (quark–antiquark).
What is a lepton? ~~ A fundamental particle that does not feel the strong force, e.g. electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos.
Give the charges of the up, down and strange quarks. ~~ Up +2/3 e; down −1/3 e; strange −1/3 e.
What is an antiparticle? ~~ A particle with the same mass but opposite charge and opposite quantum numbers to its partner particle.
What happens when a particle meets its antiparticle? ~~ They annihilate, converting their mass into energy as photons (usually two, to conserve momentum).
How does a linear accelerator (linac) work? ~~ Charged particles pass through drift tubes connected to an alternating pd; they accelerate across each gap, and tube lengths increase so the timing matches the AC frequency.
How does a cyclotron work? ~~ A magnetic field makes particles move in semicircles within two dees; an alternating pd across the gap accelerates them each half-turn, so they spiral outwards.
Why is the cyclotron frequency independent of speed (non-relativistically)? ~~ The time per revolution T = 2πm/(BQ) does not depend on speed or radius.
Why do particle detectors use magnetic fields? ~~ Charged particles curve; the direction of curvature shows the sign of the charge and the radius gives the momentum (r = p/BQ).
Why do heavier particles need more energetic collisions to create? ~~ By E = mc^2, the collision energy must at least equal the rest energy of the particles created.
What is the unit MeV/c^2 used for? ~~ Mass in particle physics: rest energy (MeV) divided by c^2.
What is the Standard Model? ~~ The theory that describes fundamental particles (six quarks, six leptons) and their interactions via exchange particles (gluons, photons, W and Z bosons), plus the Higgs boson.
What is conserved in all particle interactions? ~~ Charge, baryon number, lepton number (each family), momentum and mass–energy.
What is strangeness and when is it conserved? ~~ A quantum number for the strange quark (s = −1). It is conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions but not in weak interactions.
Why can't free quarks be observed? ~~ Quark confinement: the energy needed to separate quarks creates new quark–antiquark pairs instead.
What was the evidence for quarks from deep inelastic scattering? ~~ High-energy electrons scattering off protons were deflected through large angles, showing point-like charged constituents inside the proton.
What is the de Broglie wavelength equation? ~~ λ = h/p = h/(mv).
Why is the electronvolt used for particle energies? ~~ It is a convenient size for atomic and nuclear energies: 1 eV = 1.6 × 10^-19 J.

## T9
What is absolute zero? ~~ 0 K (−273 °C), the lowest possible temperature, where particles have minimum kinetic energy.
Convert 27 °C to kelvin. ~~ 27 + 273 = 300 K.
Describe how to measure the specific heat capacity of a metal block. ~~ Heat a block of known mass with an electric heater, measuring V, I and time. Record the temperature rise; c = VIt/(mΔθ). Insulate the block to reduce heat loss.
What is Boyle's law? ~~ For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pV = constant.
Describe how to investigate Boyle's law. ~~ Trap air in a tube using oil; change the pressure with a pump and read pressure and volume. Plot p against 1/V for a straight line through the origin.
What is Charles's law? ~~ For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, V/T = constant.
What is the pressure law? ~~ For a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, p/T = constant.
State the Boltzmann constant. ~~ k = R/N_A ≈ 1.38 × 10^-23 J K^-1.
What is the mean kinetic energy of an ideal gas molecule? ~~ ½m<c^2> = (3/2)kT.
What is the root mean square speed? ~~ The square root of the mean of the squares of the molecular speeds; c_rms = √(3kT/m).
Why do gases exert pressure? ~~ Molecules collide with the walls and change momentum; the rate of change of momentum per unit area is the pressure.
What happens to the internal energy of an ideal gas as temperature increases? ~~ It increases, because the internal energy of an ideal gas is entirely kinetic and proportional to T.
What happens to the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution when temperature increases? ~~ The peak moves to higher speed and becomes lower and broader; the area under the curve stays the same.
How can energy transferred be found from a heating curve? ~~ Flat sections: E = mL (change of state). Sloping sections: E = mcΔθ.
What is the internal energy of a system? ~~ The sum of the random kinetic and potential energies of all its molecules.
Why must gas laws use temperature in kelvin? ~~ The relationships are proportional only on an absolute scale, where zero means minimum energy.
What does "ideal gas" mean? ~~ A gas that obeys pV = NkT exactly: molecules have negligible volume and no intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions.
Calculate the number of molecules in 1.0 m^3 of gas at 1.0 × 10^5 Pa and 300 K. ~~ N = pV/(kT) = (1.0 × 10^5 × 1.0)/(1.38 × 10^-23 × 300) ≈ 2.4 × 10^25.

## T10
What is a black body? ~~ An ideal body that absorbs all radiation falling on it and emits a characteristic spectrum that depends only on its temperature.
Give Stefan's law. ~~ L = σAT^4, where σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant.
What is Wien's law? ~~ λ_max T = 2.898 × 10^-3 m K.
What is the inverse square law for intensity from a star? ~~ I = L/(4πd^2).
How are the radius of a star and its luminosity related? ~~ L = 4πr^2σT^4, so r can be found from luminosity and surface temperature.
What is trigonometric parallax? ~~ The apparent shift of a nearby star against distant stars as the Earth orbits the Sun. d (parsec) = 1/p (arcsecond).
What is a standard candle? ~~ An object of known luminosity, e.g. a Cepheid variable or a Type 1a supernova. Measuring its intensity gives its distance.
Where is the main sequence on an HR diagram? ~~ A diagonal band from hot, luminous stars (top left) to cool, dim stars (bottom right).
Where are red giants and white dwarfs on an HR diagram? ~~ Red giants: top right (cool, luminous). White dwarfs: bottom left (hot, dim).
What happens when a star leaves the main sequence? ~~ Hydrogen in the core runs out, the core contracts and heats, hydrogen fuses in a shell, and the outer layers expand to form a red giant.
What is a neutron star? ~~ The very dense remnant core of a supernova, made mainly of neutrons, supported by neutron degeneracy pressure.
What is a black hole? ~~ A region where gravity is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light.
What does Hubble's constant tell us about the universe? ~~ The universe is expanding; 1/H0 estimates its age.
What is dark matter? ~~ Matter that does not emit light, inferred from the gravitational effects (e.g. galaxy rotation curves) that visible matter cannot explain.
What is the ultimate fate of the universe believed to depend on? ~~ Its density compared with the critical density (and dark energy): it may expand forever, stop, or collapse.
Why is it difficult to predict the fate of the universe? ~~ The amount of dark matter and the nature of dark energy are uncertain.
What is the Hubble constant's approximate value? ~~ About 70 km s^-1 Mpc^-1 (about 2.3 × 10^-18 s^-1).
Why can parallax only be used for nearby stars? ~~ For distant stars the angle is too small to measure accurately.

## T11
What are the three types of nuclear radiation and their natures? ~~ Alpha: helium nucleus (2p + 2n). Beta−: fast electron. Gamma: high-frequency electromagnetic radiation.
What stops alpha, beta and gamma radiation? ~~ Alpha: paper or a few cm of air. Beta: a few mm of aluminium. Gamma: several cm of lead (only reduced).
What is ionising radiation and why is it harmful? ~~ Radiation that removes electrons from atoms, creating ions. This can damage DNA and cause cell mutation or death.
What is background radiation and give its main sources? ~~ Radiation always present, mainly from radon gas, rocks and buildings, cosmic rays, food, and medical sources.
What is the decay constant? ~~ The probability per unit time that a nucleus will decay: λ = A/N.
Give the half-life in terms of the decay constant. ~~ t½ = ln 2/λ.
What is meant by radioactive decay being random and spontaneous? ~~ Random: you can't predict which nucleus decays next. Spontaneous: decay is unaffected by external conditions such as temperature.
Give the exponential decay equation for activity. ~~ A = A0 e^(−λt).
How can you find half-life from a graph of ln A against t? ~~ The gradient is −λ; then t½ = ln 2/λ.
Describe how to investigate the absorption of gamma radiation by lead. ~~ Measure the background count. Place increasing thicknesses of lead between the source and the GM tube, record count rates and subtract background. Plot ln(count rate) against thickness.
What is beta-plus decay? ~~ A proton changes into a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino.
Why is a GM tube placed at a fixed distance from a source? ~~ Count rate depends on distance (inverse square for gamma), so distance must be controlled.
Why must gamma sources be handled with tongs? ~~ Distance reduces exposure (intensity falls with the inverse square of distance).
What is the unit of activity? ~~ The becquerel (Bq): one decay per second.
Why is a radioactive tracer chosen with a short half-life? ~~ It decays quickly, so the patient's dose is limited, while still lasting long enough for the procedure.
What happens to the nucleon and proton numbers in alpha decay? ~~ The nucleon number decreases by 4 and the proton number decreases by 2.
What happens to the nucleon and proton numbers in beta-minus decay? ~~ The nucleon number is unchanged and the proton number increases by 1.
How is a nucleus stabilised after emitting alpha or beta? ~~ It may be left in an excited state and emit a gamma photon to reach its ground state.

## T12
State Newton's law of gravitation in equation form. ~~ F = Gm1m2/r^2, with G = 6.67 × 10^-11 N m^2 kg^-2.
What is the gravitational field strength near Earth's surface? ~~ g ≈ 9.81 N kg^-1 (equivalent to m s^-2).
Define gravitational potential. ~~ The work done per unit mass bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point: V = −GM/r.
What is the relationship between g and gravitational potential? ~~ g = −ΔV/Δr.
Give the gravitational potential energy of a mass m at distance r from M. ~~ E_p = −GMm/r.
What is the orbital speed of a satellite at radius r? ~~ v = √(GM/r).
What is the period of a geostationary satellite? ~~ 24 hours (one sidereal day, about 23 h 56 min), orbiting above the equator in the same direction as Earth rotates.
Why does a satellite in a lower orbit travel faster? ~~ v = √(GM/r), so a smaller r gives a larger orbital speed.
What happens to the total energy of a satellite moved to a higher orbit? ~~ Its total energy (−GMm/2r) increases (becomes less negative), so work must be done on it.
Sketch the variation of g with distance from the centre of a uniform planet. ~~ g increases linearly from zero at the centre to a maximum at the surface, then decreases as 1/r^2 outside.
What is the gravitational field inside a hollow spherical shell? ~~ Zero.
Why do astronauts in orbit appear weightless? ~~ They and the spacecraft are both in free fall, accelerating at the same rate towards Earth, so there is no contact force.
Define a radial field and a uniform field. ~~ Radial: field lines spread out from a point, so strength falls with distance. Uniform: parallel, equally spaced lines with constant strength (e.g. near Earth's surface).
What is the work done to move a satellite from the Earth's surface to infinity? ~~ W = GMm/R: the magnitude of its gravitational potential energy at the surface.
Why is the escape velocity independent of the mass of the object? ~~ Both kinetic and potential energy are proportional to m, so m cancels: v = √(2GM/r).
What is the gravitational field strength at a height equal to Earth's radius above the surface? ~~ The distance from the centre doubles, so g falls to a quarter: about 2.45 N kg^-1.
How can the mass of a planet be found using a moon's orbit? ~~ Measure the orbital radius r and period T; then M = 4π^2r^3/(GT^2).
What is the difference between gravitational potential and gravitational potential energy? ~~ Potential is energy per unit mass (J kg^-1) at a point; potential energy is the energy of a specific mass at that point (J).
Why is the gravitational force always attractive? ~~ Mass is only ever positive, unlike charge, so gravitational forces always attract.
How does the gravitational force change if the separation between two masses is tripled? ~~ It falls to 1/9 of its original value (inverse square law).

## T13
Give the defining equation of SHM. ~~ a = −ω^2x.
Give the displacement equation for SHM starting from maximum displacement. ~~ x = A cos ωt.
Give the maximum speed in SHM. ~~ v_max = ωA, at the equilibrium position.
Give the maximum acceleration in SHM. ~~ a_max = ω^2A, at maximum displacement.
Where in SHM is velocity maximum and acceleration zero? ~~ At the equilibrium position (x = 0).
What is the total energy of a simple harmonic oscillator? ~~ E = ½mω^2A^2, which is constant for undamped motion.
What is the phase difference between velocity and displacement in SHM? ~~ π/2 rad (a quarter of a cycle); velocity leads displacement.
Describe free oscillations. ~~ Oscillations at the natural frequency with no external driving force and no energy loss.
What are forced oscillations? ~~ Oscillations caused by an external periodic driving force; the system oscillates at the driving frequency.
What is critical damping? ~~ The level of damping that brings a system to rest at equilibrium in the shortest time without overshooting.
How does increasing damping affect the resonance curve? ~~ The peak amplitude decreases, the peak becomes broader, and the resonant frequency shifts slightly lower.
Describe how to investigate SHM using a mass on a spring. ~~ Displace the mass and time 10 oscillations with a fiducial marker. Repeat for different masses and plot T^2 against m; the gradient is 4π^2/k.
How does the period of a mass-spring system change if the mass is quadrupled? ~~ T ∝ √m, so the period doubles.
What is the angular frequency? ~~ ω = 2πf = 2π/T, in rad s^-1.
What is the small-angle approximation needed for simple pendulum SHM? ~~ sin θ ≈ θ (in radians) for angles below about 10°, so restoring force is proportional to displacement.
What is the phase relationship between driver and oscillator at resonance? ~~ The oscillator lags the driver by π/2.
