## S1
Why do larger animals need a mass transport system? ~~ They have a small surface area to volume ratio and large diffusion distances, so diffusion alone is too slow to supply all cells.
Describe the structure of an artery and relate it to function. ~~ Thick muscular and elastic walls withstand and maintain high pressure, and recoil to smooth blood flow; the lumen is narrow.
How does blood clotting occur? ~~ Damaged tissue and platelets release thromboplastin, which converts prothrombin to thrombin (with Ca2+); thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin, forming a mesh that traps cells.
What are monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides? ~~ Monosaccharides are single sugar units (glucose); disaccharides are two joined by a glycosidic bond (maltose); polysaccharides are many joined (starch, glycogen).
Describe the structure of amylose and amylopectin. ~~ Amylose: unbranched chains of α-glucose with 1,4-glycosidic bonds, coiling into a helix. Amylopectin: branched, with 1,6-glycosidic bonds as well.
How does glycogen differ from amylopectin? ~~ Glycogen has more branches and shorter chains, so it can be hydrolysed faster to release glucose.
How is a triglyceride formed? ~~ By condensation of one glycerol and three fatty acids, forming three ester bonds and releasing three water molecules.
What is the role of blood cholesterol in CVD? ~~ High LDL levels deposit cholesterol in artery walls, contributing to atheroma formation.
What is an energy budget? ~~ The balance between energy intake (food) and energy output (BMR and activity). A positive budget leads to weight gain.
How are BMI and waist-to-hip ratio used? ~~ BMI = mass (kg)/height^2 (m^2); a waist-to-hip ratio above 0.9 (men) or 0.85 (women) suggests increased CVD risk from abdominal fat.
How do antihypertensives reduce CVD risk? ~~ They lower blood pressure (e.g. diuretics, beta blockers, ACE inhibitors), reducing endothelial damage.
Give a risk of taking statins. ~~ Side effects such as muscle pain, liver damage and digestive problems.
How do anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors reduce CVD risk? ~~ They reduce the formation of blood clots (e.g. warfarin, aspirin), but increase the risk of excessive bleeding.
What is hypertension and how does it increase CVD risk? ~~ Persistently high blood pressure, which damages the endothelium of arteries and starts atherosclerosis.
Why are people's perceptions of risk often inaccurate? ~~ They overestimate unfamiliar or dramatic risks and underestimate familiar or voluntary ones (e.g. smoking).
What is the role of a double-blind trial? ~~ Neither participants nor researchers know who receives the treatment or placebo, reducing bias.
Why is the heart's left ventricle wall thicker than the right? ~~ It pumps blood around the whole body at high pressure, while the right ventricle only pumps to the nearby lungs.

## S2
What is the role of the quaternary structure of haemoglobin? ~~ Four polypeptide chains (2α, 2β), each with a haem group, allow cooperative binding of four O2 molecules.
What is a globular protein? Give an example. ~~ A compact, spherical, soluble protein with a specific shape, e.g. haemoglobin or enzymes.
What is a fibrous protein? Give an example. ~~ A long, insoluble protein with a structural role, e.g. collagen, which forms strong fibres from three chains wound together.
What is the role of mRNA? ~~ It carries a copy of the genetic code from DNA in the nucleus to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
Why does a mutation not always affect protein structure? ~~ The code is degenerate, so a substitution may still code for the same amino acid, or the mutation may be in an intron.
What is a gene? ~~ A sequence of bases on DNA that codes for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.
Explain how enzymes lower activation energy. ~~ They form an enzyme–substrate complex, which puts strain on bonds or brings reactants together, making the reaction easier.
How is the initial rate of an enzyme reaction calculated? ~~ Draw a tangent to the curve at t = 0 and calculate its gradient.
How does the CFTR protein function normally? ~~ It is a channel protein that transports chloride ions out of epithelial cells, drawing water after them to keep the mucus thin.
Why does cystic fibrosis affect the digestive system? ~~ Thick mucus blocks the pancreatic duct, so digestive enzymes can't reach the small intestine; food is poorly absorbed.
Why does cystic fibrosis affect the reproductive system? ~~ Thick mucus blocks the sperm ducts (vas deferens) in males and the cervix in females, reducing fertility.
What is gene therapy for cystic fibrosis? ~~ Inserting normal CFTR alleles into the epithelial cells of the lungs using viral vectors or liposomes.
What is a carrier? ~~ A heterozygous individual with one recessive allele for a condition, who doesn't show symptoms but can pass it on.
What is the difference between a genotype and a phenotype? ~~ Genotype: the alleles an organism has. Phenotype: its observable characteristics, resulting from its genotype and environment.
What is preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)? ~~ Testing embryos produced by IVF for genetic disorders before implantation, so only unaffected embryos are implanted.

## S3
What is the role of ribosomes? ~~ The site of protein synthesis (translation), made of rRNA and protein.
What is the role of rough endoplasmic reticulum? ~~ It has ribosomes on its surface, and it transports and folds proteins made on them.
How are proteins secreted from a cell? ~~ Made on the RER, transported in vesicles to the Golgi to be modified and packaged, then carried in vesicles to fuse with the cell membrane (exocytosis).
What is the structure of a prokaryotic cell? ~~ No nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; circular DNA, plasmids, 70S ribosomes, a cell wall, and sometimes a capsule, pili and flagella.
What are the functions of the acrosome and zona pellucida? ~~ The acrosome releases enzymes that digest the zona pellucida (the glycoprotein layer around the egg) so sperm can penetrate.
What is the cortical reaction? ~~ After a sperm enters, cortical granules release enzymes that harden the zona pellucida, preventing polyspermy.
What happens in the S phase of interphase? ~~ DNA is replicated.
What is a chromatid? ~~ One of two identical copies of a replicated chromosome, joined at the centromere.
Why is mitosis important? ~~ Growth, repair, asexual reproduction, and producing genetically identical cells.
What is a homologous pair of chromosomes? ~~ Two chromosomes (one maternal, one paternal) with the same genes at the same loci, but possibly different alleles.
What happens in anaphase of mitosis? ~~ Spindle fibres shorten, separating the chromatids at the centromere and pulling them to opposite poles.
What is cell differentiation? ~~ The process by which cells become specialised for a function, by expressing only certain genes.
How can differential gene expression lead to specialisation? ~~ Transcription factors activate or repress certain genes, so only specific proteins are produced in each cell type.
What is polygenic inheritance? ~~ A characteristic controlled by many genes, giving continuous variation, e.g. height.
What is the role of the environment in phenotype? Give an example. ~~ The environment can modify gene expression, e.g. nutrition affects height, and sunlight affects skin colour.
What is epigenetics? ~~ Changes in gene expression without changes to DNA sequence, e.g. DNA methylation or histone modification, which can be inherited.
How does cell division by meiosis produce haploid cells? ~~ Two divisions: homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I, then chromatids separate in meiosis II, giving four haploid cells.

## S4
What is natural selection? ~~ Individuals with advantageous alleles are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing on those alleles, so allele frequency increases over generations.
What is an adaptation? ~~ A feature that increases an organism's chance of survival and reproduction in its environment.
Distinguish behavioural, physiological and anatomical adaptations. ~~ Behavioural: actions (migration). Physiological: internal processes (hibernation). Anatomical: structures (thick fur).
What is species richness? ~~ The number of different species in an area.
What is genetic diversity? ~~ The variety of alleles in the gene pool of a species or population.
How is heterozygosity index calculated? ~~ H = number of heterozygotes ÷ number of individuals in the population.
Describe the structure of cellulose. ~~ Long, straight chains of β-glucose linked by 1,4-glycosidic bonds, with alternate glucose molecules inverted; hydrogen bonds link chains into microfibrils.
What is the function of sclerenchyma fibres? ~~ They provide mechanical support; they are dead cells with walls thickened with lignin.
Why are plant fibres useful to humans? ~~ They are strong and are used for ropes and fabrics, e.g. hemp and flax.
How are starch and cellulose used in sustainable products? ~~ Starch can make bioplastics, and cellulose fibres make biodegradable products.
What minerals do plants need and why? ~~ Nitrates (amino acids, nucleic acids), magnesium (chlorophyll), calcium (cell walls, calcium pectate) and phosphates (ATP, DNA).
What is the role of the plasmodesmata? ~~ Channels through plant cell walls linking cytoplasm of neighbouring cells, allowing transport and communication.
How can the antimicrobial properties of plants be investigated? ~~ Place discs soaked in plant extracts on agar seeded with bacteria and measure the zones of inhibition, using aseptic technique.
What is the role of the Millennium Seed Bank? ~~ It stores seeds of many plant species at low temperature and humidity to conserve genetic diversity for future reintroduction.
What are the problems with captive breeding? ~~ Small populations have low genetic diversity, animals may not adapt to the wild, and it is expensive.
How can reintroduction programmes succeed? ~~ Habitat restoration, community involvement, monitoring, and gradual acclimatisation of animals.

## S5
What is the role of ATP in photosynthesis? ~~ It provides energy for the reduction of GP to GALP in the Calvin cycle and for regenerating RuBP.
What is photophosphorylation? ~~ The production of ATP from ADP and Pi using light energy, via an electron transport chain in the thylakoid membrane.
What happens to GALP produced in the Calvin cycle? ~~ Most is used to regenerate RuBP; some is used to make glucose, starch, cellulose, amino acids and lipids.
What is the role of NADP in photosynthesis? ~~ It accepts electrons and hydrogen ions in the light-dependent reaction (forming reduced NADP), which are used to reduce GP in the Calvin cycle.
What is gross primary productivity? ~~ The total rate at which energy is fixed as chemical energy by producers through photosynthesis.
Why is energy lost between trophic levels? ~~ Energy is lost through respiration (heat), excretion, egestion, and parts not eaten.
How is efficiency of energy transfer calculated? ~~ (Energy in the higher trophic level ÷ energy in the lower trophic level) × 100%.
What is the greenhouse effect? ~~ Greenhouse gases absorb infrared radiation re-emitted from the Earth's surface and re-radiate it, warming the atmosphere.
Why are methane and CO2 levels rising? ~~ CO2: burning fossil fuels and deforestation. Methane: cattle farming, rice paddies and landfill.
How are dendrochronology and ice cores used as evidence for climate change? ~~ Tree ring widths show past growing conditions; air bubbles in ice cores show past atmospheric CO2 and temperature.
How does temperature affect enzyme-controlled processes in organisms? ~~ Rates increase up to an optimum; above it, enzymes denature. The Q10 value describes the change for a 10 °C rise.
How is Q10 calculated? ~~ Q10 = rate at (T + 10 °C) ÷ rate at T.
What is a biotic and an abiotic factor? ~~ Biotic: living factors (predation, competition, disease). Abiotic: non-living factors (temperature, light, pH, water).
What is a climax community? ~~ The final, stable community in succession, e.g. oak woodland in the UK.
How do scientists use evidence to reach a consensus on climate change? ~~ Peer review of data and models, repeated measurements from different sources, and bodies such as the IPCC assessing all the evidence.
What is the role of decomposers in the carbon cycle? ~~ They break down dead organic matter, releasing CO2 through respiration.

## S6
What is the structure of a bacterium and a virus? ~~ Bacterium: prokaryotic cell with a cell wall, circular DNA, plasmids and ribosomes. Virus: nucleic acid in a protein capsid, sometimes with a lipid envelope.
How does Mycobacterium tuberculosis cause disease? ~~ It infects lung macrophages and survives inside them, forming tubercles; the lung tissue is damaged and the immune system is weakened.
How does HIV affect the immune system? ~~ It infects T helper cells, reducing their number, so the immune system can't respond effectively, leading to AIDS and opportunistic infections.
What is the lytic cycle of a virus? ~~ The virus attaches, injects its nucleic acid, replicates using host cell machinery, assembles new viruses, and bursts (lyses) the cell.
What is the latent (lysogenic) cycle? ~~ Viral DNA integrates into the host genome and stays dormant, replicating with the host, until triggered to enter the lytic cycle.
What are the barriers to infection? ~~ Skin, stomach acid, mucous membranes, lysozyme in tears, and gut and skin microbiota.
What is inflammation? ~~ Mast cells release histamine, dilating blood vessels and increasing permeability, so phagocytes and plasma move into the tissue.
What is the role of interferon? ~~ Proteins released by virus-infected cells that inhibit viral replication in neighbouring cells.
What are memory cells? ~~ Long-lived B and T cells that remain after infection and respond rapidly to the same antigen on reinfection.
What are bacteriostatic and bactericidal antibiotics? ~~ Bacteriostatic antibiotics prevent bacterial growth (e.g. tetracycline); bactericidal antibiotics kill bacteria (e.g. penicillin).
How can hospitals reduce antibiotic resistance? ~~ Prescribing antibiotics only when necessary, completing courses, hand hygiene, and screening and isolating infected patients.
How does rigor mortis help estimate time of death? ~~ Muscles stiffen 2–6 hours after death as ATP runs out and cross-bridges stay fixed; it passes after about 36 hours as proteins break down.
How does body temperature help estimate time of death? ~~ The body cools at about 1.5 °C per hour after death, depending on conditions.
How does forensic entomology estimate time of death? ~~ The species and developmental stage of insects (e.g. blowfly larvae) on the body indicate how long it has been dead.
What is the role of gel electrophoresis in DNA profiling? ~~ It separates DNA fragments by length: smaller fragments move faster through the gel towards the positive electrode.
What is the evolutionary race between pathogens and host? ~~ Pathogens evolve to evade the immune system (e.g. antigenic variation), while hosts evolve better defences.

## S7
What is the role of coenzyme A? ~~ It carries acetyl groups from the link reaction to the Krebs cycle.
Where does glycolysis take place and what is produced? ~~ In the cytoplasm: glucose is converted into 2 pyruvate, with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 reduced NAD.
What happens to lactate after anaerobic respiration? ~~ It is carried to the liver and converted back to pyruvate (or glucose), using oxygen, which repays the oxygen debt.
What is the role of the electron transport chain? ~~ Electrons pass along carriers, releasing energy to pump H+ into the intermembrane space. H+ flows back through ATP synthase, making ATP.
How do skeletal muscles and joints work together? ~~ Muscles are attached to bones by tendons and work in antagonistic pairs across joints; ligaments hold bones together.
What is the role of troponin and tropomyosin? ~~ At rest, tropomyosin blocks the myosin binding sites on actin. Ca2+ binds to troponin, moving tropomyosin and exposing the sites.
What are the adaptations of slow-twitch muscle fibres? ~~ Many mitochondria, lots of myoglobin, and a rich capillary supply, suited to aerobic respiration and endurance.
What does the SAN do? ~~ The sinoatrial node is the pacemaker: it generates waves of excitation that spread across the atria.
How does the cardiovascular control centre alter heart rate? ~~ In the medulla, it receives input from chemoreceptors and baroreceptors, and sends impulses by sympathetic (increase) or vagus/parasympathetic (decrease) nerves.
How does exercise affect breathing rate? ~~ Increased CO2 lowers blood pH, detected by chemoreceptors; the ventilation centre increases the rate and depth of breathing.
What is the role of the hypothalamus in thermoregulation? ~~ It detects blood temperature and triggers responses: sweating and vasodilation to cool, or shivering and vasoconstriction to warm.
What is negative feedback? ~~ A change from the norm triggers a response that restores the norm.
What is positive feedback? ~~ A change triggers a response that increases the change further, e.g. oxytocin release during childbirth.
How can excessive exercise damage the body? ~~ Wear and tear on joints, and suppression of the immune system, increasing the risk of infection.
How does erythropoietin (EPO) enhance performance? ~~ It stimulates red blood cell production, increasing the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
What are the risks of taking anabolic steroids? ~~ Liver damage, high blood pressure, infertility, and aggression.

## S8
What is the structure of a sensory, relay and motor neurone? ~~ Sensory: long dendron and short axon, cell body in the middle. Relay: short, many connections, in the CNS. Motor: cell body in the CNS, long axon.
What is the function of the autonomic nervous system? ~~ Controls involuntary actions (heart rate, digestion), with sympathetic (fight or flight) and parasympathetic (rest and digest) divisions.
What is the role of the sodium–potassium pump? ~~ It actively pumps 3 Na+ out of and 2 K+ into the axon, helping to maintain the resting potential.
Describe depolarisation and repolarisation. ~~ Depolarisation: voltage-gated Na+ channels open, Na+ floods in. Repolarisation: Na+ channels close and K+ channels open, K+ diffuses out.
What is hyperpolarisation? ~~ The potential becomes more negative than the resting potential because K+ channels are slow to close.
What is the pupil reflex? ~~ Bright light is detected by the retina; impulses go to the brain, and circular muscles of the iris contract, making the pupil smaller.
How is light detected by rods? ~~ Light bleaches rhodopsin into retinal and opsin, which closes Na+ channels, hyperpolarising the rod and stopping neurotransmitter release. This triggers an action potential in bipolar neurones.
What is habituation? ~~ A reduced response to a repeated harmless stimulus, caused by less neurotransmitter being released at synapses.
What is the critical window for visual development? ~~ A period in early life when the visual cortex must receive stimulation to develop normally, shown by Hubel and Wiesel's kittens.
What is the role of the hypothalamus? ~~ It regulates body temperature, water balance and hunger, and controls the pituitary gland.
How do fMRI scans work? ~~ They detect changes in blood flow (oxygenated haemoglobin) to active brain areas, showing function.
How does a drug like L-DOPA treat Parkinson's disease? ~~ It is converted to dopamine in the brain, replacing lost dopamine and reducing symptoms.
What is depression linked to? ~~ Low levels of serotonin. SSRIs block serotonin reuptake, increasing its level at synapses.
What is the Human Genome Project's role in developing drugs? ~~ It helps identify target genes and proteins, and personalised medicine based on genotype (pharmacogenomics).
How are GM organisms used to produce drugs? ~~ Genes for human proteins are inserted into microorganisms, plants or animals, which produce the proteins (e.g. insulin by bacteria).
