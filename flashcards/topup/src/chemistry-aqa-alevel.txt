## P1
What is the relative charge and relative mass of a proton, neutron and electron? ~~ Proton +1, mass 1; neutron 0, mass 1; electron −1, mass about 1/1840.
Why is there a decrease in first ionisation energy from Mg to Al? ~~ Al's outer electron is in a 3p sub-shell, which is higher in energy and slightly more shielded than Mg's 3s, so it is easier to remove.
Why do successive ionisation energies always increase? ~~ Each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion, so there is less repulsion and the remaining electrons are held more strongly.
State Avogadro's constant and how to calculate moles from mass. ~~ N_A = 6.022 × 10^23 mol^-1. Moles = mass (g) ÷ M_r.
How is concentration calculated from moles and volume? ~~ Concentration (mol dm^-3) = moles ÷ volume (dm^3). Divide cm^3 by 1000 to get dm^3.
Define percentage yield. ~~ (Actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%.
What is ionic bonding? ~~ Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice.
What is a dative (coordinate) covalent bond? ~~ A covalent bond in which both shared electrons come from the same atom, e.g. in NH4+ or H3O+.
What is metallic bonding? ~~ Electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
State the shapes and bond angles for 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 electron pairs (all bonding). ~~ Linear 180°; trigonal planar 120°; tetrahedral 109.5°; trigonal bipyramidal 90° and 120°; octahedral 90°.
Why is the bond angle in H2O (104.5°) smaller than in CH4? ~~ H2O has two lone pairs, which repel more strongly than bonding pairs, compressing the H–O–H angle.
Why is graphite a good conductor but diamond is not? ~~ In graphite each C forms 3 bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that can move between layers. In diamond all 4 electrons are used in bonding.
Define standard enthalpy of formation. ~~ The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K).
Define activation energy. ~~ The minimum energy that colliding particles need for a reaction to occur.
Why does increasing concentration increase rate? ~~ There are more particles per unit volume, so collisions happen more often and there are more successful collisions per second.

## P2
Define the half-life of a first-order reaction and state a key property. ~~ The time taken for the concentration of a reactant to halve; for a first-order reaction it is constant.
How is the order of reaction found from a concentration–time graph? ~~ Zero order: straight line. First order: constant half-life. Second order: the half-life increases as concentration falls.
What does a rate–concentration graph look like for zero, first and second order? ~~ Zero: horizontal line. First: straight line through the origin. Second: upward curve (parabola).
How is the rate-determining step linked to the rate equation? ~~ Species in the rate equation (with their orders) appear in the rate-determining step; species not in the rate equation react after it.
How is activation energy found graphically from the Arrhenius equation? ~~ Plot ln k against 1/T: the gradient is −Ea/R.
Define mole fraction and partial pressure. ~~ Mole fraction = moles of gas ÷ total moles of gas. Partial pressure = mole fraction × total pressure.
How does increasing total pressure affect Kp? ~~ It has no effect on Kp; only temperature changes Kp. The equilibrium position may shift, but Kp stays the same.
Define standard enthalpy of hydration. ~~ The enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form 1 mole of aqueous ions.
How is enthalpy of solution related to lattice and hydration enthalpies? ~~ ΔH_solution = −ΔH_lattice formation + ΣΔH_hydration (equivalently lattice dissociation + hydration).
At what temperature does a reaction become feasible? ~~ When ΔG = 0: T = ΔH/ΔS (with ΔS in kJ K^-1 mol^-1 to match ΔH).
What is the ionic product of water, Kw? ~~ Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1.00 × 10^-14 mol^2 dm^-6 at 298 K.
Calculate the pH of 0.050 mol dm^-3 NaOH at 298 K. ~~ [H+] = 1.0 × 10^-14/0.050 = 2.0 × 10^-13, so pH = 12.7.
Describe the standard hydrogen electrode. ~~ Hydrogen gas at 100 kPa bubbled over a platinum electrode in 1.00 mol dm^-3 H+ at 298 K. Its E° is defined as 0.00 V.
Give the half-equations for the lithium-ion cell in outline. ~~ Positive electrode: Li+ + CoO2 + e− → Li+[CoO2]−. Negative electrode: Li → Li+ + e−. The reactions are reversed on recharging.

## I1
Why does first ionisation energy generally increase across Period 3? ~~ Nuclear charge increases while shielding stays similar, so outer electrons are held more strongly.
Why does silicon have a very high melting point? ~~ It has a giant covalent (macromolecular) structure, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
Why does sulfur (S8) have a higher melting point than phosphorus (P4)? ~~ S8 molecules are larger with more electrons, so van der Waals forces between them are stronger.
Why does first ionisation energy decrease down Group 2? ~~ The outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded, so less energy is needed to remove them.
Give the equation for calcium reacting with water. ~~ Ca + 2H2O → Ca(OH)2 + H2.
Give a use of magnesium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide. ~~ Mg(OH)2: an antacid to neutralise excess stomach acid. Ca(OH)2: slaked lime, used to neutralise acidic soils.
Why is barium sulfate used in barium meals despite barium ions being toxic? ~~ BaSO4 is insoluble, so it isn't absorbed into the blood; it absorbs X-rays, outlining the gut.
How is titanium extracted using magnesium? ~~ TiO2 is converted to TiCl4, which is reduced by Mg at high temperature in an argon atmosphere: TiCl4 + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl2.
How is CaO used to remove SO2 from flue gases? ~~ CaO (or CaCO3) reacts with SO2 to form calcium sulfite: CaO + SO2 → CaSO3.
Why does electronegativity decrease down Group 7? ~~ The atomic radius increases and shielding increases, so the attraction of the nucleus for bonding electrons decreases.
Why do boiling points increase down Group 7? ~~ Molecules get larger with more electrons, so van der Waals forces are stronger.
Give the products of NaBr with concentrated sulfuric acid. ~~ HBr (steamy fumes), then Br2 (brown fumes) and SO2, as Br− reduces sulfuric acid from +6 to +4.
Give the equation for chlorine reacting with water. ~~ Cl2 + H2O ⇌ HCl + HClO (a disproportionation; in sunlight 2Cl2 + 2H2O → 4HCl + O2).
What is the test for hydroxide ions? ~~ Add a few drops of red litmus or universal indicator; it turns blue or purple. Warming with NH4+ also gives ammonia gas.
How are carbonate ions tested for? ~~ Add dilute acid; effervescence gives CO2, which turns limewater cloudy.

## I2
Give the equations for Na2O and MgO with water and the pH values. ~~ Na2O + H2O → 2NaOH (pH about 14). MgO + H2O → Mg(OH)2 (pH about 9, as it is only slightly soluble).
Give the equation for P4O10 reacting with water. ~~ P4O10 + 6H2O → 4H3PO4 (pH about 0).
Why is Al2O3 described as amphoteric? ~~ It reacts with both acids and bases: with HCl to form AlCl3, and with NaOH to form Na[Al(OH)4].
Give the equation for SiO2 reacting with NaOH. ~~ SiO2 + 2NaOH → Na2SiO3 + H2O (SiO2 is acidic but insoluble in water).
Give four characteristic properties of transition metals. ~~ Variable oxidation states, coloured ions, catalytic activity, and the ability to form complex ions.
What is the shape of [Ag(NH3)2]+ and give its use? ~~ Linear (180°). It is Tollens' reagent, used to test for aldehydes.
What is the geometry of cisplatin? ~~ Square planar, with two Cl− and two NH3 ligands cis to each other.
How is the colour of a complex ion determined? ~~ Ligands split the d orbitals; electrons absorb light of energy ΔE = hν to move between them, and the complementary colour is seen.
State three factors that change the colour of a transition metal complex. ~~ Changing the oxidation state, the ligand, or the coordination number (and so the shape).
What is a homogeneous catalyst and give an example? ~~ A catalyst in the same phase as the reactants, e.g. Fe2+ in the reaction between I− and S2O8^2−.
How does Fe2+ catalyse the reaction of I− and S2O8^2−? ~~ Fe2+ reduces S2O8^2− to SO4^2−, forming Fe3+; Fe3+ then oxidises I− to I2, regenerating Fe2+. Both steps involve oppositely charged ions.
What is autocatalysis? Give an example. ~~ A reaction catalysed by one of its products, e.g. Mn2+ catalysing the reaction of MnO4− with C2O4^2−.
Give the colour and formula of the precipitate when NaOH is added to Fe2+(aq) and Fe3+(aq). ~~ Fe(OH)2(H2O)4: green precipitate (darkens on standing). Fe(OH)3(H2O)3: brown/orange precipitate.
What happens when Na2CO3 is added to [Al(H2O)6]3+? ~~ A white precipitate of Al(OH)3(H2O)3 forms with effervescence (CO2), because the 3+ ion is acidic enough to release CO2.
How is manganate(VII) titration used to find the concentration of Fe2+? ~~ Titrate acidified Fe2+ with KMnO4 (self-indicating: the endpoint is the first permanent pink). MnO4− + 8H+ + 5Fe2+ → Mn2+ + 4H2O + 5Fe3+.

## O1
What is a functional group? ~~ An atom or group of atoms that gives a compound its characteristic chemical properties.
What is chain isomerism? ~~ Isomers with the same molecular formula but different carbon skeletons (branching).
What is position isomerism? ~~ Isomers with the same carbon skeleton and functional group, but with the functional group in a different position.
What is fractional distillation of crude oil? ~~ Separating crude oil into fractions by boiling point in a column that is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top.
Compare thermal and catalytic cracking. ~~ Thermal: high temperature and pressure, producing alkenes. Catalytic: zeolite catalyst at a slight pressure, producing branched alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatics for fuels.
What pollutants are formed from burning alkanes, and how are they removed? ~~ CO, NOx and unburnt hydrocarbons, removed by a catalytic converter (Pt/Rh). SO2 is removed by flue gas desulfurisation.
Why do CFCs damage the ozone layer? ~~ UV light breaks C–Cl bonds, forming Cl radicals, which catalyse the breakdown of O3: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2; ClO• + O3 → 2O2 + Cl•.
Give the reagent and conditions to form a nitrile from a halogenoalkane. ~~ KCN in ethanol and water, heated under reflux (nucleophilic substitution, adding a carbon).
Give the reagent and conditions to form an amine from a halogenoalkane. ~~ Excess ethanolic ammonia, heated under pressure (nucleophilic substitution).
Describe the mechanism of electrophilic addition of Br2 to ethene. ~~ The π bond attacks Br (the Br–Br bond is polarised); a carbocation and Br− form; Br− attacks the carbocation to give 1,2-dibromoethane.
How is ethanol produced industrially by hydration of ethene? ~~ Ethene and steam over a phosphoric acid catalyst at about 300 °C and 60–70 atm.
What are the advantages and disadvantages of producing ethanol by fermentation? ~~ Advantages: renewable, low energy, can be called carbon neutral. Disadvantages: slow, batch process, impure product, uses land.
How is an alcohol dehydrated to an alkene? ~~ Heat with concentrated phosphoric or sulfuric acid catalyst (elimination).
What are the fingerprint region and its use in IR? ~~ Below 1500 cm^-1; its complex pattern is unique to each molecule, so comparing with a database identifies it.
What is high-resolution mass spectrometry used for? ~~ Measuring M_r to four or more decimal places to tell apart molecules with the same nominal mass but different molecular formulae.

## O2
What is optical isomerism and what feature causes it? ~~ Stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images, caused by a chiral carbon with four different groups.
What is a racemic mixture and why is it optically inactive? ~~ A 50:50 mixture of two enantiomers. Their opposite rotations of plane-polarised light cancel.
Why does nucleophilic addition of HCN to an aldehyde give a racemic mixture? ~~ The C=O is planar, so CN− can attack from either side with equal probability.
How is an acyl chloride reacted with ammonia and what is formed? ~~ Nucleophilic addition–elimination at room temperature gives an amide and HCl (which reacts with more NH3 to form NH4Cl).
Why are acid anhydrides preferred to acyl chlorides in the manufacture of aspirin? ~~ They are cheaper, less corrosive, react less violently with water, and don't produce corrosive HCl.
Give the conditions for acid and base hydrolysis of esters. ~~ Acid: heat under reflux with dilute acid (reversible). Base: heat under reflux with NaOH, giving a carboxylate salt and alcohol (goes to completion).
How is biodiesel made? ~~ Transesterification of vegetable oils (triglycerides) with methanol, using a KOH catalyst.
Describe the mechanism for nitration of benzene. ~~ NO2+ (from conc. HNO3 and H2SO4 at 50 °C) is attacked by the benzene π system, giving an intermediate that loses H+ to restore aromaticity.
How is an amine formed by reducing a nitrile? ~~ With LiAlH4 in dry ether, or with H2 and a Ni catalyst: RCN + 4[H] → RCH2NH2.
Why are quaternary ammonium salts useful? ~~ As cationic surfactants in fabric softeners and hair conditioners.
What is the structure of a condensation polyamide such as nylon-6,6? ~~ Formed from a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid (or diacyl chloride), with amide links (–CONH–) and water (or HCl) lost.
Why is Kevlar so strong? ~~ Its rigid, linear chains of benzene rings are held together by many hydrogen bonds between chains.
Describe the primary, secondary and tertiary structure of proteins. ~~ Primary: sequence of amino acids. Secondary: α-helix or β-pleated sheet held by H bonds. Tertiary: 3D folding held by H bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges.
How does 13C NMR differ from 1H NMR? ~~ 13C NMR gives one peak per carbon environment, with no splitting, over a range of about 0–220 ppm.
Describe column chromatography. ~~ A column is packed with a stationary phase (silica or alumina); a solvent (mobile phase) carries the mixture through, and components separate by relative affinity.
