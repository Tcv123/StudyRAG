## DF
Define standard enthalpy of formation. ~~ The enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions.
Why are standard enthalpies of formation of elements zero? ~~ By definition, no change occurs in forming an element from itself in its standard state.
Give the calorimetry equation for energy transferred. ~~ q = mcΔT, where m is the mass of water heated, c is 4.18 J g^-1 K^-1, and ΔT is the temperature change.
What is bond enthalpy? ~~ The energy needed to break 1 mole of a bond in gaseous molecules. Bond breaking is endothermic; bond making is exothermic.
Why is octane rating important for petrol? ~~ A higher octane number means less auto-ignition (knocking); branched alkanes, cycloalkanes and arenes have higher octane numbers.
What is reforming? ~~ Converting straight-chain alkanes into branched alkanes, cycloalkanes and arenes (using a Pt catalyst) to improve octane number.
What is isomerisation in fuel production? ~~ Heating straight-chain alkanes with a catalyst to produce branched isomers.
What is cracking and why is it done? ~~ Breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter alkanes and alkenes; it matches supply to demand and produces alkenes for polymers.
Name the pollutants from car engines and their harmful effects. ~~ CO (toxic), NOx (acid rain, photochemical smog), unburnt hydrocarbons (smog), particulates (respiratory problems), CO2 (climate change).
How does a catalytic converter work? ~~ Pt/Rh on a honeycomb: CO and hydrocarbons are oxidised to CO2 and H2O, and NOx is reduced to N2.
What is catalyst poisoning? ~~ Impurities (e.g. lead) bond strongly to the active sites of a heterogeneous catalyst and block them.
How is a structural isomer different from a stereoisomer? ~~ Structural isomers have different atom connectivity; stereoisomers have the same connectivity but a different spatial arrangement.
What are the shapes around carbon in alkanes and alkenes? ~~ Tetrahedral (109.5°) around sp3 carbon; trigonal planar (120°) around the C=C carbons.
What is the difference between a σ and a π bond? ~~ σ: direct overlap on the internuclear axis. π: sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the axis; weaker and more reactive.
Why are alkenes more reactive than alkanes? ~~ The π bond is weaker and has high electron density exposed above and below the plane, attracting electrophiles.
Describe the mechanism of electrophilic addition of bromine to ethene. ~~ The π electrons attack δ+ Br; a carbocation and Br− form; Br− attacks to form 1,2-dibromoethane.
What are the benefits and drawbacks of hydrogen as a fuel? ~~ Benefits: only produces water and has a high energy per mass. Drawbacks: energy is needed to produce it, and storage and transport are difficult.
What is meant by a carbon-neutral fuel? ~~ A fuel whose combustion releases the same amount of CO2 as was absorbed in producing it (e.g. bioethanol, in theory).

## ES
What is the redox reaction in iodine–thiosulfate titrations used for? ~~ To find the concentration of an oxidising agent: the agent releases I2 from excess I−, and the I2 is titrated with thiosulfate.
Why is starch added near the end point? ~~ Adding it too early lets it form an insoluble complex with lots of iodine; near the end point the blue-black to colourless change is sharp.
How is bromine extracted from sea water? ~~ Acidify and oxidise Br− with Cl2; blow out Br2 with air; react with SO2 to concentrate as HBr; oxidise again with Cl2 and distil.
What is the hazard of chlorine gas? ~~ It is toxic and irritates the respiratory system. Handle it in a fume cupboard.
Give a use of sodium chlorate(I). ~~ Household bleach and disinfecting water.
Why is hydrogen chloride soluble in water and acidic? ~~ It reacts with water to form H3O+ and Cl− ions (fully dissociates), giving hydrochloric acid.
What does "oxidising agent" mean? ~~ A species that accepts electrons and is itself reduced.
Why are the hydrogen halides more easily oxidised down the group? ~~ The H–X bond gets weaker down the group and larger halide ions lose electrons more easily, so HI and HBr are stronger reducing agents.

## OZ
What is the difference between UV-A, UV-B and UV-C? ~~ UV-C has the highest energy and is completely absorbed by O2 and O3. UV-B damages DNA and is mostly absorbed by ozone. UV-A has the lowest energy and mostly reaches the ground.
Why does ozone absorb UV radiation? ~~ UV photons have enough energy to break the O–O bond in O3, forming O2 and O atoms.
What is the relationship between photon energy and frequency? ~~ E = hν.
How do nitrogen oxides destroy ozone? ~~ NO• + O3 → NO2• + O2, then NO2• + O → NO• + O2; NO acts as a catalyst.
Why is the rate of ozone destruction by Cl radicals so high? ~~ Cl• is regenerated each cycle, so one radical can destroy thousands of ozone molecules before termination.
What are the effects of increased UV reaching Earth? ~~ More skin cancer, cataracts and sunburn, and damage to crops and plankton.
Define activation enthalpy. ~~ The minimum energy colliding particles need to react.
How does concentration affect the rate of reaction? ~~ Higher concentration means more frequent collisions and so more successful collisions per unit time.
What is the effect of pressure on gaseous reaction rates? ~~ Higher pressure increases concentration, so collisions are more frequent.
How is a halogenoalkane converted into an amine? ~~ Heat it with excess concentrated ammonia in ethanol in a sealed tube.
What is a nucleophile? ~~ A species with a lone pair of electrons that attacks electron-deficient (δ+) carbon atoms.
Why does the C–F bond make HFCs unreactive? ~~ The C–F bond is very strong, so it is not broken by UV in the stratosphere.
What is a greenhouse gas and why are HFCs a concern? ~~ A gas that absorbs infrared radiation from Earth. HFCs are powerful greenhouse gases, even though they don't damage ozone.
Why do haloalkane boiling points increase with halogen size? ~~ Larger halogens have more electrons, so London forces between molecules are stronger.

## WM
How is an aldehyde distinguished from a ketone using Fehling's solution? ~~ Warm with Fehling's: an aldehyde gives a brick-red precipitate (Cu2O); a ketone gives no change (stays blue).
What reagent tests for a carboxylic acid? ~~ Sodium carbonate or hydrogencarbonate: effervescence of CO2.
How are esters hydrolysed? ~~ Heat under reflux with dilute acid (reversible) or NaOH (gives a carboxylate salt and alcohol).
What is reflux used for? ~~ Heating a reaction mixture for a long time without losing volatile components, which condense and return to the flask.
How can thin-layer chromatography monitor a reaction? ~~ Spot samples of the reaction mixture at intervals alongside the reactant; the reaction is complete when the reactant spot disappears.
What is the base peak in a mass spectrum? ~~ The tallest peak, representing the most abundant (most stable) fragment ion.
What IR absorption identifies an O–H in an alcohol? ~~ A broad absorption at about 3200–3600 cm^-1.
Why is aspirin a modified natural product? ~~ Salicylic acid from willow bark was irritating to the stomach; acetylating the phenol –OH gave aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), which is less irritating.
What is the purpose of a melting point test for aspirin? ~~ To check purity: pure aspirin melts sharply at about 136 °C; impurities lower and broaden the range.

## CI
What factors are considered when siting a chemical plant? ~~ Raw material supply, transport links, workforce, energy supply, water supply, waste disposal and environmental impact.
Why are many industrial processes run as continuous rather than batch? ~~ Continuous processes give a constant output, are easier to automate, and have lower running costs for large volumes.
How is heat energy recovered in industry? ~~ Heat exchangers use heat from exothermic reactions to preheat reactants or generate steam.
What are the costs of a chemical process? ~~ Fixed costs (plant, labour) and variable costs (raw materials, energy, waste treatment).
Give the equation and conditions for the Haber process. ~~ N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, ΔH = −92 kJ mol^-1. Iron catalyst, about 450 °C, about 200 atm.
Why is ammonia important? ~~ It is used to make fertilisers (e.g. ammonium nitrate), nitric acid, explosives and nylon.
What is the environmental problem with nitrate fertilisers? ~~ Run-off into rivers causes eutrophication: algal blooms deplete oxygen, killing aquatic life.
How does temperature affect Kc for an exothermic reaction? ~~ Increasing temperature decreases Kc; the equilibrium shifts in the endothermic (reverse) direction.
How do you calculate the rate of reaction from a graph? ~~ Draw a tangent at the chosen time and find its gradient (change in concentration ÷ change in time).

## O
Define lattice enthalpy (formation). ~~ The enthalpy change when 1 mole of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions.
Define enthalpy change of solution. ~~ The enthalpy change when 1 mole of solute dissolves completely in water to form an infinitely dilute solution.
How is enthalpy of solution calculated from lattice and hydration enthalpies? ~~ ΔH_solution = −ΔH_lattice + ΣΔH_hydration.
What factors affect hydration enthalpy? ~~ Smaller ions with higher charge have higher charge density, so hydration enthalpy is more exothermic.
Why do ions dissolve in water? ~~ Water molecules are polar: δ− O is attracted to cations and δ+ H to anions, releasing hydration energy.
What is the total entropy change and when is a process spontaneous? ~~ ΔS_total = ΔS_sys + ΔS_surr, where ΔS_surr = −ΔH/T. The process is spontaneous if ΔS_total > 0.
Why does CO2 dissolve in oceans and how does it form an acid? ~~ CO2(aq) + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−, which lowers ocean pH.
Why is ocean acidification a problem for marine life? ~~ The higher [H+] reacts with carbonate ions, reducing their availability for shells and coral, and can dissolve CaCO3.
What is the solubility product Ksp? ~~ For a sparingly soluble salt AB: Ksp = [A+][B−] in a saturated solution. It is used to predict precipitation.
What is the effect of temperature on the solubility of gases in water? ~~ Gases are less soluble at higher temperatures, which is why warmer oceans absorb less CO2.
What is the ionic product of water? ~~ Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10^-14 mol^2 dm^-6 at 298 K.
Calculate the pH of a 0.010 mol dm^-3 NaOH solution. ~~ [H+] = 10^-14/0.010 = 10^-12, so pH = 12.
How is the pH of a buffer calculated? ~~ [H+] = Ka × [HA]/[A−], then pH = −log[H+].
Why do buffers only work over a limited range? ~~ Once most of the HA or A− is used up, the buffer can no longer absorb added acid or base.
What is the greenhouse effect? ~~ Greenhouse gases absorb IR radiated by the Earth, causing bond vibrations, and re-emit it in all directions, warming the lower atmosphere.

## DM
What is a half-cell? ~~ An element in contact with a solution of its ions (or two ions of the same element with a Pt electrode), which produces an electrode potential.
Why is platinum used in some half-cells? ~~ It is inert, conducts electricity, and provides a surface for reactions where no solid metal is involved (e.g. Fe3+/Fe2+).
What are standard conditions for electrode potentials? ~~ 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm^-3 solutions.
What is the effect of increasing ion concentration on electrode potential? ~~ The equilibrium Mn+ + ne− ⇌ M shifts right, so E becomes more positive.
How does rusting occur electrochemically? ~~ Fe → Fe2+ + 2e− (anode); O2 + 2H2O + 4e− → 4OH− (cathode). Fe(OH)2 forms and is oxidised to hydrated Fe2O3 (rust).
How can rusting be prevented? ~~ Barrier methods (paint, oil, plastic) or sacrificial protection (zinc, as in galvanising), where the more reactive metal is oxidised instead.
What is a ligand? ~~ A molecule or ion with a lone pair that forms a dative covalent bond to a central metal ion.
Define coordination number. ~~ The number of dative bonds from ligands to the central metal ion.
What is a complex ion? ~~ A central metal ion surrounded by ligands bonded by dative covalent bonds.
Why do different ligands produce different colours? ~~ Different ligands split the d orbitals by different amounts, changing the frequency of light absorbed.
What is observed when concentrated HCl is added to [Cu(H2O)6]2+? ~~ The blue solution turns yellow-green as [CuCl4]2− forms (a tetrahedral complex).
What is the precipitate when NaOH is added to Cu2+? ~~ Pale blue Cu(OH)2 (or [Cu(OH)2(H2O)4]).
Why is Zn not a transition metal? ~~ Its only ion, Zn2+, has a full 3d10 sub-shell (no incomplete d sub-shell).
How is a calibration curve used in colorimetry? ~~ Measure the absorbance of standard solutions of known concentration, plot absorbance against concentration, then read off the unknown's concentration.
Why is a filter chosen in colorimetry? ~~ The filter selects the complementary colour, which the solution absorbs most strongly, giving the greatest sensitivity.
Why do transition metals act as catalysts? ~~ They can change oxidation state easily and provide surfaces for adsorption of reactants.
What is the half-equation for dichromate(VI) acting as an oxidising agent? ~~ Cr2O7^2− + 14H+ + 6e− → 2Cr3+ + 7H2O (orange to green).

## CD
What is a chromophore? ~~ The part of a molecule responsible for its colour, usually an extended delocalised system such as –N=N– linking aromatic rings.
What are auxochromes? ~~ Groups (e.g. –OH, –NH2) attached to a chromophore that modify the colour and intensity and can improve solubility.
Why must diazotisation be done below 10 °C? ~~ Diazonium salts are unstable and decompose above about 10 °C, releasing N2.
What is a coupling agent in azo dye synthesis? ~~ A phenol (in alkaline solution) or an aromatic amine that reacts with the diazonium ion by electrophilic substitution.
What is a triglyceride? ~~ An ester formed from glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) and three fatty acids.
What is hydrogenation of unsaturated oils? ~~ Adding H2 across C=C bonds with a nickel catalyst to form more saturated fats (e.g. margarine).
What is the difference between cis and trans fatty acids? ~~ Cis: chains bend at the C=C, so they pack poorly (liquid). Trans: straighter chains pack closely; linked to heart disease.
How does Friedel–Crafts acylation modify benzene? ~~ An acyl chloride with an AlCl3 catalyst forms an acylium ion (RCO+) electrophile, giving an aromatic ketone.
How does gas chromatography separate compounds? ~~ Compounds are carried by an inert gas through a column; they separate by their different solubility in the stationary liquid phase, giving different retention times.
What is retention time? ~~ The time from injection to a compound reaching the detector in GC or HPLC.
How can GC be used to find relative amounts of components? ~~ The area under each peak is proportional to the amount of that component.
What is the mechanism of reduction of aldehydes with NaBH4? ~~ Nucleophilic addition: H− attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon, and the O− is then protonated by water to form the alcohol.
Why do aromatic compounds with more delocalisation absorb longer wavelengths? ~~ A larger delocalised system means the energy gap between orbitals is smaller, so lower-energy (longer-wavelength) visible light is absorbed.
What does the term "hydrolysis of a fat" produce under alkaline conditions? ~~ Glycerol and the sodium salts of the fatty acids (soap), a reaction called saponification.
