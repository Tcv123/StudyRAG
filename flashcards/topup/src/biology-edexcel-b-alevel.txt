## 1
What is a glycosidic bond? ~~ A covalent bond between two monosaccharides, formed by condensation with the loss of water.
Why is glycogen more branched than amylopectin? ~~ More branches give more ends for enzymes to act on, so glucose can be released quickly to meet animals' high energy demands.
What is an ester bond? ~~ The bond formed between the –OH of glycerol and the –COOH of a fatty acid in a condensation reaction.
Distinguish saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. ~~ Saturated: no C=C double bonds, straight chains. Unsaturated: one or more C=C bonds, causing kinks in the chain.
What is the primary structure of a protein? ~~ The sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain, held by peptide bonds.
What is the secondary structure of a protein? ~~ Folding of the polypeptide into α-helices or β-pleated sheets, held by hydrogen bonds.
What is the role of disulfide bridges? ~~ Strong covalent bonds between sulfur atoms of cysteine R groups, stabilising the tertiary structure.
Describe the structure of a DNA nucleotide. ~~ A deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base (adenine, thymine, cytosine or guanine).
What is a phosphodiester bond? ~~ The bond linking the phosphate of one nucleotide to the sugar of the next, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone.
What is the role of mRNA in protein synthesis? ~~ It carries a complementary copy of the gene's base sequence from the nucleus to the ribosome.
How does a non-competitive inhibitor work? ~~ It binds to an allosteric site, changing the shape of the active site so the substrate no longer fits. Adding more substrate doesn't reverse it.
What is end-product inhibition? ~~ The final product of a metabolic pathway inhibits an enzyme earlier in the pathway, regulating production.

## 2
What is the role of ribosomes? ~~ The site of translation (protein synthesis). 80S in eukaryotes, 70S in prokaryotes.
What is the role of the cell wall in plants? ~~ It provides support and strength and prevents the cell bursting when turgid; it is made of cellulose microfibrils.
What is the role of the capsule in bacteria? ~~ A slimy layer that protects against phagocytosis and desiccation, and helps bacteria stick to surfaces.
What is the difference between Gram-positive and Gram-negative cell walls? ~~ Gram-positive: thick peptidoglycan layer, stains purple. Gram-negative: thin peptidoglycan with an outer lipid membrane, stains red/pink.
What is a bacteriophage? ~~ A virus that infects bacteria.
What is a retrovirus? ~~ An RNA virus that uses reverse transcriptase to make DNA from its RNA, which is inserted into the host genome, e.g. HIV.
What happens during prophase of mitosis? ~~ Chromosomes condense and become visible, the nuclear envelope breaks down, and spindle fibres form.
What happens during metaphase of mitosis? ~~ Chromosomes line up at the equator and attach to spindle fibres by their centromeres.
What happens during telophase of mitosis? ~~ Chromatids reach the poles, nuclear envelopes reform and chromosomes uncoil. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm.
What is the mitotic index? ~~ The proportion of cells in a sample undergoing mitosis: cells in mitosis ÷ total cells.
What happens in the acrosome reaction? ~~ When sperm meet the zona pellucida, the acrosome releases digestive enzymes that allow the sperm to penetrate it.
What is the endosperm? ~~ A triploid tissue formed when a male nucleus fuses with the two polar nuclei; it provides food for the embryo.

## 3
What is phylogeny? ~~ The evolutionary history and relationships of organisms.
What is a cladogram? ~~ A branching diagram showing evolutionary relationships based on shared derived characteristics.
Why do molecular data give better classification than physical features? ~~ Physical features can be similar due to convergent evolution; DNA and protein sequences give more objective evidence of relatedness.
What is ring species? ~~ Populations where neighbouring groups can interbreed, but those at the ends of the ring cannot, showing speciation in progress.
What are the domains Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya? ~~ Archaea: prokaryotes with unique membranes, often in extreme environments. Bacteria: prokaryotes with peptidoglycan cell walls. Eukarya: cells with a nucleus.
What is a behavioural adaptation? ~~ A way an organism acts to increase survival, e.g. birds migrating.
What is a physiological adaptation? ~~ An internal process that increases survival, e.g. hibernation or producing venom.
What is an anatomical adaptation? ~~ A structural feature that increases survival, e.g. a thick blubber layer in whales.
What is speciation? ~~ The formation of new species when populations become reproductively isolated and diverge genetically.
Give two types of reproductive isolation. ~~ Prezygotic (e.g. different mating seasons or courtship behaviour) and postzygotic (e.g. hybrid sterility).
What is endemism? ~~ A species found only in one particular geographic location.
What is biodiversity? ~~ The variety of living organisms: species diversity, genetic diversity and habitat diversity.

## 4
How does the structure of the alveolus maximise gas exchange? ~~ Large surface area, thin walls (one cell thick), moist lining, good blood supply and ventilation maintain steep concentration gradients.
What is the role of surfactant? ~~ It reduces surface tension in the alveoli, preventing them collapsing during exhalation.
How does counter-current flow in fish gills work? ~~ Blood and water flow in opposite directions, maintaining a concentration gradient along the whole gill lamella.
What is the role of the sinoatrial node? ~~ It generates electrical impulses that start each heartbeat, making the atria contract.
Why is there a delay at the atrioventricular node? ~~ It gives the atria time to empty into the ventricles before the ventricles contract.
What is the role of the Purkyne fibres? ~~ They carry impulses from the bundle of His to the ventricle walls, so the ventricles contract from the apex upwards.
What happens to the oxygen dissociation curve during exercise? ~~ It shifts to the right (the Bohr effect) as CO2 increases, so haemoglobin releases more oxygen to muscles.
Why does fetal haemoglobin have a higher affinity for oxygen? ~~ It can load oxygen from the mother's blood in the placenta, where the partial pressure of oxygen is low.
What is transpiration? ~~ The loss of water vapour from the leaves, mainly through the stomata.
How does cohesion–tension move water up the xylem? ~~ Water evaporates from leaves, pulling water molecules up because hydrogen bonds hold them together (cohesion); they also stick to xylem walls (adhesion).
What is translocation? ~~ The movement of organic substances such as sucrose through the phloem from sources to sinks.
How are companion cells adapted? ~~ They have many mitochondria for active transport of sucrose into the sieve tubes.

## 5
What is the role of NAD in respiration? ~~ It accepts hydrogen (becoming reduced NAD) and carries it to the electron transport chain.
What happens to pyruvate in the absence of oxygen in animals? ~~ It is reduced to lactate by reduced NAD, regenerating NAD so glycolysis can continue.
What is substrate-level phosphorylation? ~~ ATP produced directly from a phosphorylated intermediate transferring a phosphate to ADP, e.g. in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle.
What is the respiratory quotient? ~~ RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 consumed. Carbohydrates give about 1.0, lipids about 0.7 and proteins about 0.9.
Why do mitochondria have folded inner membranes (cristae)? ~~ To increase the surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthase.
What is photolysis of water? ~~ The splitting of water by light energy into protons, electrons and oxygen.
What is the role of photosystems I and II? ~~ They contain chlorophyll that absorbs light, exciting electrons. PSII feeds electrons to the chain; PSI re-excites them to reduce NADP.
What is the role of RuBisCO? ~~ It catalyses the combination of CO2 with RuBP to form two molecules of GP.
Why does the rate of photosynthesis plateau at high light intensity? ~~ Another factor, such as CO2 concentration or temperature, becomes limiting.
What happens in the thylakoid membranes? ~~ The light-dependent reactions: light absorption, photolysis, electron transport, and ATP and reduced NADP production.
What happens in the stroma? ~~ The Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions), where CO2 is fixed and reduced to make GALP.
What is the Hill reaction? ~~ Isolated chloroplasts reduce an artificial electron acceptor (e.g. DCPIP, blue to colourless) in light, showing the light-dependent reaction.

## 6
What is a nutrient medium? ~~ A substance containing the nutrients needed for microorganisms to grow, e.g. nutrient agar or broth.
What are the phases of a bacterial growth curve? ~~ Lag, log (exponential), stationary and death (decline).
Why do bacteria grow slowly in the lag phase? ~~ They are adapting to the new environment, synthesising enzymes and taking in water, before dividing.
Why does growth stop in the stationary phase? ~~ Nutrients run out and toxic waste builds up, so the birth rate equals the death rate.
What is a haemocytometer used for? ~~ Counting cells (total count) in a known volume under a microscope.
How does turbidimetry measure bacterial growth? ~~ A colorimeter measures cloudiness (absorbance) of a culture, which increases with the number of cells (both living and dead).
What is a pathogen? ~~ A microorganism that causes disease.
How does a vector transmit disease? ~~ An organism (e.g. a mosquito) carries a pathogen from one host to another without being affected.
What is the role of phagocytes in the immune response? ~~ They engulf and digest pathogens and present antigens to T cells.
What is antigen presentation? ~~ Phagocytes display a pathogen's antigens on their surface, activating T helper cells.
What is the role of T helper cells? ~~ They release cytokines that activate B cells, T killer cells and phagocytes.
What do plasma cells do? ~~ They secrete large amounts of antibodies specific to one antigen.

## 7
What is a DNA probe? ~~ A short, single strand of labelled DNA complementary to a target sequence, used to find specific genes.
What is gel electrophoresis? ~~ A method that separates DNA fragments by size using an electric current; smaller fragments move further towards the positive electrode.
What is the role of primers in PCR? ~~ Short DNA sequences complementary to the start of the target region, providing a starting point for DNA polymerase.
Why is PCR carried out at 95 °C, 55 °C and 72 °C? ~~ 95 °C separates the strands; 55 °C lets primers anneal; 72 °C is the optimum for Taq polymerase to extend.
What is a promoter region? ~~ A DNA sequence upstream of a gene where RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind to start transcription.
How can genetic modification improve crop yields? ~~ Introducing genes for pest resistance, herbicide tolerance or drought resistance.
What are the ethical concerns about GMOs? ~~ Possible effects on the environment (gene transfer to wild plants), effects on biodiversity, corporate control of seeds, and long-term health effects.
What is a vector in genetic engineering? ~~ A carrier, such as a plasmid or virus, used to transfer DNA into a host cell.
What is the role of marker genes? ~~ They identify cells that have taken up recombinant DNA, e.g. antibiotic resistance or fluorescence genes.
What are stem cells used for in medicine? ~~ Replacing damaged cells or tissues, e.g. treating Parkinson's disease, spinal cord injury and type 1 diabetes.
Why are embryonic stem cells controversial? ~~ Obtaining them destroys an embryo, which some people consider a potential human life.
What is a transgenic organism? ~~ An organism containing a gene from a different species.

## 8
What is a mutation? ~~ A change in the base sequence of DNA.
What is a point mutation? ~~ A change in a single base: a substitution, insertion or deletion.
What is a chromosome mutation? ~~ A change in the structure or number of chromosomes, e.g. deletion, duplication or translocation.
What is dominant and recessive? ~~ Dominant: an allele always expressed in the phenotype if present. Recessive: expressed only when two copies are present.
What is a heterozygote advantage? ~~ When heterozygotes have a survival advantage, e.g. sickle cell carriers are more resistant to malaria.
What is epistasis? ~~ When one gene affects or masks the expression of another gene.
What is sex determination in humans? ~~ Females are XX, males are XY. The SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers male development.
Why are males more likely to show X-linked recessive conditions? ~~ They have only one X chromosome, so a single recessive allele is expressed.
What is directional selection? ~~ Selection that favours one extreme phenotype, shifting the mean, e.g. antibiotic resistance.
What is allele frequency? ~~ The proportion of a particular allele in a population's gene pool.
How does genetic drift differ from natural selection? ~~ Genetic drift is random and not related to fitness; natural selection is non-random, favouring advantageous alleles.
What are the Hardy–Weinberg equations? ~~ p + q = 1 and p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1.

## 9
What are the components of a control system? ~~ A receptor, a coordinator (e.g. the brain) and an effector.
What is the role of ADH? ~~ Released from the posterior pituitary when blood water potential is low; it increases the permeability of the collecting duct to water, so more water is reabsorbed.
What is ultrafiltration in the kidney? ~~ High blood pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules (water, glucose, urea, ions) through the capillary walls into the Bowman's capsule.
What is selective reabsorption? ~~ The reabsorption of useful substances, such as glucose, amino acids and some water, back into the blood from the proximal convoluted tubule.
Why is the loop of Henle longer in desert animals? ~~ A longer loop creates a steeper water potential gradient in the medulla, so more water is reabsorbed and urine is more concentrated.
What are the roles of the pancreas in blood glucose control? ~~ β cells release insulin when glucose is high; α cells release glucagon when glucose is low.
What is the difference between the nervous and endocrine systems? ~~ Nervous: fast, short-lived, localised electrical impulses. Endocrine: slower, longer-lasting, widespread chemical hormones.
What is a steroid hormone and how does it act? ~~ A lipid-soluble hormone that diffuses into the cell and binds to receptors, acting as a transcription factor.
What is the role of the fovea? ~~ The part of the retina with many cones and no rods, giving the highest visual acuity.
What is a reflex arc? ~~ A rapid, involuntary response: receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone → motor neurone → effector.
What is the role of the hypothalamus in temperature control? ~~ It detects changes in blood temperature and coordinates responses such as sweating, shivering and changes in blood flow to the skin.
What is the role of ethylene in plants? ~~ A plant hormone that promotes fruit ripening and leaf abscission.

## 10
What is a food web? ~~ A diagram showing the interconnected feeding relationships between organisms in an ecosystem.
What is productivity? ~~ The rate at which energy is converted into biomass, per unit area per unit time.
What is the role of decomposers? ~~ They break down dead organic matter and waste, releasing nutrients back into the ecosystem.
What is nitrification? ~~ The conversion of ammonium into nitrites and then nitrates by nitrifying bacteria (Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter).
What is denitrification? ~~ Anaerobic bacteria convert nitrates back into nitrogen gas.
What is the carbon cycle? ~~ The movement of carbon through photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition, fossilisation and combustion.
How does deforestation affect the carbon cycle? ~~ It reduces CO2 uptake by photosynthesis, and burning or decaying trees releases CO2.
What is a density-dependent factor? ~~ A factor whose effect depends on population density, e.g. disease, competition and predation.
What is a density-independent factor? ~~ A factor whose effect doesn't depend on population density, e.g. floods, fire and temperature.
What is intraspecific competition? ~~ Competition between members of the same species for resources.
What is the Lincoln index? ~~ The population estimate from mark–recapture: N = (number marked first × total caught second) ÷ number marked in second sample.
What is conservation? ~~ The protection and management of species and habitats to maintain biodiversity.
