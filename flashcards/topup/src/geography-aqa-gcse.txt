## 1.1
What is the structure of the Earth? ~~ Crust, mantle, outer core and inner core.
What is a tectonic plate? ~~ A large section of the Earth's crust that moves slowly on the mantle.
What causes plates to move? ~~ Convection currents in the mantle, and slab pull.
What is a destructive plate margin? ~~ Where an oceanic plate is forced under a continental plate, causing earthquakes and violent volcanoes.
What is a constructive plate margin? ~~ Where plates move apart and magma rises to form new crust, causing gentle volcanoes.
What is a conservative plate margin? ~~ Where plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes but no volcanoes.
What is a collision margin? ~~ Where two continental plates collide, forming fold mountains.
What is the focus of an earthquake? ~~ The point underground where the earthquake starts.
What is the epicentre? ~~ The point on the surface directly above the focus.
How are earthquakes measured? ~~ Using the moment magnitude scale (or Richter scale) and a seismometer.
What is a composite volcano? ~~ A steep-sided volcano made of layers of ash and lava, with explosive eruptions.
What is a shield volcano? ~~ A wide, gently sloping volcano made of runny lava, with gentle eruptions.
What is a hotspot? ~~ An area of volcanic activity away from plate margins, e.g. Hawaii.
What are the primary effects of earthquakes? ~~ Buildings collapse, roads and bridges are damaged, and people are killed or injured.
What are the secondary effects of earthquakes? ~~ Fires, tsunamis, landslides, disease, and homelessness.
What is a tsunami? ~~ A large sea wave caused by an earthquake under the ocean.
What are the primary effects of volcanoes? ~~ Lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash falls and gas emissions.
What are the secondary effects of volcanoes? ~~ Lahars (mudflows), flooding, loss of tourism, and fertile soils in the long term.
Why do people live near volcanoes? ~~ Fertile soils, tourism, geothermal energy and minerals.
How can the effects of earthquakes be reduced? ~~ Earthquake-proof buildings, education, emergency plans, and early warning systems.
What is an earthquake-proof building? ~~ A building designed to withstand shaking, e.g. with deep foundations, cross-bracing and shock absorbers.
How can volcanic eruptions be predicted? ~~ Monitoring ground swelling, gas emissions and small earthquakes.
Why do earthquakes have more impact in poorer countries? ~~ Poor-quality buildings, fewer emergency services, and less preparation.
Give an example of an earthquake in a richer country. ~~ Japan (Tōhoku 2011) or Christchurch, New Zealand (2011).
Give an example of an earthquake in a poorer country. ~~ Nepal (2015) or Haiti (2010).
What is the Pacific Ring of Fire? ~~ A zone around the Pacific Ocean with many volcanoes and earthquakes.
What is a pyroclastic flow? ~~ A fast-moving cloud of hot gas, ash and rock.
What is a lahar? ~~ A mudflow of volcanic material mixed with water.
What is hazard mapping? ~~ Mapping areas at risk from hazards to plan land use and evacuation.
What is the magnitude of an earthquake? ~~ A measure of the energy released by an earthquake.

## 1.2
What is a natural hazard? ~~ A natural process or event that poses a threat to people and property.
What is hazard risk? ~~ The probability or chance that a natural hazard will happen and affect people.
What causes global atmospheric circulation? ~~ Unequal heating of the Earth — the Equator receives more energy than the poles.
What pressure is found at the Equator? ~~ Low pressure, where warm air rises (the ITCZ).
What pressure is found at 30° north and south? ~~ High pressure, where cool air sinks — this is where hot deserts are found.
What are trade winds? ~~ Surface winds blowing from the sub-tropical high (30°) towards the Equator.
What are the westerlies? ~~ Surface winds blowing from 30° towards 60°, bringing mild, wet Atlantic weather to the UK.
What are the three circulation cells in each hemisphere? ~~ The Hadley cell, the Ferrel cell and the Polar cell.
Where do tropical storms form? ~~ Over warm oceans between about 5° and 30° north and south of the Equator.
What sea temperature do tropical storms need? ~~ At least 26–27°C.
Why don't tropical storms form at the Equator? ~~ The Coriolis effect is too weak to make the air spin.
What powers a tropical storm? ~~ Latent heat released as water vapour condenses in rising air.
What is the eye of a tropical storm? ~~ The calm, clear centre with sinking air and the lowest pressure.
What is the eye wall? ~~ The ring of tall cloud around the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
Why do tropical storms weaken over land? ~~ They lose their supply of warm, moist air and are slowed by friction.
What is a storm surge? ~~ A rise in sea level caused by strong winds and low pressure pushing water onshore.
How might climate change affect tropical storm intensity? ~~ More storms are likely to reach the highest categories, with heavier rain.
How might climate change affect tropical storm distribution? ~~ Storms may form further from the tropics as more ocean reaches 26–27°C.
When and where did Typhoon Haiyan strike? ~~ November 2013, the Philippines — especially Tacloban on Leyte.
How many people died in Typhoon Haiyan? ~~ Over 6,300, with around 1,000 more missing.
How high was Haiyan's storm surge? ~~ Up to 5–6 metres in Tacloban.
Give a secondary effect of Typhoon Haiyan. ~~ Food and water shortages led to disease and looting; 6 million people lost their income.
Give an immediate response to Typhoon Haiyan. ~~ 800,000 people evacuated before landfall; international aid from the UK, USA and NGOs.
Give a long-term response to Typhoon Haiyan. ~~ Build Back Better reconstruction, cash-for-work, new cyclone shelters and mangrove replanting.
What are the four ways of reducing tropical storm effects? ~~ Monitoring, prediction, protection and planning.
Give an example of protection against tropical storms. ~~ Raised cyclone shelters in Bangladesh, storm-surge barriers or buildings on stilts.
What caused the Somerset Levels floods of 2013–14? ~~ A series of Atlantic depressions brought the wettest winter on record onto flat, low-lying land.
Give a long-term response to the Somerset Levels floods. ~~ A £100 million Flood Action Plan: 8 km of rivers dredged, pumps upgraded, banks raised.
What is the UK's highest recorded temperature? ~~ 40.3°C at Coningsby, Lincolnshire, on 19 July 2022.
Give evidence that UK weather is becoming more extreme. ~~ All ten warmest years since 2002, record rainfall in 2014 and 2015, and more frequent major floods.

## 1.3
What is climate change? ~~ A long-term change in the Earth's climate, including temperature and rainfall patterns.
What is the greenhouse effect? ~~ Gases in the atmosphere trap heat from the Sun, keeping the Earth warm.
What is the enhanced greenhouse effect? ~~ Human activities increase greenhouse gases, trapping more heat and causing global warming.
Name three greenhouse gases. ~~ Carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide.
What are the human causes of climate change? ~~ Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, farming (methane from cattle), and industry.
What are the natural causes of climate change? ~~ Changes in the Earth's orbit, volcanic eruptions and variations in solar output.
What is the evidence for climate change? ~~ Rising global temperatures, melting ice caps, rising sea levels, and ice core records.
What do ice cores show? ~~ Air bubbles trapped in ice show past levels of CO₂ and temperature.
What are the effects of climate change on the environment? ~~ Rising sea levels, melting ice, more extreme weather, and loss of habitats.
What are the effects of climate change on people? ~~ Flooding, food shortages, water shortages, and health problems.
What is mitigation? ~~ Reducing the causes of climate change, e.g. cutting emissions.
What is adaptation? ~~ Changing how we live to cope with the effects of climate change.
Give examples of mitigation. ~~ Renewable energy, planting trees, carbon capture, and international agreements.
Give examples of adaptation. ~~ Flood defences, drought-resistant crops, and changing farming practices.
What is carbon capture and storage? ~~ Capturing CO₂ from power stations and storing it underground.
What is the Paris Agreement? ~~ A 2015 international agreement to limit global warming to well below 2 °C.
What is a carbon footprint? ~~ The total amount of greenhouse gases produced by a person, organisation or activity.
How can individuals reduce their carbon footprint? ~~ Using public transport, saving energy, recycling, and eating less meat.
How does climate change affect the UK? ~~ Hotter summers, wetter winters, more flooding and rising sea levels.
How does climate change affect poorer countries? ~~ They are often more vulnerable to droughts, floods and storms, with fewer resources to adapt.
What is sea-level rise caused by? ~~ Melting ice sheets and glaciers, and thermal expansion of water.
What is thermal expansion? ~~ Water expanding as it warms, raising sea levels.
What is the Quaternary period? ~~ The last 2.6 million years, with glacial and interglacial periods.
What is an interglacial period? ~~ A warmer period between glacial periods.
How does deforestation contribute to climate change? ~~ Trees absorb CO₂; removing them increases CO₂ in the atmosphere.
What is the role of methane in climate change? ~~ Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas released by cattle, rice farming and landfill.
What is renewable energy? ~~ Energy from sources that won't run out, e.g. wind, solar and hydroelectric power.
What is a climate refugee? ~~ Someone forced to move because of the effects of climate change.
How do rising temperatures affect ecosystems? ~~ Species may move, adapt or become extinct, e.g. coral bleaching.
What is coral bleaching? ~~ Corals expel algae when water is too warm, turning white and possibly dying.

## 1.4
What is an ecosystem? ~~ A community of living things interacting with each other and their non-living environment.
What is a biome? ~~ A large-scale ecosystem, e.g. tropical rainforest or hot desert.
Where are tropical rainforests found? ~~ Near the equator, e.g. the Amazon, Congo Basin and Southeast Asia.
What is the climate of a tropical rainforest? ~~ Hot (about 27 °C) and wet all year, with over 2000 mm of rain.
What are the layers of the rainforest? ~~ Emergent layer, canopy, understorey and forest floor.
What is the canopy? ~~ The dense layer of tree tops that absorbs most sunlight.
What are buttress roots? ~~ Large roots that support tall trees in shallow soils.
What are drip tips? ~~ Pointed leaves that allow heavy rain to run off quickly.
What are lianas? ~~ Woody vines that climb trees to reach sunlight.
What are epiphytes? ~~ Plants that grow on other plants to reach light, e.g. orchids.
How are animals adapted to the rainforest? ~~ E.g. sloths move slowly and are camouflaged; monkeys have prehensile tails; frogs have sticky feet.
What is the nutrient cycle in rainforests? ~~ Nutrients are rapidly recycled from leaf litter to plants, so the soil is poor.
Why is rainforest soil infertile? ~~ Heavy rain washes nutrients away (leaching), and nutrients are held in plants.
What is biodiversity? ~~ The variety of living things in an area.
Why do rainforests have high biodiversity? ~~ Warm, wet climate all year allows plants to grow quickly, providing many habitats.
What is deforestation? ~~ The cutting down or removal of forests.
What are the causes of deforestation? ~~ Logging, cattle ranching, farming (e.g. palm oil and soya), mining, road building and hydroelectric dams.
What are the impacts of deforestation? ~~ Loss of biodiversity, soil erosion, climate change, and loss of indigenous people's homes.
How does deforestation contribute to climate change? ~~ Trees store carbon; burning or cutting them releases CO₂, and fewer trees absorb CO₂.
What is sustainable forestry? ~~ Managing forests so trees are replanted and the forest can continue to provide resources.
What is selective logging? ~~ Only cutting down some mature trees, leaving the rest of the forest intact.
What is ecotourism? ~~ Tourism that conserves the environment and benefits local people.
What is agroforestry? ~~ Growing crops and trees together, protecting soil and providing resources.
What is debt-for-nature swap? ~~ A country's debt is reduced in exchange for protecting its rainforest.
What role do indigenous people play in rainforests? ~~ They often live sustainably and help protect forests from exploitation.
What is a food web? ~~ A diagram showing how feeding relationships are linked in an ecosystem.
What is a producer? ~~ An organism that makes its own food through photosynthesis, e.g. plants.
What is a consumer? ~~ An organism that eats other organisms.
What is a decomposer? ~~ An organism that breaks down dead material, releasing nutrients, e.g. fungi and bacteria.
Why is the Amazon rainforest important globally? ~~ It stores carbon, regulates climate, has huge biodiversity, and provides medicines.

## 1.5
What is a hot desert? ~~ An area with very low rainfall (under 250 mm a year) and high daytime temperatures.
Where are hot deserts found? ~~ Around 30° north and south of the equator, e.g. the Sahara and Thar deserts.
What is the climate of a hot desert? ~~ Very hot days, cold nights and very little rainfall.
Why are desert nights cold? ~~ Clear skies allow heat to escape quickly.
How are plants adapted to hot deserts? ~~ Cacti store water, have spines instead of leaves, and have long or wide roots.
How are animals adapted to hot deserts? ~~ Camels store fat in humps and have wide feet; many animals are nocturnal.
What is desert soil like? ~~ Sandy or stony, dry, and low in nutrients.
What is desertification? ~~ The process by which land becomes desert-like due to climate change and human activity.
What are the causes of desertification? ~~ Overgrazing, overcultivation, deforestation, population growth and climate change.
Where is desertification a problem? ~~ In semi-arid areas such as the Sahel in Africa.
How can desertification be reduced? ~~ Planting trees, water conservation, appropriate technology, and improving farming methods.
What are magic stones (bunds)? ~~ Lines of stones that slow water runoff and trap soil, reducing erosion.
What opportunities do hot deserts offer? ~~ Mining, energy (solar), farming with irrigation, and tourism.
What challenges do hot deserts present? ~~ Extreme temperatures, water shortages, and poor accessibility.
What is irrigation? ~~ Artificially watering crops.
What is solar energy's potential in deserts? ~~ Deserts receive lots of sunshine, making them ideal for solar power plants.
What is the Great Green Wall? ~~ A project to plant a belt of trees across the Sahel to stop desertification.
What is an oasis? ~~ A fertile area in a desert where water is found.
What is a nomad? ~~ A person who moves from place to place with their animals to find food and water.
What is overgrazing? ~~ When too many animals graze an area, removing vegetation and exposing soil.
What is overcultivation? ~~ When land is farmed too intensively, exhausting the soil.
Why are desert ecosystems fragile? ~~ Low rainfall and extreme conditions mean they recover slowly from damage.
What is biodiversity like in hot deserts? ~~ Lower than rainforests, but many species are specially adapted.
How does climate change affect hot deserts? ~~ Higher temperatures and less rainfall may make them hotter and drier, expanding their area.
What is a sand dune in a desert? ~~ A hill of sand shaped by the wind.
What is the water cycle like in deserts? ~~ Low rainfall and high evaporation mean little water is stored.
What is the role of tourism in deserts? ~~ It provides jobs and income, but can put pressure on water and fragile environments.
What is mining's impact in deserts? ~~ It provides jobs and resources but uses water and can damage landscapes.
What is the Thar Desert? ~~ A hot desert in north-west India and Pakistan.
What is the Sahel? ~~ A semi-arid region south of the Sahara, at risk of desertification.

## 1.6
What are the two main types of cold environment? ~~ Polar (ice sheets, e.g. Antarctica) and tundra (treeless land fringing the Arctic).
Where is tundra found? ~~ Roughly 60°–75°N in northern Canada, Alaska, northern Russia and Scandinavia.
What is permafrost? ~~ Ground that stays frozen for at least two consecutive years.
What is the active layer? ~~ The top layer of ground above the permafrost that thaws each summer.
Why is the tundra waterlogged in summer? ~~ Meltwater cannot drain through the impermeable permafrost below.
How cold do polar winters get? ~~ Often below −50°C; Antarctica's record is −89.2°C.
How much precipitation do polar areas get? ~~ Under 250 mm a year — they are polar deserts.
Why are polar areas so cold? ~~ Low-angle sunlight is spread out and high-albedo snow and ice reflect most of it.
How long is the tundra growing season? ~~ About 6–10 weeks.
Why can't trees grow in the tundra? ~~ Permafrost stops roots growing deep and the summer is too short and cold.
Why are tundra soils infertile? ~~ They are thin, acidic and decomposition is very slow, so peat builds up.
How do plants protect permafrost? ~~ Vegetation and peat insulate the ground from summer heat.
Give two plant adaptations to the tundra. ~~ Low cushion shapes to avoid wind, and shallow roots in the active layer.
Give two animal adaptations to cold environments. ~~ Thick fur or blubber for insulation, and small extremities to reduce heat loss.
Why do caribou migrate? ~~ To find food and avoid the harshest winter conditions — up to 5,000 km a year.
Why is biodiversity low in cold environments? ~~ The harsh climate allows few species, so food webs are simple.
Why are cold environment food webs fragile? ~~ With few species, losing one (e.g. lemmings) affects many others.
What is the main economic opportunity in Alaska? ~~ Oil, especially from Prudhoe Bay on the North Slope.
What is the Alaska Permanent Fund? ~~ A fund built from oil revenue that pays every resident an annual dividend.
Give two other opportunities in Alaska. ~~ Fishing (about 60% of US seafood) and tourism (over 2 million visitors a year).
Give two challenges of developing Alaska. ~~ Extreme cold and inaccessibility — vast distances and few roads.
Why are Alaskan buildings raised on piles? ~~ To stop heat from the building thawing the permafrost and causing subsidence.
How was the Trans-Alaska Pipeline designed? ~~ Raised on supports with heat pipes, built in a zigzag, with crossings for caribou.
What happened in the Exxon Valdez disaster? ~~ In 1989, about 41 million litres of oil spilled into Prince William Sound, killing wildlife.
What is a wilderness area? ~~ A natural area largely undisturbed by human activity.
Why are cold environments valuable? ~~ Scientific research (ice cores), unique wildlife, carbon storage and indigenous cultures.
Why are cold environments fragile? ~~ Slow plant growth, slow decomposition and simple food webs mean damage lasts for decades.
What does the Antarctic Treaty do? ~~ Reserves Antarctica for peaceful scientific research; its Madrid Protocol bans mining.
Give an example of a government protecting a cold environment. ~~ The Arctic National Wildlife Refuge (ANWR) in Alaska protects 78,000 km².
Why is thawing permafrost a climate risk? ~~ It releases methane and CO₂, causing more warming — a positive feedback.

## 1.7
What are the four processes of coastal erosion? ~~ Hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition and solution.
What is hydraulic action? ~~ Waves force air into cracks in the cliff, which breaks the rock apart.
What is abrasion? ~~ Rocks carried by waves are thrown against the cliff, wearing it away.
What is attrition? ~~ Rocks carried by waves knock against each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
What is solution? ~~ Seawater dissolves soluble rocks such as chalk and limestone.
What is longshore drift? ~~ The movement of sediment along the coast by waves approaching at an angle.
What is the swash? ~~ The movement of water up the beach after a wave breaks.
What is the backwash? ~~ The movement of water back down the beach under gravity.
What is a constructive wave? ~~ A low, gentle wave with a strong swash and weak backwash, which builds up beaches.
What is a destructive wave? ~~ A tall, steep wave with a weak swash and strong backwash, which erodes beaches.
What is fetch? ~~ The distance over which the wind blows across open water, affecting wave size.
How is a headland formed? ~~ Resistant rock erodes more slowly than softer rock, leaving land sticking out into the sea.
How is a bay formed? ~~ Softer rock is eroded more quickly, forming a curved inlet between headlands.
How is a stack formed? ~~ Waves erode a crack into a cave, then an arch; the arch roof collapses, leaving a stack.
What is a stump? ~~ The remains of a stack after it has been eroded further.
What is a wave-cut platform? ~~ A flat area of rock at the base of a cliff, left as the cliff retreats through erosion.
How is a spit formed? ~~ Longshore drift carries sediment past a bend in the coastline, where it is deposited in the sea.
What is a bar? ~~ A spit that grows across a bay, joining two headlands and sometimes trapping a lagoon.
What is a beach? ~~ A deposit of sand and shingle along the coast, formed by constructive waves.
What is a sand dune? ~~ A hill of sand formed when wind blows sand inland, trapped by vegetation such as marram grass.
What is hard engineering? ~~ Building artificial structures to control the sea, e.g. sea walls, groynes and rock armour.
What is soft engineering? ~~ Working with natural processes to manage the coast, e.g. beach nourishment and dune regeneration.
What is a sea wall? ~~ A concrete wall that reflects waves back out to sea; effective but expensive.
What is a groyne? ~~ A wooden or rock barrier built out to sea to trap sediment moved by longshore drift.
What is rock armour? ~~ Large boulders placed at the foot of a cliff to absorb wave energy.
What is beach nourishment? ~~ Adding sand or shingle to a beach to make it wider, absorbing wave energy.
What is managed retreat? ~~ Allowing the coast to erode or flood naturally in low-value areas.
What is sea-level rise's effect on coasts? ~~ It increases flooding and erosion, threatening homes, farmland and habitats.
What is a Shoreline Management Plan? ~~ A plan that decides how each stretch of coast should be managed: hold the line, advance, retreat or do nothing.
What is mass movement at the coast? ~~ The downhill movement of material under gravity, e.g. slumping and rockfalls.

## 1.8
What is the source of a river? ~~ Where the river begins, often in upland areas.
What is the mouth of a river? ~~ Where the river meets the sea or a lake.
What is a tributary? ~~ A smaller river that joins a larger one.
What is a confluence? ~~ The point where two rivers meet.
What is a drainage basin? ~~ The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.
What is a watershed? ~~ The boundary of a drainage basin, usually high ground.
How does a river change from source to mouth? ~~ It becomes wider and deeper, with a greater discharge and gentler gradient.
What is vertical erosion? ~~ Erosion that deepens the river channel, common in the upper course.
What is lateral erosion? ~~ Erosion that widens the river channel, common in the middle and lower course.
What are the four types of river transportation? ~~ Traction, saltation, suspension and solution.
What is traction? ~~ Large boulders are rolled along the river bed.
What is saltation? ~~ Small pebbles bounce along the river bed.
What is suspension? ~~ Fine particles are carried in the water.
When does a river deposit sediment? ~~ When it loses energy, e.g. when it slows down on the inside of a bend or enters the sea.
How is a waterfall formed? ~~ Soft rock beneath hard rock is eroded, creating an overhang that collapses; the waterfall retreats, leaving a gorge.
What is a gorge? ~~ A steep-sided valley formed as a waterfall retreats upstream.
What is a V-shaped valley? ~~ A steep valley in the upper course, formed by vertical erosion and weathering.
What is interlocking spurs? ~~ Hills that stick out alternately on either side of a river in the upper course.
How is a meander formed? ~~ Erosion on the outside of a bend (fast flow) and deposition on the inside (slow flow).
How is an oxbow lake formed? ~~ Erosion narrows the neck of a meander until the river cuts through it during a flood; deposition seals off the old loop.
What is a floodplain? ~~ Flat land either side of a river in the lower course, covered with deposits during floods.
What is a levée? ~~ A raised bank along a river, formed by deposition of sediment during floods.
What is a hydrograph? ~~ A graph showing river discharge over time, often in relation to rainfall.
What is lag time? ~~ The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
What factors increase flood risk? ~~ Heavy rainfall, impermeable rock, steep slopes, urbanisation and deforestation.
How does urbanisation increase flood risk? ~~ Impermeable surfaces increase surface runoff, and drains carry water quickly to rivers.
What is a flood defence? ~~ A structure or strategy to reduce flooding, e.g. dams, embankments, flood relief channels.
What is a flood relief channel? ~~ An artificial channel that diverts water away from a town when river levels are high.
What is river restoration? ~~ Returning a river to a more natural state to reduce flood risk and improve habitats.
What is flood zoning? ~~ Restricting building on floodplains in areas at high risk of flooding.

## 1.9
What is a glacier? ~~ A large mass of ice that moves slowly downhill under gravity.
What is plucking? ~~ Ice freezes onto rocks and pulls them away as the glacier moves.
What is abrasion by glaciers? ~~ Rocks frozen into the base of the glacier scrape the rock below like sandpaper.
What is freeze–thaw weathering? ~~ Water in cracks freezes and expands, widening the crack until the rock breaks.
What is a corrie? ~~ An armchair-shaped hollow on a mountainside, formed by glacial erosion; it may contain a lake (tarn).
What is an arête? ~~ A sharp, knife-edge ridge between two corries.
What is a pyramidal peak? ~~ A sharp mountain peak formed when three or more corries erode back to back.
What is a U-shaped valley? ~~ A wide, flat-bottomed valley with steep sides, formed by a glacier.
What is a truncated spur? ~~ A cut-off end of a spur, eroded by a glacier as it moved down the valley.
What is a hanging valley? ~~ A small valley left high above a main glacial valley, often with a waterfall.
What is a ribbon lake? ~~ A long, narrow lake in the bottom of a U-shaped valley.
What is moraine? ~~ Rocks and sediment deposited by a glacier.
What is a terminal moraine? ~~ A ridge of material marking the furthest point reached by a glacier.
What is lateral moraine? ~~ Material deposited along the sides of a glacier.
What is a drumlin? ~~ An egg-shaped hill of glacial deposits, with a blunt end facing the direction the ice came from.
What is an erratic? ~~ A boulder carried by a glacier and deposited in an area with different rock.
What is till? ~~ Unsorted sediment deposited by a glacier.
Where are glaciated landscapes found in the UK? ~~ In upland areas such as the Lake District, Snowdonia and the Scottish Highlands.
What was the last glacial period? ~~ The most recent ice age, which ended about 10,000 years ago.
How is glaciated land used? ~~ Farming (sheep), tourism, forestry, quarrying, and hydroelectric power.
What are the impacts of tourism in glaciated areas? ~~ Jobs and income, but also footpath erosion, traffic congestion and litter.
What is footpath erosion? ~~ Damage to paths from many walkers, removing vegetation and exposing soil.
How can footpath erosion be managed? ~~ Laying stone paths, fencing off areas, and signs to keep walkers on paths.
What is a honeypot site? ~~ A popular tourist location that attracts large numbers of visitors.
What conflicts can occur in glaciated areas? ~~ Between farmers, tourists, conservationists and residents over land use.
What is a national park? ~~ A protected area of countryside managed for conservation and recreation.
Why are glaciers retreating? ~~ Global warming is melting ice faster than it accumulates.
What is a glacial trough? ~~ Another name for a U-shaped valley.
What is a tarn? ~~ A small lake in a corrie.
What is the difference between erosion and weathering? ~~ Weathering breaks down rock in place; erosion wears it away and moves it.

## 2.1
What is urbanisation? ~~ The increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities.
What is a megacity? ~~ A city with a population of more than 10 million.
What causes urbanisation? ~~ Rural–urban migration and natural increase.
What are push factors? ~~ Reasons that make people leave rural areas, e.g. lack of jobs, poor services and natural disasters.
What are pull factors? ~~ Reasons that attract people to cities, e.g. jobs, better healthcare and education.
What is natural increase? ~~ When the birth rate is higher than the death rate.
Where is urbanisation happening fastest? ~~ In lower-income and newly emerging countries in Africa and Asia.
What is a squatter settlement? ~~ An area of poor-quality housing built illegally on land the residents don't own.
What are the problems of squatter settlements? ~~ Poor sanitation, lack of clean water, disease, crime, and flood or fire risk.
What is the informal economy? ~~ Jobs that aren't officially recorded or taxed, e.g. street selling.
What is a site-and-service scheme? ~~ Authorities provide land with basic services, and residents build their own homes.
What is self-help housing? ~~ Residents are given materials and support to improve their own homes.
What is suburbanisation? ~~ The movement of people from the inner city to the suburbs.
What is counter-urbanisation? ~~ The movement of people from cities to rural areas.
What is re-urbanisation? ~~ The movement of people back into city centres, often after regeneration.
What is a global city? ~~ A city with major international influence in finance, culture and politics, e.g. London or New York.
What are the challenges of rapid urbanisation? ~~ Housing shortages, traffic, pollution, unemployment and pressure on services.
What is urban sprawl? ~~ The spread of cities into the surrounding countryside.
What is a brownfield site? ~~ Previously developed land that can be reused.
What is a greenfield site? ~~ Land that hasn't been built on before.
What is a sustainable city? ~~ A city that meets people's needs without damaging the environment for future generations.
Give an example of a sustainable urban feature. ~~ Public transport, green spaces, recycling schemes and energy-efficient buildings.
What is the CBD? ~~ The central business district, the commercial centre of a city.
What is the inner city? ~~ The area around the CBD, often with older housing and industry.
What is the rural–urban fringe? ~~ The area where the city meets the countryside.
What is gentrification? ~~ Wealthier people moving into a poorer area and improving properties, which can displace original residents.
What is regeneration? ~~ Improving a run-down area through investment in housing, jobs and services.
What are the benefits of urbanisation? ~~ Better access to jobs, education and healthcare, and economic growth.
What is an urban transport problem? ~~ Traffic congestion, which causes pollution and delays.
How can urban transport problems be reduced? ~~ Park-and-ride, congestion charges, bus lanes, cycle lanes and trams.

## 2.2
What is the UK's population distribution? ~~ Most people live in cities and the south-east of England, with lower densities in upland areas.
What is deindustrialisation? ~~ The decline of manufacturing industry.
What caused deindustrialisation in UK cities? ~~ Competition from cheaper overseas producers, automation, and the decline of coal and steel.
What is the impact of deindustrialisation? ~~ Job losses, derelict land, and outmigration from affected areas.
What is the post-industrial economy? ~~ An economy based on services, finance, research and IT rather than manufacturing.
What is a science park? ~~ A group of high-tech businesses located near a university.
What are the challenges facing UK cities? ~~ Housing shortages, inequality, traffic, pollution and derelict land.
What is urban deprivation? ~~ A lack of access to jobs, services and good housing in parts of a city.
What is the Index of Multiple Deprivation? ~~ A measure of deprivation using income, employment, health, education, crime, housing and environment.
What is urban regeneration? ~~ Improving run-down urban areas to attract people and businesses.
Give an example of a UK regeneration project. ~~ London Docklands, Cardiff Bay, or Manchester's Salford Quays.
What is Cardiff Bay regeneration? ~~ Redevelopment of the old docks into a waterfront area with offices, housing, the Senedd and leisure facilities.
What are the benefits of regeneration? ~~ New jobs, improved environment, better housing and increased investment.
What are the problems of regeneration? ~~ Rising house prices may push out original residents, and not all benefit.
What is a retail park? ~~ A group of large stores on the edge of a town, with free parking.
Why have high streets declined? ~~ Online shopping, out-of-town retail parks and high rents.
What is a commuter? ~~ Someone who travels from where they live to work in another place.
What is a commuter village? ~~ A village where many residents travel to work in a nearby town or city.
What is rural depopulation? ~~ The decline in the population of rural areas as people move to cities.
What is the impact of second homes in rural areas? ~~ Higher house prices, fewer local services used year-round, and communities left empty.
What is counter-urbanisation's effect on rural areas? ~~ Increased house prices, more traffic, and changes to village character.
What is a green belt? ~~ An area of protected land around a city where building is restricted.
What is the housing crisis in the UK? ~~ A shortage of affordable homes, especially in London and the south-east.
What is affordable housing? ~~ Housing sold or rented below market price for people on low incomes.
What is sustainable transport in UK cities? ~~ Buses, trams, cycling schemes and electric vehicle charging points.
What is ethnic diversity in UK cities? ~~ Cities like London and Birmingham have diverse populations from many backgrounds.
What is the impact of international migration on UK cities? ~~ Population growth, cultural diversity and a larger workforce, but pressure on services.
What is a Local Development Plan? ~~ A council's plan for how land will be used and developed in its area.
What is urban greening? ~~ Adding green spaces, trees and parks to cities to improve the environment.
What is a sustainable community? ~~ A place with good housing, jobs, transport, services and environment for current and future residents.

## 2.3
What is development? ~~ The process of improving people's standard of living and quality of life.
What is GNI per capita? ~~ Gross national income divided by the population, a measure of average wealth.
What is the Human Development Index? ~~ A measure of development combining life expectancy, education and income.
What is life expectancy? ~~ The average number of years a person is expected to live.
What is literacy rate? ~~ The percentage of adults who can read and write.
What is infant mortality rate? ~~ The number of babies who die before their first birthday per 1000 live births.
What is a high-income country (HIC)? ~~ A wealthy, developed country, e.g. the UK or USA.
What is a low-income country (LIC)? ~~ A poorer country with lower development, e.g. Malawi.
What is a newly emerging economy (NEE)? ~~ A country experiencing rapid economic growth, e.g. India or Brazil.
What is the development gap? ~~ The difference in wealth and quality of life between richer and poorer countries.
What are the causes of uneven development? ~~ Physical factors (climate, disasters), economic factors (trade, debt) and historical factors (colonialism).
How does trade affect development? ~~ Countries exporting low-value primary products earn less than those exporting manufactured goods.
What is aid? ~~ Help given by one country or organisation to another, e.g. money, food or expertise.
What is short-term aid? ~~ Emergency help after a disaster, e.g. food, shelter and medicine.
What is long-term aid? ~~ Help to improve development over time, e.g. building schools and wells.
What is fair trade? ~~ A system ensuring producers in poorer countries receive a fair price and good working conditions.
What is debt relief? ~~ Cancelling some of a country's debt so it can spend more on development.
What is microfinance? ~~ Small loans to people in poorer countries to start businesses.
What is intermediate technology? ~~ Simple, affordable technology suited to local needs, e.g. water pumps.
What is a multinational company? ~~ A company that operates in more than one country.
What are the advantages of TNCs to poorer countries? ~~ Jobs, investment, skills and infrastructure.
What are the disadvantages of TNCs to poorer countries? ~~ Low wages, poor conditions, profits going abroad, and environmental damage.
What is the Demographic Transition Model? ~~ A model showing how birth and death rates change as a country develops.
What is the dependency ratio? ~~ The proportion of people too young or old to work compared with the working population.
What is industrialisation? ~~ The development of manufacturing industry in a country.
What is globalisation? ~~ The increasing connection of countries through trade, communication and culture.
How can tourism help development? ~~ It brings jobs, income and investment, but can harm the environment and culture.
What are the Sustainable Development Goals? ~~ 17 UN goals to end poverty, protect the planet and improve lives by 2030.
What is a top-down development project? ~~ A large-scale project planned by governments or organisations, e.g. a dam.
What is a bottom-up development project? ~~ A small-scale project run by local communities, e.g. a village well.

## 2.4
Why are food, water and energy significant? ~~ They are essential for health, economic development and social wellbeing.
What is resource security? ~~ Reliable access to enough affordable resources to meet needs.
How many people worldwide are undernourished? ~~ Around 700 million, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
How much food is wasted globally? ~~ About a third of all food produced.
How much water does a UK resident use per day? ~~ About 140 litres, compared with 300–400 in the USA and under 20 in parts of Africa.
How many people lack electricity? ~~ Around 700 million, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa.
Why is UK demand for exotic food rising? ~~ Higher incomes, foreign travel, a multicultural population and supermarket competition.
How much of its food does the UK import? ~~ Around 40–45%.
What are food miles? ~~ The distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten.
What is a carbon footprint? ~~ The total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product or activity.
Why are food miles an incomplete measure? ~~ Production methods matter — heated UK greenhouses can emit more than sun-grown imports.
What is local sourcing? ~~ Buying food produced nearby, e.g. from farmers' markets, farm shops and veg boxes.
What is organic food? ~~ Food grown without artificial fertilisers or pesticides.
What is agribusiness? ~~ Large-scale, intensive, mechanised farming run as a commercial business.
Give two impacts of agribusiness. ~~ Cheap, plentiful food, but loss of hedgerows and pollution from fertiliser run-off.
Why is UK water demand rising? ~~ Population growth, more households and more water-using appliances.
Name two sources of UK water pollution. ~~ Farm fertiliser run-off and sewage overflows.
What is the Thames Tideway Tunnel? ~~ A 25 km "super sewer" that captures sewage overflows before they reach the Thames.
Where is the UK's water surplus? ~~ The north and west — high rainfall, lower population.
Where is the UK's water deficit? ~~ The south and east — low rainfall, high population.
What is water transfer? ~~ Moving water from areas of surplus to areas of deficit through pipes, canals or rivers.
Give an example of UK water transfer. ~~ The Elan Valley reservoirs in Wales supply Birmingham via a 118 km aqueduct.
Give a disadvantage of a national water grid. ~~ High cost of pumping and ecological risks from mixing river basins.
When did the UK's last coal power station close? ~~ September 2024 (Ratcliffe-on-Soar).
What is the UK's largest single source of electricity? ~~ Natural gas, followed closely by wind.
What share of UK electricity comes from renewables? ~~ Around half, with offshore wind the biggest contributor.
Why does the UK import more energy than before? ~~ North Sea oil and gas production peaked around 2000 and has declined.
What is fracking? ~~ Pumping water, sand and chemicals into shale rock at high pressure to release gas.
Why was fracking halted in England? ~~ Earth tremors, including a 2.9 magnitude tremor at Preston New Road in 2019.
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of nuclear power. ~~ Reliable and low-carbon, but expensive and produces radioactive waste.

## 2.5
What is food security? ~~ When all people always have access to enough safe, nutritious food for a healthy life.
What is food insecurity? ~~ When people lack reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
Give two areas of food surplus. ~~ North America and Europe (also Australia and Argentina).
Give two areas of food deficit. ~~ Much of sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia (also Yemen and Haiti).
Give two reasons for rising global food consumption. ~~ Population growth and economic development increasing meat and dairy demand.
How do biofuels affect food supply? ~~ Land used for fuel crops is not used for food, raising food prices.
Name two physical factors affecting food supply. ~~ Climate (droughts and floods) and pests or disease (e.g. locusts).
Name two human factors affecting food supply. ~~ Conflict and poverty (also technology and water stress).
What is famine? ~~ A widespread, severe shortage of food causing starvation and death.
What is undernutrition? ~~ Not eating enough of the right food, causing stunting and weak immunity.
How can food insecurity cause soil erosion? ~~ Overcultivation and overgrazing remove vegetation, leaving soil bare.
How can food insecurity cause social unrest? ~~ Shortages and high prices lead to protests and riots, e.g. 2008 and the Arab Spring.
What is irrigation? ~~ Artificially watering crops using canals, sprinklers or drip systems.
Give a disadvantage of irrigation. ~~ Salinisation of soils and depletion of rivers and aquifers.
What is hydroponics? ~~ Growing plants with roots in nutrient-rich water instead of soil.
What is aeroponics? ~~ Growing plants with roots in air, misted with nutrient solution.
What was the Green Revolution? ~~ From the 1960s, high-yield seeds, fertilisers and irrigation greatly raised yields in Asia.
What is the new Green Revolution? ~~ Using biotechnology and sustainable methods to raise yields, especially in Africa.
Give one argument for and one against GM crops. ~~ For: higher yields and added nutrients. Against: biodiversity risks and dependence on seed companies.
What is appropriate technology? ~~ Simple, affordable technology suited to local needs and skills, e.g. treadle pumps.
What is Thanet Earth? ~~ The UK's largest hydroponic greenhouse complex, in Kent, opened in 2008.
Give an advantage of Thanet Earth. ~~ Year-round salad crops, fewer food miles and over 500 jobs.
Give a disadvantage of Thanet Earth. ~~ Built on greenfield farmland; gas-fired power plants emit CO₂; light pollution.
What is permaculture? ~~ Farming that copies natural ecosystems so it maintains its own soil fertility.
Give an example of urban farming. ~~ Incredible Edible Todmorden in West Yorkshire, or community gardens in Havana.
How can fish be eaten sustainably? ~~ By choosing fish with the Marine Stewardship Council label and avoiding overfished species.
Why is seasonal food more sustainable? ~~ It cuts energy used for transport and heated greenhouses.
Where is the Makueni food and water scheme? ~~ In semi-arid south-east Kenya.
How do sand dams work? ~~ A wall across a seasonal river traps sand that stores rainwater, protected from evaporation.
Why is the Makueni scheme sustainable? ~~ Low-cost, built with local materials and labour, and maintained by the community.

## 2.6
What is water security? ~~ Having reliable access to enough safe, clean water.
What is water scarcity? ~~ When there isn't enough water to meet demand.
What is physical water scarcity? ~~ Not enough water available because of the climate or overuse.
What is economic water scarcity? ~~ Water is available but people can't afford the infrastructure to access it.
Why is demand for water increasing? ~~ Population growth, economic development, and increased use in farming and industry.
Where is water surplus in the UK? ~~ The north and west, which have high rainfall and low population.
Where is water deficit in the UK? ~~ The south-east, which has lower rainfall and high population.
What is a water transfer scheme? ~~ Moving water from areas of surplus to areas of deficit.
What is a reservoir? ~~ An artificial lake used to store water.
What are the advantages of dams and reservoirs? ~~ Water supply, hydroelectric power, flood control and recreation.
What are the disadvantages of dams and reservoirs? ~~ Flooding of land and homes, loss of habitats, and high costs.
What is desalination? ~~ Removing salt from seawater to make fresh water.
What is a disadvantage of desalination? ~~ It is expensive and uses a lot of energy.
What is groundwater? ~~ Water stored underground in rocks (aquifers).
What is water conservation? ~~ Reducing water use, e.g. using water meters, efficient appliances and fixing leaks.
What is greywater recycling? ~~ Reusing water from sinks, showers and washing machines for flushing toilets or watering gardens.
What is rainwater harvesting? ~~ Collecting and storing rainwater for use.
How does farming affect water supply? ~~ Irrigation uses large amounts of water, and fertilisers can pollute rivers.
What is water pollution? ~~ Contamination of water by chemicals, sewage or waste.
What is a water-borne disease? ~~ A disease spread through contaminated water, e.g. cholera and typhoid.
How can water supply be improved in poorer countries? ~~ Wells, hand pumps, rainwater harvesting and water purification.
What is a sustainable water supply? ~~ Using water in a way that meets current needs without harming future supply.
What is the water cycle? ~~ The continuous movement of water between the sea, air and land.
What is evaporation? ~~ Water changing from liquid to gas.
What is condensation? ~~ Water vapour cooling and changing into liquid, forming clouds.
What is precipitation? ~~ Water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
What is infiltration? ~~ Water soaking into the soil.
What is surface runoff? ~~ Water flowing over the ground into rivers.
What is water stress? ~~ When demand for water exceeds the available supply.
What is a water conflict? ~~ A dispute between groups or countries over access to water, e.g. over the Nile.

## 2.7
What is energy security? ~~ Access to reliable, affordable and sufficient energy.
What is energy insecurity? ~~ When energy supply is unreliable, too expensive or insufficient.
What is an energy surplus? ~~ When a country produces more energy than it uses, e.g. Saudi Arabia or Norway.
What is an energy deficit? ~~ When a country uses more energy than it produces, e.g. Japan or the UK.
Where are most oil reserves? ~~ The Middle East holds around half of proven oil reserves.
Give two reasons for rising energy consumption. ~~ Economic development and population growth (also technology and rising living standards).
Name four factors affecting energy supply. ~~ Physical factors, cost of exploitation, technology and politics.
How did politics affect energy in 2022? ~~ Russia's invasion of Ukraine caused European gas prices to soar.
Give two impacts of energy insecurity. ~~ Exploitation of sensitive areas (Arctic, tar sands) and reduced industrial output.
What is load-shedding? ~~ Planned power cuts, e.g. in South Africa, which reduce industrial output.
Give an advantage and disadvantage of wind power. ~~ Low carbon and cheap, but intermittent.
Give an advantage and disadvantage of solar power. ~~ Cheap and works in remote areas, but only generates in daylight.
Give an advantage and disadvantage of HEP. ~~ Reliable and controllable, but dams flood valleys and displace people.
Give an advantage and disadvantage of geothermal energy. ~~ Reliable and low carbon, but only possible in tectonically active areas.
Give an advantage and disadvantage of nuclear power. ~~ Reliable and low carbon, but expensive with radioactive waste.
Give an advantage and disadvantage of fossil fuels. ~~ Reliable with existing infrastructure, but finite and the main cause of climate change.
What is fracking? ~~ Pumping water, sand and chemicals into shale rock at high pressure to release gas.
Give two advantages of fracking. ~~ Domestic gas supply improves energy security; gas emits less CO₂ than coal.
Give two disadvantages of fracking. ~~ Earth tremors and risk of groundwater pollution.
Why is gas called a "bridge" fuel? ~~ It emits about half the CO₂ of coal while renewables expand.
What is a carbon footprint? ~~ The total greenhouse gases caused by a person, product or activity.
Give two ways to reduce your carbon footprint. ~~ Insulate your home and use public transport, cycling or walking.
How can homes be designed to save energy? ~~ Insulation, triple glazing, solar panels, heat pumps and passive solar design.
How can transport use less energy? ~~ Electric vehicles, public transport, cycle lanes and congestion charges.
How do smart meters help? ~~ They show real-time energy use so people waste less.
Where is the Chambamontera micro-hydro scheme? ~~ A remote village in the Andes of northern Peru.
Who helped build the Chambamontera scheme? ~~ The charity Practical Action, with the local community.
How does a micro-hydro scheme work? ~~ Stream water flows through a pipe to turn a small turbine and generate electricity.
Give two benefits of the Chambamontera scheme. ~~ Lighting for study after dark, and less smoke from kerosene and firewood.
Why is Chambamontera's scheme sustainable? ~~ Renewable, no large dam, low-cost and maintained by trained local people.

## 3.1
How long is AQA GCSE Geography Paper 3? ~~ 1 hour 15 minutes, 76 marks including 6 for SPaG.
What are the two sections of Paper 3? ~~ Section A: issue evaluation; Section B: fieldwork.
When is the pre-release booklet issued? ~~ 12 weeks before the exam.
What is the final issue evaluation question worth? ~~ 9 marks, plus 3 marks for SPaG.
Is there a right answer in the issue evaluation decision? ~~ No — marks come from justifying your choice using evidence from the resources.
How many fieldwork enquiries must you complete? ~~ Two: one physical and one human, in contrasting environments.
What are the six stages of an enquiry? ~~ Question, data collection, processing and presenting, analysing and explaining, conclusions, evaluation.
What is a hypothesis? ~~ A statement that can be tested and proved or disproved by an investigation.
What is primary data? ~~ Data you collect yourself during fieldwork.
What is secondary data? ~~ Data collected by others, e.g. census data, maps or Environment Agency records.
What is random sampling? ~~ Every item has an equal chance of being selected.
What is systematic sampling? ~~ Taking samples at regular intervals, e.g. every 10 m along a transect.
What is stratified sampling? ~~ Sampling sub-groups in proportion to their size in the population.
What does the Bradshaw model predict? ~~ Width, depth, velocity and discharge increase downstream; gradient and particle size decrease.
How do you measure river velocity simply? ~~ Time a float over a set distance (e.g. 10 m) and repeat three times.
Name two risks in river fieldwork. ~~ Slipping or drowning, and waterborne disease such as Weil's disease.
What is an environmental quality survey? ~~ A survey scoring places on features such as litter, graffiti and green space.
Why are environmental quality surveys unreliable? ~~ Scoring is subjective and varies between people.
Which graph shows a relationship between two variables? ~~ A scatter graph with a line of best fit.
What is a choropleth map? ~~ A map shaded in different intensities to show data values for areas.
What is the mean? ~~ The total of all values divided by the number of values.
What is the median? ~~ The middle value when data is placed in order.
What is the mode? ~~ The value that occurs most often.
What is the range? ~~ The difference between the highest and lowest values.
What is the interquartile range? ~~ The spread of the middle 50% of data — upper quartile minus lower quartile.
What is an anomaly? ~~ A result that does not fit the general pattern.
What does correlation not prove? ~~ Causation — another factor may cause both variables to change.
What should a fieldwork conclusion do? ~~ Answer the original question using evidence from the results.
What does fieldwork evaluation involve? ~~ Identifying problems with methods and data, their effects, and improvements.
How can fieldwork reliability be improved? ~~ More sites, larger samples, repeat visits and better equipment.
