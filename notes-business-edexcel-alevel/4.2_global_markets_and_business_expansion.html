4.2 Global Markets and Business Expansion

Business — Edexcel (9BS0) — Theme 4: Global business

Topic 4.1 explained why the world economy has become more integrated. This topic looks at it from the individual firm's side: why a business decides to trade internationally (push and pull factors), where it should sell and where it should produce, how it might expand through global mergers and joint ventures, and what determines whether it can compete once it gets there — exchange rates, cost, differentiation and skills. Most exam questions give you a firm and a country and ask whether expansion is a good idea; the factors below are your checklist, and the evaluation lies in deciding which ones matter most for that firm.

Push factor — a condition in the domestic market that pushes a firm to seek sales abroad (e.g. saturation, intense competition).
Pull factor — an attraction of overseas markets that draws a firm to trade internationally (e.g. economies of scale, risk spreading).
Saturated market — a market in which most potential customers already own the product, so growth comes only from replacement sales or taking share from rivals.
Off-shoring — relocating a business activity (production, customer service, IT) to another country; the firm may still own and run it.
Outsourcing — contracting an activity out to another business (in the UK or abroad) rather than doing it in-house.
Joint venture — two or more businesses create a new, jointly owned enterprise, sharing investment, risk, control and profit.
Exchange rate — the value of one currency expressed in terms of another (e.g. £1 = $1.30).
Appreciation / depreciation — a rise / fall in the value of a currency against another.
Skill shortage — a situation where employers cannot recruit enough workers with the skills they need.

1. Conditions that prompt trade (4.2.1)

Push factors

  • Saturated markets — when the domestic market has stopped growing, the only way to grow sales is abroad. UK grocery, mobile phones and many consumer durables are saturated. Greggs is an exception that proves the point: it can still grow organically in the UK; many retailers cannot.
  • Competition — intense domestic rivalry squeezes margins, pushing firms to find less competitive markets abroad. Tesco's earlier moves into Central Europe and Asia were partly a response to competition at home (it has since withdrawn from most, which is a lesson in the risks).

Pull factors

  • Economies of scale — selling to a larger global market increases output, spreads fixed costs (R&D, design, brand building) over more units and lowers unit cost. Essential in industries with huge fixed costs: pharmaceuticals, aircraft, cars, software.
  • Risk spreading — selling in many countries reduces dependence on one economy; a recession or new regulation in one market is offset by growth elsewhere.

Off-shoring and outsourcing

Off-shoringOutsourcing
MeaningMoving an activity to another countryContracting an activity to another firm
CombinedOff-shore outsourcing: contracting an overseas firm (e.g. Apple uses Foxconn and other contract manufacturers in China and India to assemble iPhones)
ExamplesDyson moved vacuum-cleaner manufacturing from Wiltshire to Malaysia in 2002; UK banks' customer-service centres in IndiaPayroll, cleaning, IT, logistics outsourced to specialist providers

Reshoring — bringing activities back home — has grown since 2020 as firms prioritise supply-chain resilience over lowest cost, and tariffs make distant production less attractive.

Extending the product life cycle by selling in multiple markets

A product in the maturity or decline stage at home may be at the introduction or growth stage in another country. Selling it abroad extends its life and earns further returns on the original development costs with little extra investment. Examples: older smartphone models and car platforms sold in emerging markets; Cadbury Dairy Milk growing in India while mature in the UK. Limitations: the product may need adapting (see 4.3), and consumers in emerging markets increasingly expect the latest products.

5. Global competitiveness (4.2.5)

The impact of movements in exchange rates

Remember SPICED: Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer. (And the reverse — a weak pound makes imports dearer and exports cheaper.)

Worked example 1 — exporter. A UK firm sells a jumper for £40 in the USA.

  • At £1 = $1.25: US price = 40 × 1.25 = $50.00
  • If the pound appreciates to £1 = $1.35: US price = 40 × 1.35 = $54.00 — 8% dearer for US customers, so sales may fall (if demand is price elastic). Alternatively the firm could keep the price at $50, receiving only 50 ÷ 1.35 = £37.04 per jumper — a lower margin.
  • If the pound depreciates to £1 = $1.15: US price = 40 × 1.15 = $46.00 — more competitive.

Worked example 2 — importer. A UK retailer imports a product from the eurozone for €30 and sells it for £35.

  • At £1 = €1.20: cost = 30 ÷ 1.20 = £25.00; profit per unit = 35 − 25.00 = £10.00
  • If the pound depreciates to £1 = €1.10: cost = 30 ÷ 1.10 = £27.27; profit per unit = 35 − 27.27 = £7.73 — a fall of 22.7%.

To convert from pounds into a foreign currency, multiply by the exchange rate; to convert into pounds, divide. The June 2024 Paper 1 calculation followed exactly this method (converting a euro price into sterling by dividing by 1.19).

Evaluation: the impact depends on price elasticity of demand; on how much of the firm's costs are in foreign currency (an exporter that imports components gains less from a weak pound); on whether the change is temporary or sustained; on whether the firm has hedged using forward contracts; and on non-price factors such as quality and brand, which make demand less sensitive to price. After the 2016 referendum the pound fell sharply, which helped exporters such as Burberry but raised costs for importers and led to “shrinkflation” (the Toblerone case).

Competitive advantage through cost competitiveness

Being able to offer lower prices than rivals, or equal prices with higher margins, by having lower unit costs (link to Porter's cost leadership in 3.1). Sources: economies of scale; lower labour costs or higher labour productivity; lean production and efficient supply chains; off-shoring and outsourcing; technology and automation. Examples: Chinese EV maker BYD's vertically integrated battery production gives it a cost advantage in Europe; Primark's scale buying and low-cost sourcing.

Competitive advantage through differentiation

Offering products that customers perceive as unique or superior, so they will pay a premium and are less sensitive to price and exchange rates. Sources: brand and heritage (“Made in Britain” for Burberry, Mulberry, Rolls-Royce cars); design and innovation (Dyson, Apple); quality and reliability; after-sales service; ethical and sustainable credentials. Differentiation is especially important for high-cost countries such as the UK, which cannot compete on labour cost.

Skill shortages and their impact on international competitiveness

  • Causes: insufficient training and education in key subjects (engineering, digital, green skills), ageing workforce, restrictions on migration, fast technological change.
  • Impact on firms: unfilled vacancies limit output; wage inflation as firms compete for scarce workers raises costs; lower productivity and quality; slower innovation; projects delayed; firms may relocate or off-shore activities to countries with the skills.
  • UK examples: the HGV-driver shortage of 2021 disrupted supermarket and fuel deliveries; persistent shortages of engineers, construction workers, care workers and cyber-security specialists; stricter Skilled Worker visa rules from July 2025 raised the skill and salary thresholds for recruiting from abroad.
  • Responses: in-house training and apprenticeships (the Apprenticeship Levy), higher pay and better conditions, automation, recruiting overseas where permitted, partnerships with universities and colleges.

How Edexcel rewards this topic

  • Knowledge: definitions of push/pull factors, off-shoring vs outsourcing, joint venture; the exchange-rate formula (worth the knowledge mark on a 4-mark calculation).
  • Application: use the extract's country, product, data and exchange rates; calculate accurately to the stated decimal places.
  • Analysis: e.g. appreciation of the pound → higher foreign-currency price of exports → lower demand if elastic → lower revenue — or maintain price and accept lower margin.
  • Evaluation: weigh market attractiveness against risks; assess what the decision depends on (elasticity, hedging, political stability, the firm's resources and experience); make a recommendation with a clear condition.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying when you should divide in exchange-rate calculations — check the answer makes sense (a stronger pound should make a sterling price higher in dollars).
  • Using “off-shoring” and “outsourcing” interchangeably.
  • Confusing factors for a market (4.2.2) with factors for a production location (4.2.3) — read the question.
  • Assuming low wages mean low costs — productivity, transport and quality costs matter.
  • Ignoring the risks of international expansion: many famous retailers have retreated (Tesco from the US in 2013, Starbucks largely from Australia in 2008).

Quick recall

  • Push: saturated markets, competition; pull: economies of scale, risk spreading
  • Off-shoring = another country; outsourcing = another firm; PLC extended by selling in new markets
  • Market assessment: disposable income, ease of doing business, infrastructure, political stability, exchange rate
  • Location assessment: costs, skills, infrastructure, trade bloc, incentives, ease of doing business, stability, natural resources, return on investment
  • Global mergers/JVs: spread risk, enter markets/blocs, acquire brands/patents, secure resources, stay competitive
  • SPICED; from £ multiply, into £ divide; effect depends on elasticity, hedging, imported inputs
  • Competitive advantage: cost competitiveness or differentiation; skill shortages raise costs and cut productivity
