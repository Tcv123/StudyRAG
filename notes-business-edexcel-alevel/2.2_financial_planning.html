2.2 Financial Planning

Business — Edexcel (9BS0) — Theme 2: Managing business activities

Financial planning is about looking ahead: forecasting sales, understanding how costs behave as output changes, calculating how much must be sold to break even, and setting budgets to control spending. It is the most calculation-heavy topic in Theme 2 and regularly supplies 4-mark “calculate” questions on Paper 2 (revenue, costs, contribution, break-even, margin of safety, variances). The evaluation skill is to recognise that every one of these tools rests on forecasts and assumptions — a break-even point is only as reliable as the price and cost figures that go into it.

Sales forecast — a prediction of future sales volume or value over a given period.
Sales volume — the number of units sold.
Sales revenue — the value of sales: selling price × quantity sold.
Fixed costs — costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run (rent, salaries, insurance).
Variable costs — costs that change in direct proportion to output (raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages).
Contribution — the amount each unit sold contributes towards fixed costs and then profit: selling price − variable cost per unit.
Break-even point — the level of output at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss.
Margin of safety — the amount by which actual (or forecast) sales exceed break-even sales.
Budget — a financial plan for the future, setting targets for revenue and/or expenditure over a period.
Variance — the difference between a budgeted figure and the actual figure.

2.2.1 Sales forecasting

a) Purpose of sales forecasts

  • Production and operations planning — how much to produce, stock to hold and capacity required.
  • Staffing (workforce planning) — how many staff to recruit, when to take on seasonal workers.
  • Financial planning — the basis of cash-flow forecasts, budgets and break-even analysis; supports applications for finance.
  • Marketing — setting targets and planning promotions.

Methods range from extrapolating past trends (moving averages, covered quantitatively in 3.3) to market research and expert opinion.

b) Factors affecting sales forecasts

  • Consumer trends — changes in tastes and fashions (e.g. the shift to plant-based foods, the rise of weight-loss drugs affecting snack sales), which may not continue past patterns.
  • Economic variables — incomes, inflation, interest rates, unemployment, exchange rates, the business cycle. A product with a high YED will be especially sensitive (link to 1.2.5 and 2.5.1).
  • Actions of competitors — new entrants, price cuts, new products or promotional campaigns can take sales away unexpectedly.

c) Difficulties of sales forecasting

  • The future is uncertain; past data may not predict the future, especially in dynamic markets.
  • New businesses and new products have no past data to use.
  • External shocks (a pandemic, a heatwave, a supply crisis) cannot be predicted.
  • Research used for forecasting may be biased or based on small samples.
  • Over-optimism, particularly from entrepreneurs seeking finance.

Consequences of an inaccurate forecast: overestimates lead to excess stock, wasted production, unnecessary staff and cash-flow problems; underestimates lead to stock-outs, lost sales and disappointed customers.

2.2.2 Sales, revenue and costs

a) Sales volume and sales revenue

Sales revenue = Selling price × Quantity sold     Sales volume = Sales revenue ÷ Selling price

Worked example: a bakery sells 4,800 loaves a month at £2.50. Revenue = 4,800 × 2.50 = £12,000. If monthly revenue from cakes is £6,300 at £3.50 each, volume = 6,300 ÷ 3.50 = 1,800 cakes.

b) Fixed and variable costs

Total variable costs = Variable cost per unit × Output

Total costs = Fixed costs + Total variable costs

Profit = Total revenue − Total costs

Worked example: the bakery's fixed costs are £5,500 a month; each loaf costs £0.90 in variable costs. At 4,800 loaves: TVC = 4,800 × 0.90 = £4,320; TC = 5,500 + 4,320 = £9,820; profit = 12,000 − 9,820 = £2,180.

Some costs are semi-variable (e.g. a phone bill with a fixed line rental plus call charges). Fixed costs are fixed only in the short run and only within a range of output: expanding beyond current capacity requires new premises, creating a step in fixed costs. Average (unit) cost = total cost ÷ output = 9,820 ÷ 4,800 = £2.05 per loaf; it falls as output rises because fixed costs are spread over more units.

2.2.3 Break-even

a) Contribution

Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit

Total contribution = Contribution per unit × Units sold (or total revenue − total variable costs)

Profit = Total contribution − Fixed costs

b) and c) Break-even point

At break-even: Total fixed costs + Total variable costs = Total revenue

Using contribution: Break-even output = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit

Worked example. A company makes phone cases. Selling price £25; variable cost £10 per unit; fixed costs £30,000 per month; forecast sales 2,600 units per month.

  • Contribution per unit = 25 − 10 = £15
  • Break-even = 30,000 ÷ 15 = 2,000 units (break-even revenue = 2,000 × £25 = £50,000)
  • Check: at 2,000 units, TR = £50,000; TC = 30,000 + (2,000 × 10) = £50,000 ✓
  • Profit at 2,600 units = (2,600 × 15) − 30,000 = 39,000 − 30,000 = £9,000

What-if calculations:

  • Price rise to £28: contribution = £18; break-even = 30,000 ÷ 18 = 1,666.7, so 1,667 units (always round up — 1,666 units would still make a small loss).
  • Variable cost rises to £12: contribution = £13; break-even = 30,000 ÷ 13 = 2,307.7, so 2,308 units.
  • Target profit of £12,000: required output = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution = (30,000 + 12,000) ÷ 15 = 2,800 units.

d) Margin of safety

Margin of safety = Actual (or forecast) sales − Break-even sales

In the example: 2,600 − 2,000 = 600 units (or £15,000 of revenue). As a percentage of sales: 600 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = 23.1% — sales could fall by 23.1% before the business made a loss. Note that profit = margin of safety × contribution per unit (600 × £15 = £9,000).

e) Interpretation of break-even charts

Reading the chart: the fixed-cost line is horizontal; the total-cost line starts at the fixed-cost level (at zero output, total cost = fixed cost) and rises with variable costs; the total-revenue line starts at the origin. Where TR crosses TC is the break-even point. To the left the vertical gap between TC and TR is the loss; to the right it is the profit. The horizontal distance from break-even to actual sales is the margin of safety.

Effects of changes on the chart: a price rise makes the TR line steeper, so break-even falls; a rise in variable cost makes the TC line steeper, so break-even rises; a rise in fixed costs shifts the FC and TC lines up in parallel, so break-even rises.

f) Limitations of break-even analysis

  • Assumes all output is sold and there is no stock build-up.
  • Assumes a single selling price, whereas firms discount and sell at different prices to different customers.
  • Assumes variable costs per unit are constant; in reality bulk-buying discounts reduce them and overtime raises them.
  • Assumes fixed costs are fixed at all output levels, ignoring step changes.
  • Difficult to use for firms selling many products, where fixed costs must be allocated between products.
  • Only as good as the data: forecasts of sales, prices and costs may be wrong.
  • A static model: it shows a single point in time in a dynamic market.

Value despite limitations: it is quick and simple, useful for “what if” scenarios, an essential part of a business plan, and it shows how risky a venture is through the margin of safety.

Round break-even output up to the next whole unit. Always state units (“2,000 phone cases per month”), and for a margin-of-safety question give the figure in units and, if useful, as a percentage. In evaluation, a small margin of safety is the key sign of vulnerability — link it to the context (e.g. a new competitor opening nearby).

2.2.4 Budgets

a) Purpose of budgets

  • Planning — forces managers to think ahead about revenue and spending.
  • Control — actual performance can be compared with the budget and corrective action taken.
  • Coordination — ensures departments work towards the same objectives and do not overspend in total.
  • Motivation — budget holders have targets and responsibility (delegated budgets — links to Herzberg).
  • Allocation of resources — directs money to priorities.
  • Accountability — managers are responsible for their budgets.

Types include income (revenue) budgets, expenditure (cost) budgets and profit budgets (income minus expenditure).

c) Variance analysis

Variance = Actual figure − Budgeted figure

  • Favourable (F) — the variance increases profit: revenue higher than budgeted, or costs lower than budgeted.
  • Adverse (A) — the variance reduces profit: revenue lower than budgeted, or costs higher than budgeted.

Variances can also be expressed as a percentage of budget: revenue 4,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 8% adverse.

d) Difficulties of budgeting

  • Budgets are based on forecasts that may be inaccurate, especially in dynamic markets or for new businesses.
  • External changes (inflation, as in 2022–23 when energy and material costs rose sharply) can make budgets unrealistic quickly.
  • Inflexible budgets may prevent managers responding to opportunities.
  • Budget setting takes time; imposed budgets demotivate, while negotiated budgets may be padded (“budgetary slack”).
  • Departments may compete for resources, and managers may spend unused budget at year-end to avoid a cut next year.
  • Focus on hitting short-term budget targets can damage long-term investment.

Common mistakes

  • Calculating break-even as fixed costs ÷ selling price (it is ÷ contribution).
  • Rounding break-even down instead of up.
  • Labelling a lower-than-budgeted cost as adverse — lower costs are favourable.
  • Assuming a favourable variance is always good news.
  • Confusing contribution with profit: contribution must first cover fixed costs.
  • Drawing the TC line from the origin rather than from the fixed-cost level.

Quick recall

  • Sales forecasts: plan production, staff, finance; affected by consumer trends, economic variables, competitors; hard to get right
  • Revenue = price × quantity; TC = FC + (VC per unit × output)
  • Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit
  • Break-even = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit (round up)
  • Margin of safety = actual sales − break-even sales; profit = MoS × contribution per unit
  • Required output for a target profit = (FC + target profit) ÷ contribution
  • Break-even limitations: single price, constant VC, all output sold, fixed FC, forecasts
  • Budgets: plan, control, coordinate, motivate; historical versus zero-based
  • Variance = actual − budget; favourable raises profit, adverse lowers it
