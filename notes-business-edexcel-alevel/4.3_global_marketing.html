4.3 Global Marketing

Business — Edexcel (9BS0) — Theme 4: Global business

Once a business decides to sell abroad, it faces the central question of global marketing: how far should it standardise, and how far should it adapt? A single global approach brings economies of scale and a consistent brand; local adaptation meets different tastes, cultures, incomes and laws. This topic covers the strategic approaches (global, glocal, ethnocentric, polycentric, geocentric), how the 4Ps and Ansoff's Matrix apply internationally, global niche markets, and the cultural and social traps — language, meanings, translation and branding — that have embarrassed some of the world's biggest firms. It links directly to the marketing mix in Theme 1.3.

Global marketing — marketing a product in many countries using a strategy co-ordinated across markets.
Global marketing strategy (standardisation) — using essentially the same product, brand and promotion in all markets.
Glocalisation (global localisation) — “think global, act local”: a global brand and strategy adapted to suit local tastes, cultures and conditions.
Ethnocentric approach — a domestic orientation: the firm sells the same product overseas as at home, assuming what works domestically will work abroad.
Polycentric approach — an international orientation: each market is treated as distinct, with its own adapted marketing mix, often run by local managers.
Geocentric approach — a mixed orientation: the world is treated as one market with a global strategy, but with local adaptation where needed.
Global niche market — a small, specialised segment of a market that exists across many countries.
Cultural diversity — the existence of different values, beliefs, customs, languages and interests among groups of people across the world.

Different marketing approaches

Approach (spec wording)OrientationFeaturesSuits
Domestic / ethnocentricHome-country viewSame mix as at home; decisions made at head office; overseas sales seen as an extension of domestic salesFirms starting to export; products whose appeal is their national origin (Scotch whisky, British heritage brands). Risk: cultural blindness
International / polycentricHost-country viewEach country has its own tailored mix, often designed by local subsidiaries and managersMarkets with very different cultures and tastes, e.g. food and drink; high costs and no global economies of scale

Glocalisation and the geocentric approach are closely related — both combine global scale with local adaptation. What gets you into the higher levels is explaining which elements should be standardised (usually the brand, logo, core technology and quality) and which adapted (flavours, sizes, promotion, price) for the product in the extract, and why. Standardise what customers value for being the same everywhere; adapt what they value for being local.

Application of Ansoff's Matrix to global markets

StrategyGlobal applicationRisk
Market penetrationSelling more of existing products in existing overseas markets — more outlets, more promotion, competitive pricingLowest — the firm already knows the market

Entering a culturally distant market is riskier than Ansoff's simple “market development” label suggests, so the global version of the matrix should always be evaluated with the cultural and social factors in section 3.

2. Niche markets (4.3.2)

Cultural diversity

Groups of people across the globe have different interests and values shaped by religion, language, history, climate, income and lifestyle. But people with the same interests exist in every country. A niche too small to be profitable in one country can become substantial when aggregated globally — especially now that the internet and social media let firms reach dispersed customers cheaply.

Features of global niche markets

  • Small, specialised segments that exist across many countries — customers share interests across borders more than with their own compatriots.
  • Customers have specific needs and are often willing to pay a premium; demand is less price sensitive.
  • Fewer competitors, especially mass-market firms, which are not interested in small volumes.
  • Strong brand loyalty and word-of-mouth (online communities, specialist forums, social media).
  • Often reached through e-commerce and specialist distributors rather than mass retail.
  • Examples: luxury goods (Rolex, Hermès, Aston Martin); halal cosmetics and modest fashion; specialist outdoor and adventure equipment; K-pop and anime merchandise; high-end audio (Bowers & Wilkins); Morgan cars; organic baby food; vintage and collectable goods sold worldwide via eBay and Etsy.

Application and adaptation of the marketing mix to suit global niches

PGlobal niche approach
ProductHigh quality, specialised, authentic, often with a strong heritage or exclusive design; usually standardised because the niche's shared values cross borders; limited editions and customisation
PricePremium pricing reflecting exclusivity and lower price elasticity; price consistency across markets to protect brand image and prevent grey-market imports
PromotionTargeted rather than mass: specialist magazines and websites, social media influencers in the niche, sponsorship of niche events, word of mouth; consistent brand story worldwide
PlaceSelective or exclusive distribution: flagship stores in global cities, specialist retailers, the firm's own website shipping worldwide

Evaluation: global niches offer high margins and loyal customers, but volumes are small, the business is vulnerable if the niche shrinks or tastes change, and if the niche grows, large firms may enter and compete. Maintaining exclusivity while growing is the constant tension for luxury brands.

3. Cultural and social factors (4.3.3)

ConsiderationExplanationExamples
Cultural differencesValues, religion, customs, social norms, attitudes to time, family and gender, business etiquette, colours and numbers with symbolic meaningWhite is associated with mourning in parts of East Asia; the number 4 is considered unlucky in China and Japan; Starbucks closed most of its Australian stores in 2008 after misjudging the country's established café culture

Unintended meaningsBrand or product names that mean something different, or offensive, in another languageMitsubishi Pajero renamed Montero in Spanish-speaking markets; the Chevrolet Nova in Latin America is often cited (“no va” means “doesn't go”), though the story is largely a myth — the car sold well, a reminder to check examples
Inappropriate/inaccurate translationsSlogans translated literally can become meaningless or embarrassingHSBC dropped its “Assume nothing” slogan in 2009 after it was reported to translate in some markets as “Do nothing”, launching a costly global rebrand; KFC's “finger lickin' good” is widely reported to have been rendered in China as “eat your fingers off”
Inappropriate branding and promotionImagery, humour or messages that offend local values; ignoring religious or political sensitivitiesDolce & Gabbana's 2018 Chinese advertising, showing a model struggling to eat Italian food with chopsticks, was condemned as racist; its Shanghai show was cancelled and Chinese retailers dropped its products

Consequences and how firms avoid mistakes

  • Consequences: lost sales, damaged reputation that spreads instantly on social media, cost of withdrawing and relaunching products and campaigns, possible boycotts and legal action.
  • Avoiding mistakes: local market research and focus groups; local managers and marketing agencies; professional translation and back-translation; testing names and campaigns before launch; joint ventures with local partners; cultural-awareness training for staff.
Cultural mistakes make memorable examples, but don't just tell the story — analyse the business consequence (lost revenue, rebranding costs, damaged brand equity) and evaluate how serious and lasting it was. Many firms recover quickly from a translation error; some, like Dolce & Gabbana in China, suffer lasting damage in a key market. The difference usually depends on whether the mistake appeared to reflect genuine disrespect.

How Edexcel rewards this topic

  • Knowledge: precise meaning of glocalisation, ethnocentric, polycentric and geocentric; features of a global niche market.
  • Application: identify the firm's current approach from the extract (e.g. identical products everywhere = ethnocentric/global), the target countries and their cultural features.
  • Analysis: adaptation → better fit with local tastes → higher demand; but adaptation → lost economies of scale → higher unit costs → lower margins.
  • Evaluation: the balance between standardisation and adaptation depends on the product (luxury and technology products standardise well; food adapts), the cultural distance between markets, the firm's resources, and the strength of local competitors.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up polycentric (each country different) and geocentric (global with local adaptation).
  • Assuming adaptation is always better — it is costly, and for some products the global identity is the selling point.
  • Treating a global niche as a small local market — its defining feature is that it exists across many countries.
  • Applying the 4Ps generically: every point should refer to the specific country or product in the extract.

Quick recall

  • Global strategy (standardise) vs glocalisation (think global, act local)
  • Ethnocentric (domestic) — polycentric (international, country-by-country) — geocentric (mixed, global with adaptation)
  • 4Ps adapted for product features, price levels, promotion language/media, place channels
  • Global Ansoff: market development = new countries; risk rises with cultural distance
  • Global niche: small specialised segment across many countries; premium, targeted, selective distribution
  • Cultural/social factors: cultural differences, tastes, language, unintended meanings, poor translations, inappropriate branding and promotion
