CP1: Measure the Molar Volume of a Gas

Background Theory

The molar volume of a gas is the volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a stated temperature and pressure (about 24.0 dm3 mol−1 at room temperature and pressure). In this practical, weighed samples of calcium carbonate react with an excess of ethanoic acid and the carbon dioxide is collected and measured. Because each mole of CaCO3 gives one mole of CO2, the volume of gas per mole of carbonate gives the molar volume.

CaCO3(s) + 2CH3COOH(aq) → (CH3COO)2Ca(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Ethanoic acid (a weak acid) is used rather than a strong acid so that the reaction is slower, giving time to replace the bung before much gas escapes.

Apparatus

  • Boiling tube with bung and delivery tube
  • 100 cm3 measuring cylinder, inverted and full of water in a trough (or a 100 cm3 gas syringe)
  • About 30 cm3 of 1 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid, powdered calcium carbonate
  • Small test tube for weighing, balance reading to 0.01 g or better, clamp stand

Method

  1. Put 30 cm3 of ethanoic acid in the boiling tube and connect the delivery tube to the inverted, water-filled measuring cylinder.
  2. Weigh a small test tube containing about 0.05 g of calcium carbonate.
  3. Remove the bung, tip the carbonate into the acid, and replace the bung immediately.
  4. When fizzing stops, read the volume of gas collected.
  5. Reweigh the empty test tube; the mass of CaCO3 used is the difference.
  6. Repeat with fresh acid, increasing the mass of carbonate by about 0.05 g each time, up to about 0.40 g (larger masses would give more gas than the cylinder holds).

Analysis

  • Calculate moles of CaCO3 = mass / 100.1 for each run.
  • Plot volume of CO2 (y-axis) against moles of CaCO3 (x-axis) and draw a line of best fit.
  • The gradient is the molar volume (convert cm3 mol−1 to dm3 mol−1 by dividing by 1000).
  • If the line does not pass through the origin, a constant amount of gas has been lost (or gained) in every run.

Safety

  • Ethanoic acid is an irritant at this concentration — wear eye protection.
  • Check the delivery tube is not blocked so that pressure cannot build up.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Gas lost before the bung is replacedSystematic (volume too low)Hang the carbonate in a small tube inside the flask and tip it over after sealing
CO2 dissolves slightly in the water in the trough and in the acidSystematic (volume too low)Use a gas syringe instead of collecting over water
Low precision of the balance for small massesRandom (large % uncertainty)Use a 3 d.p. balance; weigh by difference
Reading the measuring cylinder scaleRandomRead at eye level; use a gas syringe with finer graduations
Exam Tip: Using a graph of volume against moles from several runs is better than a single measurement: it averages out random error, and a non-zero intercept reveals a systematic loss. The acid must be in excess so that the carbonate is the limiting reagent in every run. Be ready to calculate percentage uncertainties for the mass and the volume and to combine them.

CP2: Prepare a Standard Solution from a Solid Acid and Use It to Find the Concentration of Sodium Hydroxide

Background Theory

A standard solution has an accurately known concentration. It is made by dissolving an accurately weighed mass of a pure, stable solid in water and making the solution up to an exact volume in a volumetric flask. A solid acid such as sulfamic acid (NH2SO3H, a monoprotic acid, M = 97.1 g mol−1) is suitable because it is solid, stable in air and not hygroscopic. Sodium hydroxide cannot itself be used to make a standard solution because the solid absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air, so its concentration must be found by titration against a standard acid.

NH2SO3H(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NH2SO3Na(aq) + H2O(l)

Apparatus

  • Weighing bottle, balance (2 or 3 d.p.), 250 cm3 beaker, glass rod, funnel, wash bottle of distilled water
  • 250 cm3 volumetric flask with stopper
  • 25.0 cm3 volumetric pipette and filler, 50 cm3 burette, conical flasks, white tile
  • Phenolphthalein or methyl orange indicator

Method: Making the Standard Solution

  1. Weigh the weighing bottle containing about 2.5 g of sulfamic acid. Tip the solid into a beaker and reweigh the bottle; the mass used is the difference.
  2. Dissolve the solid in about 100 cm3 of distilled water, stirring with a glass rod.
  3. Transfer the solution through a funnel into the volumetric flask. Rinse the beaker, rod and funnel with distilled water and add all rinsings to the flask.
  4. Make up to the mark with distilled water, adding the last few drops with a dropping pipette so the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line at eye level.
  5. Stopper and invert the flask several times to mix.

Method: Titration

  1. Rinse the pipette with sodium hydroxide solution, then pipette 25.0 cm3 into a conical flask and add a few drops of indicator.
  2. Rinse the burette with the sulfamic acid solution, fill it, and make sure the jet is full with no air bubbles.
  3. Do a rough titration, then accurate titrations adding the acid dropwise near the end-point while swirling.
  4. Repeat until you have concordant titres (within 0.10 cm3) and calculate the mean.

Key Practical Points

  • Burette readings to 2 decimal places (nearest 0.05 cm3)
  • Rinse the burette with the solution it will contain and the pipette with the solution it will deliver; rinse the conical flask with distilled water only
  • Use a white tile to see colour changes clearly
  • Indicator: phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in alkali) or methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in alkali) both work for a strong acid with a strong base; the specification expects you to use both

Calculation

Concentration of acid = (mass / 97.1) / 0.250 mol dm−3
Moles of acid in mean titre = concentration x (titre / 1000)
Moles of NaOH = moles of acid (1 : 1 ratio)
[NaOH] = moles of NaOH / 0.0250 dm3

Safety

  • Sodium hydroxide solution is corrosive to eyes; sulfamic acid is an irritant. Wear eye protection.
  • Use a pipette filler; never pipette by mouth.
Exam Tip: A common question asks why a wet conical flask does NOT cause an error: the extra water does not change the moles of alkali already in the flask, so the same volume of acid is still needed. But a wet pipette or wet burette WOULD cause an error because the water dilutes the solution, changing its effective concentration. You may also be asked why NaOH is not used as a primary standard (it absorbs water and CO2 from the air).

CP3: Find the Concentration of a Solution of Hydrochloric Acid

Background Theory

This titration builds on CP2 and focuses on measurement uncertainty. The hydrochloric acid is too concentrated to titrate directly, so it is first diluted accurately (for example, 25.0 cm3 made up to 250 cm3, a tenfold dilution). The diluted acid is then titrated against sodium hydroxide of known concentration.

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)

Apparatus

  • 25.0 cm3 volumetric pipette and filler, 250 cm3 volumetric flask
  • 50 cm3 burette, conical flasks, white tile, indicator
  • Sodium hydroxide solution of known concentration (e.g. about 0.1 mol dm−3)

Method

  1. Pipette 25.0 cm3 of the hydrochloric acid into the volumetric flask, make up to the mark with distilled water, stopper and invert to mix.
  2. Pipette 25.0 cm3 of the diluted acid into a conical flask and add indicator.
  3. Titrate with the sodium hydroxide from the burette to the end-point.
  4. Repeat to obtain concordant titres and calculate the mean.
  5. Calculate the concentration of the diluted acid, then multiply by the dilution factor (10) to get the concentration of the original acid.

Measurement Uncertainty

ApparatusTypical uncertainty% uncertainty example
Burette (per reading)±0.05 cm3Two readings per titre: (2 x 0.05 / 24.00) x 100 = 0.42%
25.0 cm3 pipette±0.06 cm3(0.06 / 25.0) x 100 = 0.24%
250 cm3 volumetric flask±0.3 cm3(0.3 / 250) x 100 = 0.12%

The total percentage uncertainty is found by adding the percentage uncertainties of each measurement. Percentage error compares your result with an accepted value: (|experimental − accepted| / accepted) x 100. If the percentage error is larger than the total percentage uncertainty, there must be other (usually procedural) errors.

Safety

  • Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide are corrosive or irritant depending on concentration — wear eye protection.
Exam Tip: Do not forget the dilution factor in the final step. To reduce the percentage uncertainty in the titre, use a larger titre (e.g. more dilute NaOH or a larger sample). Know the difference between measurement uncertainty (the limit of the apparatus) and measurement error (difference from the true value).

CP4: Investigation of the Rates of Hydrolysis of Some Halogenoalkanes

Background Theory

Halogenoalkanes are hydrolysed by water to form alcohols and halide ions. Ethanol is used as a common solvent because halogenoalkanes do not mix with water. The halide ions released react with silver ions to give a precipitate, so the time for a precipitate to appear measures the rate of hydrolysis.

R–X + H2O → R–OH + H+ + X−     Ag+(aq) + X−(aq) → AgX(s)

Two comparisons are made: (1) different halogens (1-chloro-, 1-bromo- and 1-iodobutane), and (2) primary, secondary and tertiary structures (1-bromobutane, 2-bromobutane and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane).

Apparatus

  • Water bath at about 50 degrees C, test tubes with loose bungs, test-tube rack
  • Ethanol, aqueous silver nitrate solution, the six halogenoalkanes, stop clock

Method

  1. Put 5 cm3 of ethanol in each of three test tubes and add four drops of a different halogenoalkane to each. Loosely bung and stand them in the water bath.
  2. Put 5 cm3 of silver nitrate solution in three more tubes and warm them in the same bath.
  3. When all the tubes are at the bath temperature, add a tube of silver nitrate solution to one halogenoalkane solution and start the clock.
  4. Stop the clock when the mixture first turns cloudy. Record the colour of the precipitate.
  5. Repeat for the other two halogenoalkanes, then repeat the whole procedure with the primary, secondary and tertiary bromoalkanes.

Expected Results

ComparisonOrder of rate (fastest first)Explanation
Different halogensIodo > bromo > chloroC–I bond enthalpy is lowest, so it breaks most easily. Bond enthalpy matters more than bond polarity here.
Different structuresTertiary > secondary > primaryTertiary halogenoalkanes react by an SN1 mechanism via a relatively stable tertiary carbocation
Precipitate coloursAgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellowConfirms which halide has been released

Safety

  • Halogenoalkanes and ethanol are flammable and harmful — no naked flames; use a water bath.
  • Silver nitrate solution is corrosive or irritant and stains skin — wear eye protection and gloves.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Deciding when the solution first becomes cloudyRandomPlace tubes against a dark background with a printed mark behind; same observer each time
Different temperatures for different tubesSystematicPre-warm all solutions in the same bath and keep tubes in the bath during the reaction
Drops of different sizeRandomUse a graduated pipette to add equal volumes
1-chlorobutane may not react in a reasonable time—Set a cut-off time and record as "no precipitate within x minutes"
Exam Tip: Water, not hydroxide ions, is the nucleophile here, so the reaction is slow enough to time. The rate depends on C–X bond enthalpy, not polarity: C–Cl is the most polar bond but reacts slowest. Ethanol is a common solvent, so the halogenoalkane and the aqueous silver nitrate mix in one phase. Rate is proportional to 1/time.

CP5: The Oxidation of Ethanol

Background Theory

Ethanol, a primary alcohol, is oxidised by acidified potassium dichromate(VI). The orange Cr2O72− ions are reduced to green Cr3+ ions. The product depends on the conditions:

  • Distil the product off as it forms (alcohol in excess): ethanal (b.p. 21 degrees C) is removed before it can be oxidised further.
  • Heat under reflux with excess oxidising agent, then distil: ethanoic acid (b.p. 118 degrees C) is formed.
CH3CH2OH + [O] → CH3CHO + H2O     CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O

Apparatus

  • Pear-shaped or round-bottomed flask, Liebig condenser, still head, thermometer, receiver flask
  • Anti-bumping granules, water bath or electric heater, ice bath
  • Potassium dichromate(VI), dilute and concentrated sulfuric acid, ethanol

Method: Making Ethanoic Acid (Reflux Then Distil)

  1. Put the acidified dichromate(VI) solution in the flask with anti-bumping granules, and cool in an ice bath.
  2. Add ethanol a few drops at a time with swirling (the reaction is exothermic).
  3. Fit a vertical condenser and heat under reflux for about 15–20 minutes. The vapour condenses and returns to the flask, so volatile materials are not lost and oxidation goes to completion.
  4. Rearrange the apparatus for distillation and collect the liquid that distils at about 118 degrees C.
  5. Test the distillate: it turns blue litmus red and fizzes with sodium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate.

Method: Making Ethanal (Distil Immediately)

  1. Set up for distillation with the receiver cooled in ice.
  2. Warm the acidified dichromate(VI) and add ethanol dropwise, so the ethanal distils out as soon as it forms.
  3. Test the distillate with Fehling's or Benedict's solution (blue to red precipitate) or Tollens' reagent (silver mirror).

Safety

  • Potassium dichromate(VI) is toxic, carcinogenic and oxidising — wear gloves and eye protection.
  • Concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive; ethanol and ethanal are highly flammable — heat with a water bath or electric heater, never a naked flame.
  • Never heat a closed system: the top of the reflux condenser must be open.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Ethanal escapes as vapourSystematic (low yield)Cool the receiver in ice; keep joints sealed
Oxidation incomplete when ethanoic acid is wantedSystematic (product contains ethanal/ethanol)Use excess oxidising agent and reflux for longer
Distillate collected over too wide a temperature rangeSystematic (impure product)Collect only within a few degrees of the boiling temperature
Exam Tip: Be able to draw and label both reflux (vertical condenser, open top) and distillation (sloping condenser, thermometer bulb at the side-arm) apparatus. Explain the choice: distil immediately to stop at the aldehyde; reflux with excess oxidant to reach the carboxylic acid. Water enters the condenser at the bottom. The colour change orange → green shows the alcohol has been oxidised.

CP6: Chlorination of 2-Methylpropan-2-ol Using Concentrated Hydrochloric Acid

Background Theory

This core practical tests the techniques used to prepare and purify a liquid organic product. Many organic reactions require prolonged heating to go to completion, but the reagents and products are often volatile and would be lost if heated in an open flask. Reflux solves this: a vertical condenser is attached to the flask so that vapours condense and drip back into the reaction mixture, allowing continuous heating without loss of material. After the reaction is complete, the product is separated by distillation.

Method: 2-Chloro-2-methylpropane from 2-Methylpropan-2-ol

The tertiary alcohol reacts with concentrated hydrochloric acid at room temperature, so this preparation needs no reflux:

(CH3)3COH + HCl → (CH3)3CCl + H2O
  1. In a fume cupboard, put the 2-methylpropan-2-ol and an excess of concentrated hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel.
  2. Stopper and shake for about 20 minutes, inverting and opening the tap frequently to release pressure.
  3. Let the layers separate. The halogenoalkane is less dense than the aqueous layer, so it forms the upper layer. Run off and discard the lower aqueous layer.
  4. Wash the organic layer with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to remove remaining acid (vent often: CO2 is produced), then with water. Discard the aqueous layer each time.
  5. Run the organic layer into a dry flask and dry it with an anhydrous salt (e.g. anhydrous sodium sulfate) until the liquid is clear.
  6. Decant into a clean flask and distil, collecting the fraction boiling at about 50–52 degrees C.

The more general techniques below (reflux, washing, drying, redistillation) are also examined in this core practical and in CP5.

General Technique: Reflux

  1. Place reagents in a round-bottomed flask. Add anti-bumping granules.
  2. Attach a vertical condenser (open at the top — not sealed) to the flask. Water flows in at the bottom and out at the top.
  3. Heat the mixture gently (water bath for flammable liquids, or electric heating mantle). Vapours rise, condense in the condenser, and return to the flask. Continue for the required time (e.g. 30–60 minutes).
  4. After reflux, rearrange the apparatus for distillation: tilt the condenser to an angle, connect to a collecting flask, and distil the product at its boiling point.

Purification Steps (After Distillation)

  1. Washing: Use a separating funnel to wash the organic product with aqueous solutions to remove impurities. For example, wash with NaHCO3 (removes acidic impurities) or dilute acid (removes basic impurities). Shake, vent, and drain off the aqueous layer.
  2. Drying: Add an anhydrous drying agent (e.g. anhydrous MgSO4, CaCl2, or Na2SO4) to the organic layer. Leave for 10–15 minutes, swirling occasionally. The drying agent absorbs residual water. Filter off the drying agent by gravity filtration.
  3. Redistillation: Distil the dried product, collecting the fraction at the correct boiling point (±2 degrees C). This gives the purest product.

Purity Testing

  • Boiling point: A pure liquid has a sharp, constant boiling point matching the literature value. Impurities change the boiling temperature and make the liquid boil over a range.
  • Refractive index: Measured using a refractometer. Each pure liquid has a characteristic refractive index. Impurities alter the value.
  • GC (gas chromatography): A pure substance shows a single peak. Impurities appear as additional peaks.

Safety

  • Organic liquids are flammable — use electric heating (water bath or heating mantle). No naked flames.
  • Never heat a sealed system — the condenser must be open at the top during reflux.
  • When using a separating funnel: vent regularly by inverting and opening the tap to release gas pressure build-up.
  • Some drying agents (anhydrous CaCl2) react with alcohols and cannot be used for drying them.
Exam Tip: Reflux = heating without losing vapour (condenser vertical, vapour returns to flask). Distillation = separating by boiling point (condenser angled, vapour collected). The thermometer bulb should be at the side-arm outlet during distillation. When using a separating funnel, the organic layer may be on top (less dense than water) or bottom (if denser, e.g. DCM). Know which layer to keep! For drying: anhydrous Na2SO4 is a general-purpose drying agent suitable for most organic liquids.

CP8: Determine the Enthalpy Change of a Reaction Using Hess's Law

Background Theory

Some enthalpy changes cannot be measured directly. The thermal decomposition of potassium hydrogencarbonate is an example: it needs continuous heating, so its temperature change cannot be measured with a simple calorimeter.

2KHCO3(s) → K2CO3(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)     ΔrH = ?

Instead, the enthalpy changes of two reactions that can be measured are found, and Hess's law is used to combine them. Both solids react quickly with excess dilute hydrochloric acid:

  • Reaction 1: KHCO3(s) + HCl(aq) → KCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)   (ΔH1, endothermic: temperature falls)
  • Reaction 2: K2CO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)   (ΔH2, exothermic: temperature rises)
ΔrH = 2ΔH1 − ΔH2

Apparatus

  • Expanded polystyrene cup in a beaker (for stability), lid, thermometer reading to 0.1 or 0.2 degrees C
  • 50 cm3 measuring cylinder or burette, weighing bottles, balance
  • About 2 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid (in excess), potassium hydrogencarbonate, anhydrous potassium carbonate

Method

  1. Put 30 cm3 of the acid in the polystyrene cup and record its temperature every minute for a few minutes until steady.
  2. Weigh about 3 g of potassium hydrogencarbonate in a weighing bottle.
  3. Add the solid to the acid at a recorded time, stir, and keep recording the temperature every half-minute or minute for several minutes.
  4. Reweigh the empty weighing bottle to find the exact mass added.
  5. Repeat with fresh acid and about 3 g of anhydrous potassium carbonate.
  6. For each reaction, plot temperature against time and extrapolate the cooling (or warming) line back to the time of mixing to find the corrected ΔT.

Calculation

q = m c ΔT  (m = mass of acid solution, taking density 1.00 g cm−3; c = 4.18 J g−1 K−1)
ΔH = −q / n, where n = moles of the solid (the acid is in excess). Include the sign: positive for reaction 1 (temperature fell), negative for reaction 2.
Then ΔrH = 2ΔH1 − ΔH2 (the value is positive: the decomposition is endothermic).

Safety

  • Hydrochloric acid at this concentration is an irritant — wear eye protection.
  • Add the solids gradually if the cup is small, as the effervescence can cause the mixture to froth over.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement

Acid spray lost with the escaping CO2SystematicAdd solid in portions; loose lid
Small ΔT with a thermometer reading to 0.5 degrees CRandom (large % uncertainty)Use a 0.1 degrees C thermometer or temperature probe; use more solid

Exam Tip: Draw the Hess cycle with 2KHCO3 + 2HCl and K2CO3 + H2O + CO2 + 2HCl at the top, and 2KCl + 2H2O + 2CO2 at the bottom. Remember to double ΔH1, because the equation uses 2 mol of KHCO3. Give the sign and units (kJ mol−1) in your final answer. The acid must be in excess so that all of the solid reacts.

CP9: Finding the Ka Value for a Weak Acid

Background Theory

For a weak acid HA ⇌ H+ + A−, Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA]. When exactly half of the acid has been neutralised by a strong base, [HA] = [A−], so these terms cancel and Ka = [H+]. Therefore at half-neutralisation:

pH = pKa     Ka = 10−pH

In this practical, ethanoic acid is titrated with sodium hydroxide to find the volume needed for full neutralisation. An identical volume of acid is then half-neutralised, and the pH of the resulting buffer is measured.

Apparatus

  • Calibrated pH meter (buffer solutions for calibration)
  • 25.0 cm3 pipette and filler, 50 cm3 burette, conical flasks, beakers
  • About 0.1 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid and sodium hydroxide, phenolphthalein indicator

Method

  1. Calibrate the pH meter using buffer solutions of known pH.
  2. Pipette 25.0 cm3 of ethanoic acid into a conical flask, add phenolphthalein, and titrate with NaOH to the first permanent pink colour. Repeat for concordant results; call the mean titre V.
  3. Pipette another 25.0 cm3 of the acid into a beaker and add exactly V/2 of NaOH from the burette (no indicator). Stir.
  4. Measure the pH of this half-neutralised solution. Rinse the electrode with distilled water between readings.
  5. Alternative method: add exactly V of NaOH to a 25.0 cm3 portion of acid (fully neutralised), then add a further 25.0 cm3 of the acid. This mixture also contains equal amounts of HA and A−.
  6. Calculate Ka = 10−pH.

Safety

  • Sodium hydroxide is corrosive to eyes; ethanoic acid is an irritant — wear eye protection.
  • pH electrodes are fragile; keep them in storage solution when not in use.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
pH meter not calibratedSystematicCalibrate with at least two buffers either side of the expected pH
Not exactly half-neutralisedSystematicDeliver V/2 from a burette; use the "add another equal portion of acid" method
Temperature different from 25 degrees CSystematic (Ka depends on temperature)Record the temperature and quote Ka at that temperature
Reading taken before it has stabilisedRandomStir and wait until the reading is steady
Exam Tip: Explain why pH = pKa at half-neutralisation: [HA] = [A−], so they cancel in the Ka expression. The same idea lets you read pKa from a weak acid–strong base titration curve at half the equivalence volume. For ethanoic acid, expect pKa of about 4.8 (Ka about 1.7 x 10−5 mol dm−3).

CP10: Investigating Some Electrochemical Cells

Background Theory

An electrochemical cell is made by joining two half-cells with a salt bridge and connecting the electrodes through a high-resistance voltmeter. Each half-cell contains an oxidised and a reduced form of an element: a metal dipping into a solution of its ions (e.g. Cu/Cu2+), or a platinum electrode in a solution containing two ions of the same element (e.g. Fe3+/Fe2+). The measured cell e.m.f. is compared with the value calculated from standard electrode potentials.

Eocell = Eo(right-hand, reduction) − Eo(left-hand, oxidation)

For example, Zn(s)|Zn2+(aq)||Cu2+(aq)|Cu(s): Eocell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V.

Apparatus

  • Small beakers, strips of copper, zinc, iron and other metals, emery paper
  • 1.0 mol dm−3 solutions of the metal ions (e.g. CuSO4, ZnSO4); a solution containing both Fe2+ and Fe3+ with a platinum electrode
  • Salt bridge: strip of filter paper soaked in saturated potassium nitrate solution
  • High-resistance voltmeter, leads and crocodile clips

Method

  1. Clean each metal strip with emery paper to remove the oxide layer.
  2. Set up each half-cell by standing the electrode in about 50 cm3 of the solution of its ions.
  3. Connect two half-cells with a fresh salt bridge (made just before use) and connect the electrodes to the voltmeter.
  4. Record the e.m.f. and note which electrode is the positive terminal.
  5. Repeat for different combinations of half-cells, and compare each measured value with the value calculated from the data booklet.

Safety

  • Copper(II) sulfate and zinc sulfate are harmful and irritant — wear eye protection and wash hands.
  • Potassium nitrate is an oxidising agent; keep it away from flammables.
  • Dispose of metal ion solutions as instructed, not down the sink.

CP11: Redox Titration (Iron(II) with Manganate(VII))

Background Theory

Potassium manganate(VII) is a strong oxidising agent in acid solution. It oxidises iron(II) ions to iron(III) ions and is itself reduced to almost colourless Mn2+. A typical use is to find the percentage of iron in iron tablets.

MnO4− + 8H+ + 5Fe2+ → Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O

No indicator is needed: the manganate(VII) is self-indicating. The end-point is the first permanent pale pink colour, showing a slight excess of MnO4−.

Apparatus

  • Iron tablets, mortar and pestle, balance, beaker, 250 cm3 volumetric flask
  • 25.0 cm3 pipette and filler, 50 cm3 burette, conical flasks, white tile
  • About 0.01 mol dm−3 potassium manganate(VII) solution, 1 mol dm−3 sulfuric acid

Method

  1. Weigh several iron tablets, grind them, and dissolve the powder in dilute sulfuric acid (a little undissolved coating is normal).
  2. Transfer the solution and all washings to a 250 cm3 volumetric flask and make up to the mark with dilute sulfuric acid or distilled water. Mix well.
  3. Pipette 25.0 cm3 into a conical flask and add about 10 cm3 of dilute sulfuric acid.
  4. Titrate with the manganate(VII) solution until the first permanent pale pink colour appears.
  5. Repeat for concordant titres.

Calculation

Moles MnO4− = c x (mean titre / 1000)
Moles Fe2+ in 25.0 cm3 = 5 x moles MnO4−
Moles Fe2+ in 250 cm3 = 10 x that value
Mass of iron = moles x 55.8; % iron = (mass of iron / mass of tablets) x 100

Safety

  • Potassium manganate(VII) is oxidising and harmful and stains skin; sulfuric acid is an irritant — wear eye protection.

CP12: The Preparation of a Transition Metal Complex

Background Theory

Adding concentrated ammonia to copper(II) sulfate solution causes ligand exchange: four water ligands are replaced by ammonia, giving the deep blue tetraamminecopper(II) complex. The solid complex, tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate-1-water, is much less soluble in ethanol than in water, so adding ethanol makes it crystallise out.

CuSO4·5H2O + 4NH3 → [Cu(NH3)4]SO4·H2O + 4H2O

Apparatus

  • Test tube, balance, hot water bath, graduated pipettes, small beaker, ice bath
  • Buchner funnel and flask (vacuum filtration), filter papers
  • Copper(II) sulfate-5-water, concentrated ammonia solution, ethanol

Method

  1. Weigh about 1.5 g of copper(II) sulfate-5-water into a test tube (weigh by difference).
  2. Add about 4 cm3 of water and warm in a hot water bath, stirring, to dissolve.
  3. In a fume cupboard, add about 2 cm3 of concentrated ammonia solution while stirring. A pale blue precipitate forms first, then dissolves to give a deep blue solution.
  4. Pour the solution into about 6 cm3 of ethanol in a beaker, mix, and cool in an ice bath until dark blue crystals form.
  5. Collect the crystals by vacuum filtration. Rinse the tube with cold ethanol onto the funnel, then wash the crystals with a little cold ethanol.
  6. Dry the crystals between filter papers and weigh them.

Calculation

Moles CuSO4·5H2O = mass / 249.7
Theoretical mass of product = moles x 245.8 (M of [Cu(NH3)4]SO4·H2O)
% yield = (actual mass / theoretical mass) x 100

Safety

  • Concentrated ammonia is corrosive and gives off a toxic, choking gas — use a fume cupboard, gloves and eye protection.
  • Ethanol is highly flammable — no naked flames.
  • Copper(II) sulfate is harmful — avoid skin contact.

CP13a: Rates of Reaction — Following the Iodine–Propanone Reaction by a Titrimetric Method

Background Theory

Propanone reacts with iodine in acid solution. The acid is a catalyst:

CH3COCH3 + I2 → CH3COCH2I + H+ + I−   (H+ catalyst)

Samples are removed at intervals, the reaction in each sample is stopped (quenched), and the iodine remaining is found by titration with sodium thiosulfate. Propanone and acid are in large excess, so only [I2] changes significantly. The shape of the [I2]–time graph gives the order with respect to iodine.

Apparatus

  • Beaker, stop clock, 10 cm3 pipette and filler, conical flasks, burette
  • 1 mol dm−3 propanone, 1 mol dm−3 sulfuric acid, 0.02 mol dm−3 iodine solution
  • Sodium hydrogencarbonate (to quench), 0.01 mol dm−3 sodium thiosulfate, starch indicator

Method

  1. Mix 25 cm3 of propanone solution with 25 cm3 of sulfuric acid in a beaker.
  2. Add 50 cm3 of iodine solution, starting the clock at the moment of mixing, and swirl.
  3. Pipette out a 10 cm3 sample into a conical flask and add a spatula of sodium hydrogencarbonate. Record the exact time of quenching (leave the clock running).
  4. Titrate the iodine in the sample with sodium thiosulfate, adding starch near the end-point (blue-black → colourless).
  5. Take further samples about every 3 minutes and treat each in the same way.

Analysis

  • The titre is proportional to [I2] remaining. Plot titre (or [I2]) against time.
  • A straight line with constant negative gradient shows the rate does not depend on [I2], so the reaction is zero order with respect to iodine.
  • Further experiments varying [propanone] and [H+] show the reaction is first order in each: rate = k[CH3COCH3][H+]. Iodine is therefore not involved in the rate-determining step.

Safety

  • Propanone is highly flammable; iodine solution is harmful and stains; sulfuric acid is an irritant — wear eye protection, no naked flames.
  • Iodopropanone formed is a lachrymator (irritates eyes) — work in a well-ventilated area.

Error Analysis

Source of ErrorTypeImprovement
Time recorded at the wrong momentSystematicRecord the time when the quenching solid is added, not when the sample is drawn
Reaction continues while samplingRandomQuench immediately; practise the pipetting step
Starch added too earlySystematic (end-point unclear)Add starch only when the solution is pale yellow
Exam Tip: Sodium hydrogencarbonate quenches the reaction by neutralising the acid catalyst. A straight-line concentration–time graph means zero order; a curve with constant half-life means first order. Because iodine is zero order, it must react after the rate-determining step. Be ready to propose a mechanism consistent with rate = k[propanone][H+].

CP13b: Rates of Reaction — Investigating a Clock Reaction (Iodine Clock)

Background Theory

In the Harcourt–Esson iodine clock, hydrogen peroxide oxidises iodide ions in acid solution:

H2O2 + 2H+ + 2I− → I2 + 2H2O

A small, fixed amount of sodium thiosulfate is included. It immediately turns any iodine formed back into iodide (I2 + 2S2O32− → 2I− + S4O62−). When all the thiosulfate is used up, free iodine appears and reacts with starch to give a sudden blue-black colour. The time t is the time for a fixed amount of reaction, so the initial rate is proportional to 1/t.

Apparatus

  • Beakers or boiling tubes, burettes or graduated pipettes for each solution, stop clock, white tile
  • Solutions of potassium iodide, sulfuric acid, sodium thiosulfate, starch and hydrogen peroxide; distilled water

Method

  1. In a beaker, mix measured volumes of sulfuric acid, starch, sodium thiosulfate, potassium iodide and water.
  2. Add a measured volume of hydrogen peroxide, start the clock and stir.
  3. Stop the clock when the blue-black colour appears.
  4. Repeat, changing the volume of one reactant (e.g. KI) and adding water so the total volume stays the same. Keep all other volumes and the temperature constant.
  5. Repeat for each reactant in turn to find the order with respect to each.

Determining Order of Reaction

Straight line through the originFirst order
Upward curve; straight line when 1/t is plotted against [X]2Second order

Alternatively, plot log(1/t) against log[X]: the gradient equals the order.

Safety

  • Hydrogen peroxide and sulfuric acid are irritants at these concentrations — wear eye protection.

Exam Tip: The thiosulfate must be the limiting reagent in small amount, so the colour appears after only a small fraction of the reaction: 1/t then approximates the initial rate. CP14: Finding the Activation Energy of a Reaction

Background Theory

The Arrhenius equation links the rate constant to temperature:

k = Ae−Ea/RT   →   ln k = −Ea/(RT) + ln A

Pearson's version of this practical uses the reaction between bromide ions and bromate(V) ions in acid solution:

BrO3−(aq) + 5Br−(aq) + 6H+(aq) → 3Br2(aq) + 3H2O(l)

A small, fixed amount of phenol is added. It removes the bromine as fast as it forms:

C6H5OH + 3Br2 → C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr

When all the phenol has reacted, the bromine that is still being produced bleaches the methyl red indicator, so its red colour disappears. The time t is the time for the same fixed amount of reaction at every temperature, so k is proportional to 1/t. Substituting k = c/t into the Arrhenius equation and rearranging gives:

ln t = Ea/(RT) + constant

So a graph of ln t against 1/T is a straight line with gradient +Ea/R.

Apparatus

  • 0.01 mol dm−3 aqueous phenol solution
  • Bromide/bromate(V) solution (0.1 mol dm−3 potassium bromide and 0.02 mol dm−3 potassium bromate(V) in the same solution)
  • 0.5 mol dm−3 sulfuric acid, methyl red indicator
  • Two boiling tubes, 10 cm3 pipette, 500 cm3 beaker as a water bath, kettle, ice, thermometer (0–110 degrees C), stop clock

Method

  1. Pipette 10 cm3 of phenol solution and 10 cm3 of bromide/bromate(V) solution into one boiling tube, and add four drops of methyl red.
  2. Pipette 5 cm3 of sulfuric acid into a second boiling tube.
  3. Use kettle water in the beaker to make a water bath at 75 degrees C (±1 degrees C) and stand both tubes in it.
  4. When both tubes have reached the bath temperature, mix them by pouring one into the other and back again, and start the stop clock at the same time.
  5. Keep the reaction tube in the bath and stop the clock when the red colour of the methyl red disappears.
  6. Repeat the whole experiment at 65, 55, 45, 35, 25 and 15 degrees C, using ice for the lowest temperature.

Analysis

  • Record temperature and time, then add columns for T in kelvin (degrees C + 273), 1/T in K−1, and ln t.
  • Plot ln t (y-axis) against 1/T (x-axis) and draw a line of best fit.
  • Gradient = Ea/R, so Ea = gradient x 8.31 J mol−1. Divide by 1000 for kJ mol−1.
  • If you plot ln(1/t) (which is proportional to ln k) instead, the gradient is −Ea/R and Ea = −gradient x R. Both give the same value.

Safety

  • Wear a lab coat, gloves and eye protection.
  • Phenol is toxic and corrosive; avoid skin contact.
  • Potassium bromate(V) is oxidising; sulfuric acid is an irritant.
  • Take care with hot water from the kettle and the 75 degrees C bath.

Exam Tip: Know the role of each reagent: phenol removes bromine so that nothing is seen until a fixed amount of reaction has happened, and methyl red shows when that point is reached by being bleached. Watch the sign of the gradient: ln t against 1/T has a positive gradient (Ea/R), while ln k or ln(1/t) against 1/T has a negative gradient (−Ea/R). Ea is always positive. With R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1, Ea comes out in J mol−1. The same analysis can be applied to other clock reactions, such as sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid.

CP16: The Preparation of Aspirin

Background Theory

Organic solids (here aspirin, an ester of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid) are prepared by organic synthesis reactions, then purified by recrystallisation and their purity assessed by melting point determination. A pure substance has a sharp melting point (melts over a narrow range, typically <2 degrees C). An impure substance melts over a wider range and at a lower temperature than expected (melting point depression).

Method: Preparing Aspirin

2-hydroxybenzoic acid + ethanoic anhydride → 2-ethanoyloxybenzoic acid (aspirin) + ethanoic acid
  1. Weigh about 2 g of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid into a pear-shaped flask. In a fume cupboard, add about 4 cm3 of ethanoic anhydride and a few drops of concentrated phosphoric(V) acid (catalyst).
  2. Warm the mixture in a hot water bath (about 85 degrees C) for about 5–10 minutes, swirling, to complete the reaction.
  3. Carefully add a little cold water to destroy the excess ethanoic anhydride, then cool the flask in iced water until crystals form.
  4. Collect the crude aspirin by vacuum filtration and wash with a little ice-cold water.
  5. Recrystallise from the minimum volume of hot solvent (a mixture of ethanol and water works well), then dry and weigh.
  6. Check purity by melting temperature: pure aspirin melts at about 136 degrees C.

Recrystallisation Method

  1. Dissolve the crude product in the minimum volume of hot solvent (just enough to dissolve it at the boiling point). Common solvents: water, ethanol, ethyl ethanoate. The choice of solvent is critical: the product must be soluble in the hot solvent but insoluble in the cold solvent.
  2. Hot filter (if necessary): filter the hot solution through a pre-warmed funnel with fluted filter paper. This removes insoluble impurities. Work quickly to prevent premature crystallisation.
  3. Cool slowly: Allow the filtrate to cool slowly to room temperature, then place in an ice bath. Slow cooling produces larger, purer crystals (rapid cooling gives small, impure crystals because impurities get trapped).
  4. Filter under reduced pressure (vacuum filtration using a Buchner funnel and flask). This is faster than gravity filtration and removes more solvent. The crystals are collected on the filter paper; the impurities remain dissolved in the cold filtrate (mother liquor).
  5. Wash the crystals on the filter with a small volume of ice-cold solvent (to remove residual impurities from the crystal surfaces without re-dissolving the product).
  6. Dry the crystals: press between sheets of filter paper, then leave in a desiccator (containing a drying agent like silica gel) or a warm oven (below the melting point).

Melting Point Determination

  1. Pack a small amount of dry crystals into a melting point capillary tube (sealed at one end). The column of solid should be ~5 mm high.
  2. Place the tube in an electrically heated melting point apparatus or attach it to a thermometer in a heated oil bath.
  3. Heat slowly (~1–2 degrees C per minute near the expected melting point). Record the temperature at which the solid first starts to melt (meniscus appears) and the temperature at which it is completely liquid.
  4. A pure substance melts over a narrow range (≤2 degrees C) and matches the literature value. An impure substance melts at a lower temperature and over a wider range.

Safety

  • Hot solvents — risk of burns. Use a water bath to heat flammable solvents (never a naked flame).
  • Organic solvents (ethanol, ethyl ethanoate) are flammable and produce vapours — good ventilation.
  • Some organic products are irritants — wear gloves and goggles.
  • Ethanoic anhydride and concentrated phosphoric(V) acid are corrosive; ethanoic anhydride vapour is irritating — use a fume cupboard.

Calculating Percentage Yield and Atom Economy

Percentage yield = (actual moles of product / theoretical moles of product) x 100
Reasons yield < 100%: incomplete reaction, side reactions, loss during transfer/filtration/washing, product left in mother liquor.

Atom economy = (Mr of desired product / sum of Mr of all products) x 100
A measure of how efficiently atoms are used. Higher atom economy = less waste.

Impurities not fully removedSystematic (melting point too low and broad)Repeat the recrystallisation; use a better solvent choice

Supporting Content: Molar Mass of a Volatile Liquid Using pV = nRT

Background Theory

The molar mass (Mr) of a volatile liquid can be determined experimentally using the ideal gas equation: pV = nRT. A known mass of the volatile liquid is vaporised at a known temperature and pressure, and the volume of the resulting gas is measured. Since n = mass/Mr, the equation can be rearranged to Mr = mRT/(pV). Two methods are commonly used: the gas syringe method (inject liquid into a heated gas syringe) and the Dumas method (heat liquid in a flask with a pinhole until it all vaporises, then weigh the condensed vapour).

pV = nRT   →   n = pV/RT   →   Mr = mRT/(pV)

Where: p = pressure (Pa), V = volume (m3), n = moles, R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1, T = temperature (K), m = mass (g)

Method (Gas Syringe)

  1. Place a gas syringe (with a self-sealing rubber septum at one end) inside an oven set to a known temperature well above the boiling point of the liquid. Allow to reach thermal equilibrium. Record the temperature (T) in Kelvin and atmospheric pressure (p) in Pa.
  2. Weigh the hypodermic syringe containing a small volume (~0.2 cm3) of the volatile liquid. Record mass to ±0.001 g.
  3. Inject the liquid through the septum into the hot gas syringe. The liquid vaporises immediately. Re-weigh the hypodermic syringe.
  4. Mass of liquid injected = initial mass − final mass.
  5. Once the gas syringe has reached thermal equilibrium, record the volume of gas (V) in cm3. Convert to m3 (1 cm3 = 1 x 10−6 m3).
  6. Calculate: n = pV/(RT). Then Mr = mass/n.

Method (Dumas)

  1. Place a few drops of the volatile liquid in a round-bottomed flask. Cover the opening with aluminium foil pierced with a single pin-hole. The pinhole allows vapour to escape but prevents air from entering rapidly.
  2. Place the flask in a boiling water bath (100 degrees C / 373 K). As the liquid vaporises, excess vapour escapes through the pinhole until only vapour at atmospheric pressure remains in the flask.
  3. When no more vapour is seen escaping, remove the flask and allow it to cool. The vapour condenses back to liquid.
  4. Weigh the flask + condensed liquid + foil. Then weigh the empty, dry, clean flask + foil. Mass of vapour = difference.
  5. Measure the volume of the flask by filling with water and measuring the volume of water. This gives V.
  6. T = temperature of the water bath (in K); p = atmospheric pressure (in Pa).
  7. Calculate Mr = mRT/(pV).

Safety

  • Volatile liquids are highly flammable — no naked flames; use electric oven/water bath.
  • Handle hypodermic syringes with care (sharp needle — risk of needlestick injury).
  • Oven is hot — use heat-resistant gloves.
  • Work in a well-ventilated area (volatile vapours can be irritating).

Supporting Content: Enthalpy of Neutralisation (Calorimetry)

Background Theory

A known volume of acid is mixed with a known volume of alkali in an insulated polystyrene cup, and the temperature change is used to calculate the enthalpy change per mole of water formed. The same calorimetry skills are used in CP8. Key points:

  • The standard enthalpy of neutralisation for strong acid + strong base (e.g. HCl + NaOH) is approximately −57.1 kJ mol−1 because the net ionic equation is the same: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)
  • For reactions involving weak acids or bases, the measured ΔH is less exothermic because some energy is used to fully dissociate the weak acid/base (bond breaking is endothermic)
  • Always use the graphical extrapolation method to find the corrected ΔT: plot temperature vs time, extrapolate the pre-mixing and post-mixing lines back to the time of mixing
q = mcΔT     ΔH = −q/n     (kJ mol−1)
Exam Tip: Experimental values are always less exothermic than literature values due to heat loss. The two key assumptions: c = 4.18 J g−1 K−1 and density = 1.0 g cm−3. For neutralisation of a weak acid, ΔH is less than 57.1 kJ mol−1 because energy is needed to fully ionise the weak acid first.

Supporting Content: Enthalpy of Combustion of an Alcohol

Background Theory

The enthalpy of combustion (ΔcH) is the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen under standard conditions. This practical uses a simple spirit burner calorimeter: the alcohol burns and heats a known mass of water above it. By measuring the temperature rise of the water and the mass of alcohol burned, ΔcH can be calculated. Experimental values are always significantly less exothermic than data book values, primarily due to substantial heat loss to the surroundings. Across a homologous series of alcohols, ΔcH increases approximately linearly with each additional CH2 group (~650 kJ mol−1 per CH2), because each additional CH2 provides additional C–H and C–C bonds to be broken and additional C=O and O–H bonds to be formed.

Apparatus

  • Spirit burner containing the alcohol to be tested (e.g. methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol)
  • Copper calorimeter (or metal can — copper has high thermal conductivity, transferring heat efficiently to water)
  • Thermometer (±0.5 degrees C) or temperature probe
  • Measuring cylinder (100 cm3), balance (±0.01 g)
  • Draught shield (to reduce heat loss from wind), clamp stand, stirring rod

Method

  1. Measure 100 cm3 of cold water into the copper calorimeter. Record the initial temperature (T1).
  2. Weigh the spirit burner (with cap on) and record the mass to ±0.01 g (m1).
  3. Position the calorimeter above the spirit burner. Surround with the draught shield.
  4. Light the spirit burner. Stir the water gently with the stirring rod throughout.
  5. When the temperature has risen by approximately 20 degrees C, extinguish the flame by replacing the cap. Record the maximum temperature (T2).
  6. Immediately re-weigh the spirit burner (m2).
  7. Calculate:
    • ΔT = T2 − T1
    • Mass of fuel burned = m1 − m2
    • q = mcΔT (m = 100 g water, c = 4.18 J g−1 K−1)
    • Moles of fuel = (m1 − m2) / Mr
    • ΔcH = −q / n (kJ mol−1)
  8. Repeat for different alcohols in the same homologous series.

Safety

  • Alcohols are highly flammable — keep containers away from the flame; cap the burner promptly.
  • Spirit burner flames can be almost invisible (especially methanol) — handle carefully; assume the burner is lit until confirmed otherwise.
  • Hot apparatus — use tongs and a heatproof mat.

Analysis

  • Plot ΔcH (y-axis) against number of carbon atoms (x-axis). Expect a linear trend: each additional CH2 group adds ~650 kJ mol−1 to the combustion enthalpy.
  • Compare experimental values with data book values. Experimental values will be much less exothermic (typically 50–70% of literature values) due to the dominant source of error: heat loss.

Exam Tip: Experimental ΔcH values are always less exothermic (smaller magnitude) than data book values. The main reason is heat loss to the surroundings. Incomplete combustion also contributes (producing CO and C instead of only CO2, releasing less energy). The linear trend across the homologous series shows that each additional CH2 group contributes approximately the same amount of energy. Use bond enthalpy calculations to explain the theoretical trend: breaking C–H and C–C bonds (endothermic) and forming C=O and O–H bonds (exothermic).

Supporting Content: Rate by Continuous Monitoring (Gas Collection)

The volume of gas produced (collected in a gas syringe) or the mass lost from an open flask on a balance is recorded at regular intervals. Key points: the initial rate = gradient of the tangent at t = 0. Compare curves for different concentrations, surface areas, or temperatures. The curve levels off when one reactant is consumed.

Exam Tip: On a volume-time graph: steeper initial gradient = faster rate. If the same moles of limiting reagent are used, all curves reach the same final volume. Increasing concentration makes the reaction faster (steeper curve) but does not change the total amount of product if the limiting reagent amount is unchanged.

Supporting Content: pH Curves for Acid-Base Titrations

A pH curve is obtained by adding a titrant from a burette in small portions and recording the pH with a calibrated pH meter after each addition. The weak acid–strong base curve is used in CP9 to find Ka. Key points:

Four Titration Curve Types

Key Concepts

  • Buffer region: The flat section before equivalence in a weak acid/strong base titration. The mixture of HA and A− resists pH changes.
  • Half-equivalence point: [HA] = [A−], so pH = pKa. Read directly from the graph.
  • Equivalence point: All the acid has reacted. For weak acid/strong base, pH > 7 (conjugate base hydrolysis). For strong acid/weak base, pH < 7.
  • Indicator choice: The indicator's colour-change range must fall within the steep vertical section of the curve. If it does not, the end-point will not coincide with the equivalence point.
Exam Tip: You must be able to sketch all four titration curves and label key features (initial pH, buffer region, half-equivalence, equivalence point, final pH). At the half-equivalence point for a weak acid: pH = pKa. This is the easiest experimental method to determine pKa. Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]).
